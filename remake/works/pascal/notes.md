# Pascal, *Pensées*: the poet's notes

Piece `pascal`. Text: `pascal` (W. F. Trotter's translation, prose). Three plates, eleven lenses.
Words of the text are pointed at by part, block and word number, as `words.mjs` prints them.
Nothing of Auden's is quoted here beyond a few words used as place marks.

Checker, last run: `node build/tools/check-reading.mjs pascal --print` ends
"no problems / no warnings / the printed notes are fit to print".

## 1. The point of view

Auden's fullest statement on Pascal is in *The Prolific and the Devourer* (written in the
summer of 1939): Pascal is the noblest of the dualists; he prized the heart, which he
lacked, above the reason, which he had; he was a divided man, and what saved him was his
reason. That is the `view`, and the title follows it: "The heart and the reason".
The three passages were chosen because each sets the two faculties against each other in
a way that can be drawn:

1. the heart given its reasons, and faith defined against reason (fragments 277, 278);
2. the reason given its dignity (fragments 347, 348, the thinking reed);
3. the reason put to work on belief (fragment 233, the wager), the argument Auden
   objected to twice (in the book he wrote in 1939, and in his essay on Kierkegaard,
   which `C_auden_on_works.md` dates 1952) and doubted once more in *The Dyer's Hand*.

## 2. Why these passages

**Plate `heart`: `pascal/s04`, rows 90.0 and 92.0 (67 and 24 words).** The most quoted
sentence of the book turns on one word in two senses, which is a thing a reader can see
when the two words are joined. The next fragment is a double contrast in 24 words. Both
were written on a page of the wager, which gives a fourth lens something true to show
about a book of fragments (the lead's third item). Auden's verdict uses the same two
nouns, heart and reason, and reverses them.

**Plate `reed`: `pascal/s06`, rows 17.0, 18.0[0-6], 20.0[29-46] (77, 7 and 18 words).**
The lead names the thinking reed. Fragment 347 is shown whole; of its second paragraph
only the sentence that draws the conclusion (seven words); of fragment 348 only its last
sentence, where the image is turned into a crossing. The last sentence of 348 in French
hangs on a single verb in two senses, the same device as plate one, so the two plates
answer each other.

**Plate `wager`: `pascal/s03`, row 144.0[0-125] (126 words).** The lead names the wager.
This is the part of the dialogue where the bet is forced and the two cases are weighed.
By the electronic edition's account of the manuscript, the first state of the argument
ended with the sentence that follows it. It holds the voice (statements, questions, "let us"),
the three matched clauses, the balanced sentence and the gaming words.
*Length.* 126 words is over POET.md's 110 and under the checker's 130 (no warning). The
passage cannot stop sooner without losing the balanced sentence, and it cannot run one
sentence further, to the order to wager, because in the hosted text word 132 joins the
last word of that sentence to the first word of the next speaker, and a row range takes
whole word numbers only.

### Plate `wager`

**Game (word).** Marks: w11, w46, w103-104, w113.

- w46: French *engager*. Littré, *engager* 1 (to put in pawn) and 3 (to pledge one's

- w103-104: French *en prenant croix*. Littré, *croix* 11 (the side of a coin once

- w113: French *cas*. Littré, *cas* 1.

- Auden on the value of the wager: *Forewords and Afterwords*, p. 178.

## 6. Changed after reading the sources again

- The manuscript page. A first draft said fragment 277 stands "at the head of the
  page". It is written upside down at the top, and by the editors' account last, after
  278 had gone into the margin. The Page lens was rebuilt on that.
- The pairs. Three headings, four pairs; the additions are the first pair and the last,
  not the first and the third.
- The balanced sentence. Its second half was in the first writing; what was added is
  the weighing, the two cases and the opening of the first half.
- "Him". The fifth "him" of fragment 347 (w70) follows "over", not a verb of force. The
  span was removed and the caption now says four.
- A `why` said the fragment "defines faith twice". It defines faith once and makes its
  contrast twice. Mended.
- "Under the first" in the Reasons caption could be read as Littré's first sense. It
  now says "under motive".
- Montaigne. The caption no longer says what "Montaigne says" of man in general; it
  says what the editor quotes.
- Captions were cut to 110 words or fewer by the checker's count (two had drawn
  warnings at 123 and 121). Three `why` fields were cut to 40 or fewer. Every `stands`
  now has at most four sentences. `about` has four sentences.
- Caveat 5 said the paired fragments "were not written in the order printed". For 347
  and 348 nothing read shows the order of writing; they are on manuscript pages 63 and
  165. It now says only that.
- Scales caption and `why`: "is to suppose two Gods" became "is in effect to suppose

  the chain of causes, and that it is as if there were two Gods; the caption now claims
  no more than that.
- Pages in *Forewords and Afterwords* were checked once more against the layout of the

  - Against caption: the verdict kept six words of Auden's in a row. Scales caption: the

  - `auden` list: the entries from *The Prolific and the Devourer* (two Gods), "Hic et
