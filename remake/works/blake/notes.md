## The piece in one paragraph

Three plates from the stretch of the book that runs from the heading The
Voice of the Devil to the Proverbs of Hell: the three errors with their
three contraries (blocks 11 to 18), the sentence the Devil writes (block
32), and six proverbs in a row (rows 34.20 to 34.25). Ten lenses:
compare, sentence, word; beat, sentence, word; figure (parallelism),
sentence (the yoked verb), figure (chiasmus), word.

## Auden's view, and how it was settled

Auden wrote about the Marriage itself in one sentence that the research
notes hold: in his review of C. S. Lewis's The Great Divorce (1946) he
ranks it among the great polemics of the reaction that liberal theology
made, rightly as he thought, against a dualism of two Gods. The notes
(C_auden_on_works.md, section 13 (b) and (c), appendix H17i) have the
sentence from snippets, and say that the first half was seen only in
part. I did not see the review.

The same argument is made at length, about Blake, in The Enchafèd Flood,
which is on this machine as a scan (`research/auden/ef.txt`). I read
lines 1868 to 1940 and 2060 to 2165 again on 2026-10-03:

- Blake brought in as a strongly polemical writer, and the statements of
  his that follow, one of which names Bacon, Newton and Locke as
  unbelief: p. 44.
- the Enemy, for Blake, is the picture of the universe he links with
  Newton: p. 45.
- the Judge who makes the moral law and punishes, whom Blake detested:
  p. 49.
- Blake saw that the Incarnation and the Forgiveness of Sins were the
  points at issue; the romantics turned the dualism upside down (the
  words quoted in the view); to Blake God acts and is only in existing
  beings and men; the footnote that calls deist and romantic theology
  both heresies: p. 50.

**The page numbers of this scan.** EXAMINER.md says the page number is in
the running head above the passage. In `ef.txt` it is not. The number
stands alone at the foot of each page, and the running head of the next
page follows it. Test: the contents page puts chapter Two at page 40;
the chapter heading is at line 1696 of the file and the first number
after it is 40 (line 1749), followed by the running head of the next
page (line 1752). So the number under a passage is its page. An examiner
who takes the number above a passage will be one page low. The pages are
those of the Vintage edition of 1967, which is the scan; I did not see
the first edition of 1950.

The `view` joins the two texts. The sentence of 1946 is about the
Marriage; the pages of 1950 are about Blake and do not name the book,
but the sentence Auden gives there as Blake's position is the speaker's
own answer in block 63 of the Marriage. This is said in `open`.

**Quotations of Auden.** The piece has two, the most the rules allow: 12

## Plate 1, "creed": the errors and their contraries

**Rows.** Text 11.0 to 14.0 (59 words), pair 15.0 to 18.0 (65 words).

**Why this passage.** It is the book's own method in small. Block 7 says
that without contraries there is no progression, and here Blake numbers
three errors and sets a contrary against each. It is also where Auden's
view can be seen on the page. He reads the book as a polemic against a
dualism with a God who is a Judge. The third error has a God who will
torment man. The contraries take up the nouns of the errors and never
name God. Three things can be shown on the words: which words answer
which, the grammar (clauses that report against sentences that state),
and the words Blake chose for the senses and for Reason.

**Why a pair.** The pair is normally a translation. Here it is Blake's
second list. The two lists have four rows each, so the kit sets each
contrary under its error.

**Lenses.**

- compare, "Answer". Six ties. What comes back and what does not was
  checked word by word in the proof: Body, Soul, Energy and Reason
  return (so does man); Evil, Good and God do not; Eternity returns as
  the adjective.
- sentence, "Grammar". Each error begins with That after its number
  (12.0 w1, 13.0 w1 and w11, 14.0 w1) and hangs on the last word of row
  11.0. The finite verbs of the three contraries are has (once) and is
  (five times); the two participles in row 16.0 are not counted, and
  the verb of row 15.0 belongs to the line that introduces the
  contraries. The one future on the plate is 14.0 w3-4. Row 18.0 has
  four words after its number and is the shortest sentence on the plate.
  In the hosted text the first contrary is two sentences.
- word, "Words". Eight notes.

**Facts, and where each was read.**

- Hosted text, blocks 7, 8, 10 to 18, 21 and 30 (`show.mjs`).
- Johnson's Dictionary of 1755, read at johnsonsdictionaryonline.com:
  Code (a book; a book of the civil law); Viz and Videlicet (the
  definition used as the gloss); Energy (four senses; the example on
  God's eternal energy is under the second); Contrary, the noun (senses
  1 and 2); Inlet (one sense; examples from Wotton, Milton and Locke);
  Bound, the noun (sense 1); Circumference (sense 1, whose first example
  is the two lines of Paradise Lost in which the Son says bounds and
  circumference).
- Oxford English Dictionary, energy, n., sense 6.a.i, dated 1802 onward,
  first example from Thomas Young. The full entry opened.
- Locke, Essay, book 2, chapter 9, sections 14 and 15, and chapter 7,
  section 10 (Project Gutenberg no. 10615, lines 4794 to 4812 and 4110
  to 4112). Section 14 speaks of the senses as passages for ideas and
  as inlets; section 15 calls perception the inlet of the materials of
  knowledge; the sentence Johnson quotes is in chapter 7.
- Milton, Paradise Lost, book 7, lines 216 to 231 (Project Gutenberg no.
  26, lines 5796 to 5811). The one who takes the compasses is the Son,
  called there the Word. The name Messiah is used twelve times in the
  poem: four times in book 5, six in book 6, twice in book 12, and not
  in book 7. So the caption says "Milton's Son of God, the Messiah" and
  the note says that Milton also names him Messiah.
- Milton has bounds, in the plural; Blake has the singular. The caption
  says so.
- Wikipedia, "Content clause", for the grammar of a clause with that.
- Online Etymology Dictionary, polemic.

**Cautions.** The caption does not say that Blake took bound and
circumference from Milton. It says whose words they are in Milton and
that block 21 names Milton's Messiah as Reason. The gloss under torment
first read as an interpretation and was changed to a fact of the plate.
The errors are called "errors laid to the sacred codes", which is what
row 11.0 says; an earlier draft called them a creed and used the same
word for the contraries.

## Plate 2, "sentence": the Devil's sentence

**Rows.** 32.0 and 32.1: two lines, 23 words.

**Why this passage.** It is the hinge between the contraries and the
proverbs, and the one place in this stretch where a rhythm can be
drawn. Its two lines are fourteen syllables each. The first is close to
the opening of a stanza in a ballad by Chatterton, and the second
departs from the measure at two places that can be seen. The claim it
makes about every bird is put inside a question about what the reader
knows, and it ends on the five senses that the first contrary called
inlets.

**The scansion, and the other ways of hearing it.**

- `words.mjs` counts 14 syllables in each row. Row 32.0 w5 is printed
  in full in the hosted text and counts as two syllables, which is how
  Blake spelled it (by the Wikisource transcription of his plate). Said
  with three, the line has fifteen. Row 32.1 w6 is one syllable.
- Row 32.0, syllables 1 and 2. Marked with the stress on the first word
  and none on the second. It can also be said with the stress on the
  second, which is the measure itself. The caption does not lean on
  these syllables: it says that voice and measure agree after them, and
  a point says that there are two hearings.
- Row 32.1, first word. Marked unstressed. It can take a light stress,
  which gives the line seven stresses and not six. Nothing in the
  caption depends on it.
- Row 32.1, w2 and w3. Stress on the second syllable of w2 and on w3.
  The only other reading is to chant the measure, which puts a beat on
  w4 and leaves w3 light. No reader says it so, and I rejected it.
- Row 32.1, w6. Stressed straight after the comma, with w7 light: what
  handbooks call an inversion. I hear no other way.
- The measure is given as seven beats, rising. The pause mark in the
  second row stands at the comma, after the eighth syllable.

**Facts, and where each was read.**

- Baum, The Principles of English Versification, in
  `.cache/handbooks/baum-versification-pg21342.txt`. Chapter 4 (lines
  3038 to 3046): the line of seven stresses or fourteen syllables, its
  pause after the fourth foot, and its breaking into the ballad stanza.
  Chapter 4 (lines 3212 to 3213): the sense run on from one line into
  the next. Chapter 5 (lines 6606 to 6621): the inverted foot stands
  nearly always next to a pause. Chapter 5 (lines 6972 to 6984): lines
  overweighted with stress are slowed. Baum reports "inversion" as the
  common name and prefers another analysis, so the point says "commonly
  called".
- Johnson, Life of Dryden (Project Gutenberg no. 9823, lines 12975 to
  12978): the break always at the eighth syllable; quatrains of eight
  and six.
- Chatterton, Bristowe Tragedie, lines 133 to 136, read in the Rowley
  Poems (Project Gutenberg no. 13037, lines 3590 to 3593), with lines
  105 to 140 for the context: the speaker is the condemned knight, who
  says at line 119 that he despises death. The editor's note (line 1644)
  says that the text follows a London printing of 1772.
  The verb of line 136 is close, in the infinitive; the caption gives
  it so.
- Jonathan Lamb, "Bard of Tropes", London Review of Books, 20 September

  the Marriage. This and the wording are the whole case, and `open`

- Johnson, But, sense 7, with examples from Hooker and Shakespeare;

- Online Etymology Dictionary, immense; Johnson, Immense.
- Hosted text: the five senses three times in the prose (blocks 16, 27

- Blake's own spelling and punctuation: the Wikisource transcription of

**Cautions.** The caption says that the sense is held open across the

bird "is" a world in the line, and the caption keeps that verb.

## Plate 3, "proverbs": six Proverbs of Hell

**Rows.** 34.20 to 34.25: six lines, 59 words.

**Why this passage.** Six proverbs that stand together in the book and
show three devices in six lines: one verb yoking two clauses (34.20),
one frame kept four times (34.21 to 34.24), and a crossing (34.25).
They bear on Auden's view twice. The frame makes what is God's a
creature's, and Auden gives as Blake's position that God acts and is
only in existing beings. The yoked line makes law the stone of prisons,
and Auden says Blake detested the God who makes the moral law. The
lead in pieces.json says each proverb is a sentence in two halves set
against each other. That is true of 34.20 and 34.25. The four between
are equations with is.

**The terms.**

- 34.21 to 34.24, parallelism: the same build of clause repeated
  (Silva Rhetoricae, parallelism).
- 34.20, zeugma. Strictly it is the kind called prozeugma, where the
  verb of the first clause is left out of the next (Silva Rhetoricae,
  zeugma and prozeugma). The caption uses the general term and its
  English, a yoking. It is shown with a sentence lens because a plate
  may have only two figure lenses.
- 34.25, chiasmus: ideas returning in the opposite order (Silva
  Rhetoricae, chiasmus). It is not the kind in which the same words
  come back reversed. The grammar of the two halves is parallel and the
  ideas cross, which the caption says.

**Facts, and where each was read.**

- The King James Bible (Project Gutenberg no. 10). Verses of Proverbs
  that begin with the frame: 33 of 915, counted with the pattern
  `^The \w+( \w+)? of (the |a |his |her |their |thy |an )?[\w']+( \w+)? is `;
  five are named in the grounds (1:7, 9:10, 15:33, 20:27, 20:29).
  Phrases counted through the whole Bible: the glory of God 16 times,
  the wisdom of God 5, the work of God 6, the bounty of God never (the
  word bounty is never followed by of), the wrath of God 10. Proverbs
  19:12; Genesis 2:25 and 3:7-10; Exodus 24:12 and 31:18; Leviticus 18,
  where the word of row 34.24 w1 comes 24 times, in verses 6 to 19.
- Oxford English Dictionary, pride, n.1: sense I.1.c (the first of the
  seven deadly sins) and phrase P.2 (heraldry, of a peacock with the
  tail spread; examples of 1530, 1721, 1766 and 1790). The full entry
  opened.
- Johnson: Pride (senses 6 and 7), Lust (three senses, the third marked
  as not used), Goatish, Bounty, Wrath, Excess (five senses).
- Online Etymology Dictionary: lust, wrath, bounty, excess, peacock.
- Catholic Encyclopedia (1912), "Sin", at newadvent.org: the seven
  capital sins as Aquinas lists them. Wikipedia, "Seven deadly sins":
  the standard list, which has wrath.
- Hosted text: block 63; block 117; rows 34.2 and 34.58.
- Auden: The Enchafèd Flood, pp. 49 and 50; the foreword to the book of
  aphorisms.

**Cautions.** Three of the four first nouns name deadly sins; the
fourth does not, and its label is "sin or shame". Three of the four
endings are phrases of the English Bible; the second is not. The
captions say three, not all. The sentence about a peacock's display, a
goat's desire and a lion's anger is conditional ("read in that use")
and stands on the dictionary senses.

## For the artist

- Plate 1 uses `pair` for Blake's own second list. The contraries are
  his text, equal in weight to the errors. The kit sets a pair of the
  same number of rows under the text rows and prints it smaller, in
  italic and in a quiet color, which makes it look like a gloss. It
  should not.
- The rows of plate 1 are prose of unequal length (row 16.0 has 31
  words), so they will wrap.
- The "Yoke" lens on plate 3 has six tags, which is all the palette of
  the sentence lens holds.
- In the "Cross" lens the order of `parts` matters: the kit crosses the
  first two tags that have two parts each, and the shared noun comes
  after them.
- In the "Frame" lens every part carries the label of its tag, so that
  the key lights the same place in all four lines.
- The words in bold in the captions are spelled as the hosted text
  spells them, so that they light on the plate.
