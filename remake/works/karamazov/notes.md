## Why these three passages

All three are from Part II, Book V, chapter 5. The leads asked for the
speaker inside a speaker and the listener who never answers, the three
temptations as the plan of the speech, and the kiss. The text bears all
three out, and Auden's two sentences are about the first and the cry.

**Plate 1, "first" (block 8, words 0 to 108).** The old man's first
words in the prison, up to the threat of burning. Chosen because the
terms of the whole speech are set here and can be seen: four questions
(the first asked twice), an answer forbidden, and after each later
question a statement of his own. Ivan's aside inside the old man's
speech (words 4 to 11) shows the speaker inside a speaker in the
passage itself. Auden's sentence about Jesus not answering is about
exactly this. 109 words.

**Plate 2, "cry" (block 20, words 1047 to 1146).** A hundred words, two
thirds of the way through the speech (words 3,282 to 3,381 of 4,977).
Chosen because Auden quotes the opening of these lines as the cry of
such men, and because the change can be seen and counted: eleven
sentences where the argument around them averages 23 words a sentence,
five questions, two commands, and then the confession, which no one has
asked for. The lines also take up the opening terms again the other way
round, which is why lines from the opening are set beside them (block 8,
words 12 to 62, and block 12, words 290 to 303).

**Plate 3, "kiss" (block 31, words 50 to 130, and blocks 32, 33, 34).**
The end of the poem and the brothers' first three speeches after it. The
kiss is told in short sentences with verbs of action, the story itself
names it "all His answer", and Ivan's answer to Alyosha about the old
man (block 33) belongs with it. Beside it stand five short cuts from
later in the same talk (blocks 48, 49, 50, 51, 53) where Alyosha repeats
the kiss and Ivan names the source. 108 words on the plate, 57 beside.

What I left out and why: the three temptations are the plan of the
speech and are shown as the Plan diagram on plate 2, not as a passage,
because no hundred words hold all three. The legend of the Virgin's
descent into hell and the verse quotations at the head of the chapter
are Ivan's preface and are only counted in the Voices diagram.

## Whose craft

The English is Constance Garnett's (1912; Project Gutenberg no. 28054;
research notes D, section 18: the Modern Library edition on Auden's
sheet is the same translation). No Russian text is hosted and
`node build/tools/original.mjs list` offers none, so no Russian is shown
and every Russian fact is a gloss or a note. I read the Russian to know
what is Dostoevsky's and what is Garnett's:

- Source: F. M. Dostoevsky, Sobranie sochinenii v 15 tomakh, vols 9-10
  (Leningrad: Nauka, 1991), as given at ilibrary.ru (text 1199, page 37,
  which holds the whole chapter; the page is in the cp1251 encoding).
  Russian Wikisource was read beside it. Compared in runs of eight
  words, the two copies of the chapter are the same from Ivan's preface
  to the last paragraph, but for a footnote mark and one mistyped letter
  in the preface; every Russian phrase the piece leans on stands alike
  in both.
- Dostoevsky's: the four questions and their order in block 8, the ban
  on answering, the statements that follow; the five questions of the
  cry as five sentences; the repeated verb for hindering (the same verb
  in all five places); the repeated word for mystery; the kiss, its
  adverb and verb, repeated for Alyosha; the word for go in both
  dismissals; ninety-year-old lips; the verb burns with heart twice.
- Garnett's: the pronoun thou for Christ and God and you for everyone
  else (the Russian has one familiar pronoun); some sentence divisions
  (she ends a sentence after the threat in block 8 where the Russian runs
  on with a comma; she makes the two commands of the cry sentences of
  their own, and the moving lips after the kiss a sentence of its own);
  the past tense of the kiss (the Russian tells it in the present, all
  but one verb); "aged" for ninety-year-old; "glows" for burns; "softly"
  for an adverb she elsewhere renders slowly, peacefully and gently; she
  leaves out a clause of the cry (that he reads it in the Prisoner's
  eyes) and the second "silently" at Alyosha's kiss; she writes the
  remark about the meek look (block 12) as a statement where the Russian
  has a question.

Each caption that leans on one of these says whose it is.

## The lenses, and where each fact was read

### Plate 1

**Questions (figure, hypophora).** Marks: the four questions in one
color, the three statements that follow in a second, Ivan's three words
for the missing answer in a third, the ban in a fourth. `draw` is
"other": the parts run over several lines of prose, and the kit's "pair"
join is made for parts that sit on one line, so I ask for the colored
rules only. (While I worked, the figure lens gained a `join` field, and
POET.md now gives it. It could tie each later question to its statement
by place in the list, [[1, 4], [2, 5], [3, 6]]. I leave it unset: each
of those questions is followed at once by its statement, so the two
colors already show which follows which, and a stroke between two
neighbors would add nothing. The artist or the examiner may set it if
the page shows otherwise.)

- The figure: Silva Rhetoricae, entry anthypophora, read twice this
  session. It defines the figure as asking and at once answering one's
  own questions, says it sometimes takes the form of asking an adversary
  what can be said, lists hypophora among its other names, and cites
  Peacham 1577 for that name. The entry hypophora only points to it. The
  last caveat tells the reader the figure has several names.
- Another way to take it: the third question (about hindering) is
  followed by a statement that repeats the charge and does not answer
  the "why". So the caption says "a statement of his own" for all three
  and uses "answer" only in the general definition and in Ivan's own
  words. If an examiner holds that the name needs a true answer each
  time, the marks and the caption still stand; only the tag "replies"
  would want another name.
- Ivan's remark that the old man asks and answers for him: block 12,
  words 128 to 138, said of the question at words 108 to 127 (a later
  question, and the caption says "a later question").
- The old man's reason: words 29 to 43 of the plate; Ivan repeats it to
  Alyosha at block 12, words 8 to 27.
- Auden: p. 51 (C, E).

- Method (also under `facts.counted`): each word of the chapter, as the

  marks around it. Outside all marks is the narrator; inside double
  marks is Ivan or Alyosha, by paragraph; inside single marks in Ivan's
  paragraphs is the Grand Inquisitor in blocks 8, 12, 14, 17 to 21 and

  Ivan quotes" (among them the Jesuits' 39 words in block 12 and the two

  counted as his, since he says it.
- Results: 9,244 words; narrator 394, Alyosha 507, Ivan's own voice

  Prisoner 2; Ivan with all he quotes 8,343. Alyosha breaks in at blocks

**Words (word).** Four words of the passage.

- Thou: the Russian has the familiar pronoun (Wiktionary, Russian entry
  for it: familiar second person singular, you, thou), to the Prisoner
  and between the brothers. In Garnett there are 220 thou-forms in the
  chapter, counted on the raw text: Thou 121, Thee 52 (one of them
  lower-case), Thy 37, Thyself 8, Thine 2. I read every one in its
  place: all are said to Christ or to God. Four stand outside the old
  man's speech (block 0, word 443; block 2, word 162; block 6, words 128
  and 267) and are a hymn, a prayer and two pleas, which is the ground
  for "the pronoun of the chapter's prayers". A count by whole words
  gives 217, because three forms stand inside words joined by a dash; my
  first draft had 217 and was wrong. The one lower-case form (block 19,
  word 361) stands in a maxim about giving bread that the old man puts
  to the Prisoner. The 81 you-forms of the chapter are the brothers to
  each other, the crowd to the mother (block 6, word 229), and eight
  inside quotations in the old man's speech (Christ to men, peoples to
  one another, the people to the Church); none is said to the Prisoner.
  Merriam-Webster marks thou archaic, used especially in ecclesiastical
  or literary language (and by Quakers); the Online Etymology
  Dictionary calls it the now-little-used pronoun of the second person
  singular, superseded in Middle English by the plural form. Both
  entries, and those for hinder, semblance, heretic and heresy, were
  opened once more at the very end and say what is given here.
- hinder: Online Etymology Dictionary (Old English, to harm, check; a
  root meaning behind; ground sense to put or keep back). Five uses in
  the chapter, all the old man's: block 8, words 50 and 57; block 14,
  word 154; block 20, word 1053; block 21, word 930. The Russian has the
  same verb in all five (Wiktionary: to hinder, to impede).
- semblance: Online Etymology Dictionary (likeness, appearance); the
  Russian word in Wiktionary is glossed semblance, likeness. The later
  question that takes the Prisoner's identity as known: block 20, words
  1093 to 1100, word 3,328 of 4,977, hence "two thirds of the way".
- heretics: Online Etymology Dictionary, heretic and heresy (Greek for
  able to choose; a choosing for oneself). The Russian word is the same
  Greek word through Old Church Slavonic (Wiktionary). The complaint
  about free choice: block 19, words 456 to 490 and 641 to 669. Nothing
  I read says Dostoevsky meant the link; the caption sets the two facts
  side by side and claims no more. This is in `open`.
