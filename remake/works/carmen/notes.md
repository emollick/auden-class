### L. E. Kastner, A History of French Versification (Oxford, 1903)
Full text of the scan at archive.org (item historyoffrenchv00kastuoft);
the running heads stand at the top of each page.
- p. 1: French verse rests on a fixed number of syllables and on rhyme;
  the last sounded syllable of the line must be stressed; the other
  accents are free. p. 2: no two readers agree in all the accents.
- p. 4: the mute e at the end of a line is never counted. p. 5: inside
  the line it is elided before a vowel, and a stop does not prevent
  that. p. 7 (VI): supported by a consonant and followed by a consonant
  it counts as a syllable, as do -es and -ent.
- p. 7, note 1: in singing the mute e at the end of a line is always
  pronounced in full (his examples are from Gounod's Faust).
- pp. 63-65: the rule that masculine and feminine rhymes alternate.
  Ronsard recommended it so that verse should be fitter for music;
  p. 65: he and Du Bellay still thought of poetry as meant to be sung,
  "in which case the so-called e mute of feminine endings was, and
  still is, audible like any other vowel"; the rule has been kept
  although most poetry has long been parted from music.
- pp. 140-42: the line of eight syllables. Now almost confined to
  lyrical poetry; in the eighteenth century favoured for lyrical
  pieces, the ode and all lighter poetry; p. 142 and note 1: till the
  early seventeenth century the metre of comedy (he quotes Vauquelin
  de la Fresnaye: the line of eight suits comedies as the line of
  twelve suits grave tragedies).
- p. 201: the four-line stanza of two alexandrines and two lines of
  eight on cross rhymes is very common; lines of six often replace the
  eights, "as in Malherbe's famous Consolation à Monsieur du Périer".

### More of Kastner (same scan), read for the two later passages
- p. 82: the cut (cesura) of the line of twelve falls after the sixth
  syllable, which must be a stressed one.
- p. 107: overflow (enjambement) defined: the sense is not complete at
  the end of the line and runs into the next.
- p. 112: the classical rule allows it when the overflow fills the
  whole of the next line. pp. 113-14: with overflow the reader glides
  over the rhyme word to the next line.
- pp. 147-48: the line of twelve (alexandrine) has been since the
  sixteenth century the standard line of tragedy and of all serious
  verse.
