# Volpone: the poet's notes

Written 2026-10-03. The checker, as last run:
`node build/tools/check-reading.mjs volpone` ends with "no problems",
"no warnings" and "the printed notes are fit to print".

Words of the play are pointed at by block, row and word number in the
hosted text (`volpone/<part>`), as `show.mjs` and `words.mjs` print
them. Single words are named where a scansion or a gloss needs them.

## 1. The view the piece is built on

Auden left almost nothing on this play, and the piece says so.

- **On Volpone itself.** `remake/research/C_auden_on_works.md`, section
  10 (lines 271 to 288) and appendix H10 (line 1155): one sentence of
  table talk. He had a special liking for The Alchemist and
  Bartholomew Fair, called Volpone "really too unpleasant", and said
  his favorites were now the masques. The notes give it from Alan
  Ansen, The Table Talk of W. H. Auden (1990), "the entry immediately
  before the one dated January 15, 1947", marked verified from a search
  snippet. I did not see the book. It is not among the scans. The
  entry's own date and page are not known. The research notes say
  Ansen's record runs from November 1946, so the view says "between
  November 1946 and January 1947" (it first said "in the winter of
  1946 to 1947", which a November entry would have made wrong), and
  `view.source` says the page was not seen. The research notes label
  the remark "January 1947" in one place; that is their shorthand for
  the entry before the one of January 15.
- **Nothing else.** I searched the four scans for the play's name:
  0 hits in `dh.txt`, `fa.txt`, `ef.txt` and `pearce.txt`. The research
  notes say the same (line 278), and that no record of a 1941 class on
  Volpone was found (line 1009).
- **On Jonson's comedy as a kind**, which is what the piece leans on:
  - The Dyer's Hand, "The Globe", p. 177: Jonson's comedies are
    classical where Shakespeare's are Christian; classical comedy ends
    with its people exposed and punished, Christian comedy with them
    exposed and forgiven. `dh.txt` lines 8376 to 8385. The running head
    "178 ]" follows, so the sentences are on p. 177. The one quotation
    of the piece (24 words) is from here and was compared with the scan
    word by word.
  - The Dyer's Hand, "The Globe", p. 173: Jonson is the one highbrow
    of the playwrights; his debt to the morality play is greater than
    his debt to Latin comedy; remove Everyman, make a deadly sin the
    hero, let the other six profit by it, and that is the basic pattern
    of his comedy of humors. `dh.txt` lines 8182 to 8188; the head for
    p. 174 follows (the last line of p. 173 breaks off in the middle of
    a word).
  - The Dyer's Hand, "The Joker in the Pack", p. 252: the first sight
    of Iago and Roderigo together is like a comedy by Jonson, a clever
    rascal cheating a rich fool, and the fool deserves it. `dh.txt`
    lines 11990 to 11999.
  - Forewords and Afterwords, "The Greeks and Us" (1948), p. 30: the
    second type of comedy, invented by the Greeks, which grew into
    three kinds, one of them the comedy of humor that Jonson wrote.
    `fa.txt` lines 1486 to 1506. In this scan the page number stands at
    the foot (30, at line 1519) and the head "WH AUDEN" at line 1472.
- **What the piece does with them.** Auden did not apply any of these
  sentences to these lines. The page sets them beside the passages.
  That is said in the first caveat and in `open`. No caption says he
  said anything about a line of this play.
- **Seen and not used.** `fa.txt` line 16730 (the essay on Kipling):
  a remark that Jonson wrote a prose draft and then versified it. It is
  about his way of writing verse, not about this play, and I did not
  fix its page. The description on p. 30 says the offender collides
  with others who are outside the law, and that the law does not stoop
  to him; in Volpone a court does judge him. The piece reports the
  description under "More from Auden" and does not claim it fits.

## 2. The passages, and why these

### First passage: `volpone/a1s1`, rows 3.0 to 3.1 and 5.0 to 5.10

The first speech of Act 1. Chosen because a reader can see the whole
play's subject in its words: thirteen lines in which the words for gold
are the words of worship. Three things can be drawn on it: the words
(saint, hail, adoration, relic, sacred, blessed, shrine), the sentence
that holds back its verb and subject for three lines (5.0 word 6 to 5.3
word 1), and the two greetings of six syllables (3.0 words 0 to 4; 5.0
words 0 to 5).

The play itself confirms the reading of the words. In the same speech,
row 5.18, he calls the gold his saint again, and row 5.19 calls riches
a god. In `a3s6` block 128, row 5, Bonario speaks of an altar and calls
the gold Volpone's idol.

The heading says "the first lines of Act 1" and not "of the play": the
Epistle, the Argument and the Prologue stand before it.

The speech runs on to row 5.24, where Mosca breaks in. I stopped at
5.10, the end of a sentence, to keep the passage to thirteen lines.

### Second passage: `volpone/a3s6`, rows 106.0 to 106.9 and 106.14 to 106.17

The song to Celia. Chosen because two things can be seen side by side:
what Jonson took from Catullus, and what he added. The addition is the
closing rule (rows 14 to 17), which says the crime is to be seen. The
play answers it in the same scene (block 131, row 2: he is unmasked),
in the court (`a4s2` block 96, rows 10 to 11 and 22 to 25: taken in the
act; crimes; this foul fact), in the last scene (`a5s8` blocks 189 and
190: he uncases; block 220: Bonario on crimes that could not be hid)
and in the judge's last speech (third passage, row 265.1).

**The cut.** The song has eighteen lines and a passage may have
fourteen. I left out rows 106.10 to 106.13: two questions, whether the
eyes of a few household spies cannot be deluded, and whether "his" ears
cannot be beguiled, he being removed by their trick. The man is not
named there. The heading gives the line numbers shown, the Word caption
says what the four lines ask, and a caveat says they are left out. I
kept rows 0 to 9 whole because the Catullus lines are there, and rows
14 to 17 because the rule is the point.

### Third passage: `volpone/a5s8`, rows 265.0 (words 5 to 7), 265.1 to 265.5, 269.0 to 269.5

The first judge's last speech and Volpone's six lines to the audience.
Chosen because the moral and the plea stand together, and because the
words in them have been used before in the play: the beasts, fat, feed
and bleed of the judge (see `a1s1` block 9, rows 4 to 5, and `a5s7`
block 3, row 6), the crimes of the song, and the censure of the court
(`a5s8` block 246, row 2) and of Jonson's own Epistle (`epistle` block
3). This is also where Auden's sentence on classical comedy can be laid
beside Jonson's own defense of an ending that punishes.

Row 265.0 is shown from word 5. Words 0 to 4 finish the sentence of the
speech before (block 261, row 3), about the judgments. The build shows
the two directions that stand between the speeches (blocks 266 and
267).

**No Beat tab here.** Row 269.4 has eleven syllables and can be scanned
two ways; 265.4 needs an elision; in 269.0, 269.2 and 269.5 one word
each can be said with two syllables or three (or three or four). Too
many marks would be doubtful, so the passage has Rhyme, Figure, Word
and Names.

### Passages considered and not taken

- Mosca on parasites (`a3s1`): good verse, but nothing Auden's view
  bears on.
- The mountebank scene (`a2s1`): mostly prose.
- The speech to Celia after the song (`a3s6` blocks 110 to 116): the
  second song there takes up Catullus's counting of kisses. It is why
  the caption does not say Jonson dropped the kisses.
- The sentences themselves (`a5s8` blocks 238 to 261): used as facts
  for the Names tab.

## 3. Scansion: the lines I was unsure of

Marks follow a spoken reading. The meter is fixed by the syllable count
and the place of the beats. Where the voice can go two ways I list both.
The captions lean only on the syllables marked "sure".

### First passage (blank verse)

- 3.0: word 0 marked light. An actor could stress it. For that reason
  the caption does not count the stresses of this greeting. It says
  only that three light syllables run together before word 4 (the
  second syllable of word 1, then words 2 and 3). Sure.
- 3.1: the stress on the first syllable of word 0 falls off the beat.
  The beat on word 4 carries no stress in my marks; a light stress
  there is the other hearing.
- 5.0: words 0, 2, 3 and 5 stressed. Sure for 0, 3 and 5. Word 2 I take
  as stressed too: it is a noun, and it carries the contrast with word
  5 (the world's, and mine). A reader who let word 2 go as light as the
  article before it would have three stresses and no two together. I
  think that reading is forced, and do not count it a fair second
  hearing; at the least word 2 is a weighty syllable in a light place,
  which is the case Baum describes. Word 6 is marked light and could
  take a stress. Word 9 ends the line on a beat with no stress: the
  sense runs on.
- 5.2: the beats on words 1 and 5 carry no stress.
- 5.3: word 6 counted as two syllables (`div`). As three, the line has
  eleven. Word 4 is marked light; set against word 7 it could be
  stressed. The caption says only where the first beat falls (word 1),
  which holds either way. That word 1 and not word 0 takes the stress
  I count as sure: it is the subject, set against the earth of the
  comparison.
- 5.5: both instances of the same word (1 and 7) marked light; the
  second could take a stress.
- 5.6: words 0 and 1 both stressed, and word 5 with the first syllable
  of word 6. A plainer reading stresses word 1 and lets word 0 go.
- 5.7: word 0 stressed on its first syllable, against the measure. It
  can also be said with the stress on the second, which keeps the
  measure. Word 2 counted as two syllables (`div`). Words 3 and 4: I
  stress 3; the other hearing stresses 4.
- 5.8: the beat on word 2 carries no stress.
- 5.9: word 1 has four syllables, with a lesser stress on the first;
  word 4 counted as two syllables. Eleven syllables, the last light.
- 5.10: words 1 and 5 each two syllables (`div`). Word 4 marked light;
  it could be stressed, off the beat.
- Rows 5.0 and 5.3 end on words that chime. Nothing is made of it. The
  caption says "no pattern of rhyme", not "unrhymed".

### Second passage (seven syllables, four beats)

- 106.0: word 2 counted as two syllables (`div`).
- 106.1: word 0 stands on a beat and is marked light.
- 106.4: word 1 marked light; a stress there would fall off the beat.
- 106.6: words 0 and 1 light.
- 106.7: word 3 counted as three syllables (`div`).
- 106.8: the beat on word 2 carries no stress; it could.
- 106.9: words 3 and 4 light; word 4 could be stressed.
  For these ten lines the caption says the voice "can" keep every

- 106.14: word 1 light and word 3 stressed, off the beat. The other

- 106.15: words 2 and 3 both stressed. Sure for word 3, a noun, which
  stands off the beat whatever is done with word 2. Word 2 stands on
  the beat.
- 106.16: two stresses, on word 2 and word 5. Sure.
- 106.17: word 4 stressed as the rhyme word; it could be light.

### English words

A New English Dictionary on Historical Principles (the first edition of
the Oxford dictionary), read in the Internet Archive's page text of
each volume, saved in the scratchpad as `ned1.txt` to `ned10a.txt`
(archive items oed01arch, oed02arch, oed04arch,
newenglishdict05murrmiss, oed6aarch, oed6barch,
newenglishdic07murruoft, oed8aarch, oed8barch, oed9aarch, oed9barch
and oedxaarch, and one of the Archive's scans of volume 3). The page
text is machine-read and has misprints; I read each entry through and
did not lean on a single doubtful letter. Every gloss was taken from
the entry, opened again after the reading was drafted.

- Shrine, sb.: from Latin scrinium, a case or chest for books or
  papers. Sense 1, marked obsolete: a box, coffer, cabinet, chest; last
  examples 1516 (Edinburgh), 1560 (Stirling), 1658 (Phillips's
  wordbook). Sense 2: the box, casket or other repository in which a
  saint's relics are kept. Both are recorded from about 1000. Sense 5:
  a place where worship is offered or devotions are paid to a saint or
  deity (first example 1629). Sense 5c: figurative, in contexts
  referring to the veneration or idolizing of some person or thing
  (examples of 1575 and 1595, and then this play). **The dictionary
  quotes rows 3.0 and 3.1 of the first passage here, dated 1605.** I
  missed this on the first readings of the entry and found it on the
  last, when I read the entry to its end. The gloss keeps sense 2,
  because what is opened to show a saint is the thing that holds the
  saint, and because sense 5 has no plain example before 1629; the
  note beside the passage now reports the dictionary's quotation, and a
  caveat says how the dictionary files the lines. The chest sense is no
  longer claimed for the line (see section 6).
- After that find I searched all thirteen saved volumes for the other
  lines of the three passages (forty phrases, in old and new spelling).
  No other line of them is quoted. The page text is machine-read, so a
  quotation with a misread word could have been missed.
- Hail, int.: a greeting, equal to Latin ave; first example "Hail thou,
  Marie". Hail Mary: the angel's salutation, Ave Maria; a devotional
  recitation.
- Adoration 1; Adore (Latin adorare: to address, salute, reverence; in

- Antithesis 1. Simile 1.
- Morality 4b: the name modern writers give to the kind of drama,
  popular in the 16th century, that taught a moral or spiritual lesson
  and whose chief characters were personifications of abstract
  qualities. Examples: the half-title of a 1773 reprint of Everyman

### How the verse works

Paull F. Baum, The Principles of English Versification (1922),
`.cache/handbooks/baum-versification-pg21342.txt`:

- chapter 4, line 4741: blank verse, the unrhymed line of five
  stresses, the line of nearly all Elizabethan drama; line 2430: the
  feminine ending;
- chapter 4, lines 3143 to 3215: the short couplet of four stresses,
  used for lyric by Shakespeare and other dramatists; the repeated
  short unit gives an effect of hurry; the quick return of the rhyme
  makes the couplet a unit; a couplet can be divided so that its second
  line belongs to a new sentence;
- chapter 5, line 5838: alliteration as a means of emphasis;
- chapter 5, lines 6955 to 6985: a line with less than the normal
  stress forces attention on the important words; a stressed
  monosyllable crowded into a light place slows the tempo.

### The sentence held in suspense

Edwin A. Abbott, How to Write Clearly (Boston, 1883), rules 30 and 31,
in Project Gutenberg no. 22600: a sentence the reader feels to be
incomplete until its main words come is in suspense; suspense throws
increased emphasis on the words waited for; a long suspense is
tolerable at the opening of a poem (his examples are from Paradise
Lost). Also William Strunk, The Elements of Style (New York: Harcourt,
Brace; the title page gives copyright 1918 and 1919 by Strunk and 1920
by the publisher), rule 18, in Project Gutenberg no. 37134, and
Wikipedia, "Periodic sentence". The first draft of this caption stood
on Wikipedia alone.

## 6. What I changed after reading it again

- "The Greeks and Us": the page is 30, not 29 (the number stands at the
  foot in that scan).
- Glosses that had been written from memory were replaced by the
  dictionary's words: hail (no "reverent"), adoration (the Latin as the
  entry gives it), teeming (pregnant, fertile), shrine (no "first" and
  "later"; what was then done with its chest sense was undone later,
  see the last item of this section).
- "Unrhymed" became "no pattern of rhyme" (rows 5.0 and 5.3 chime).
- The song's Rhyme caption said each pair was closed. Two are not. It
  now says "twice a pair is split", from a count of all nine.
- "Jonson drops the kisses" was cut (the second song has them). "A
  husband's ears" became "a man's ears" (the lines do not name him).
- The song's Beat caption says the voice "can" keep the beat for ten
  lines.
- The first Beat caption counted two stresses in the greeting to the
  day. An actor could stress its first word, so the caption now rests
  on what is sure: three light syllables together there, four stresses
  in the greeting to the gold, two of them side by side.
- The Sentence caption now stands on Abbott's rules on suspense.
- "In the plays of Shakespeare's time" became "on Shakespeare's stage",
  which is what Abbott covers.
- The third paraphrase of Auden was tightened: he says the Greek type
  developed into the comedy of humor and into other kinds, and a phrase
  that was his own wording was put in mine.
- "The chief persons" became "Five persons". The simile's parts were
  retagged so the drawing joins the right two. The Names tab was
  labeled "Names". The title, the heading of the first passage and the
  date in the view were made exact.
- Captions over 110 words were cut; they now run from 105 to 110.
- A last pass compared every string of the reading, and these notes,
  with the hosted play and with the scans of Auden, looking for runs of
  the same words. Three runs of more than six words of the play were
  reworded (the antithesis caption, one word note, one working claim).
  The paraphrases of Auden were put further into my own words, so that
  the one quotation of 24 words is the only place where more than five
  of his words stand together. The one exception is the name of the
  seven deadly sins with "one of" before it, which cannot be said
  another way.
- After that pass, three things more. The paraphrase of p. 173 under
