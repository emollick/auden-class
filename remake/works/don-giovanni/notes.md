## What Auden said, and where I read it

Read in this session in the scans (scratchpad `research/auden/`). Line numbers are those of the scan files.

- The Dyer's Hand (1962), `dh.txt`:
  - "Balaam and His Ass", section VI, pp. 118-20 (scan lines 5655-5763). p. 118: the two lines of Leporello's first aria set as the motto of the section. p. 119: the man who will serve no end can be shown in a drama only as a man with one chosen end, chosen arbitrarily; such is Don Giovanni; his end is to "know" every woman in the world; three lines of the catalogue aria quoted; the sensual libertine (the Duke in Rigoletto) set against him; the Don's pleasure "not sensual but arithmetical", the name added to the list kept by Leporello; he should look so neutral that he might as well have chosen to collect stamps. p. 120: only together with his servant, as Giovanni-Leporello, can he be understood; the audience laughs at Leporello's first words because he has none of the qualities of a master; by the end one suspects the servant was the master, since he keeps the list; the Don is never seen to look at it or to take pleasure in it and only reports the latest name.
  - "The Prince's Dog", pp. 183-84 (scan lines 8640-8680): Don Giovanni's true home is music; a Don Giovanni with no name on his list is inconceivable; time for him is an endless arithmetical series of unrelated moments, which Heaven cuts short; he must be seen to sink into the earth.

## The three passages, and why

1. **The count** (`o-don-giovanni/catalogue`, rows 0.0 to 4.3; the English beside it, `don-giovanni/act-1` rows 243.0 to 243.9). The first eight lines of Leporello's catalogue: the announcement and the four lines of totals. Chosen because Auden's sentence about the Don (the pleasure is arithmetical, the list is kept by the servant) can be seen in the verse itself. The line is the ten-syllable line with fixed accents; in the four lines of totals the accents fall on countries and figures; the rhymes are made out of numbers and out of the servant's first person. Lenses: Beat, Rhyme, Words, English.
2. **The close** (rows 7.0 to 9.3 of the same cut; English rows 244.7 to 244.14). The last eight lines of the aria. Rows 9.0 to 9.2 are the three lines Auden quotes on p. 119. Rows 7.0 and 7.1 say outright that the pleasure is in putting the women on the list, in a couplet that rhymes the word for conquest with the word for list. Rows 8.0 and 8.1 give the one stated preference, the young beginner. I first meant to leave rows 8.0 and 8.1 out because this printing garbles them (a stray semicolon, a missing accent). I put them back for two reasons: they are the one place in the close where the Don is given a taste, and hiding it would flatter Auden's reading; and Auden's own account of the myth (the first lover of every woman) fits exactly this couplet. Lenses: Words, Figure (anaphora), Rhyme, English.
3. **The duet** (`o-don-giovanni/duet`, rows 1.0 to 4.2; English rows 357.0-357.3, 359.0-359.3, 361.0, 363.0, 365.0, 367.0, 373.0-373.1). All fifteen lines of the duet text. pieces.json gives this as the first lead (the form is the seduction). It is one line over the brief's fourteen and under the checker's sixteen. It cannot be cut: the point is the whole scheme, his stanza, her stanza in the same pattern, four single lines in which she supplies his rhymes, three lines for both. The English rows skip blocks 369 and 371, which print sung repeats of the first two stanzas. Lenses: Rhyme, Voices (a sentence lens on the verbs), Words, English.

Passages considered and not used: Leporello's first aria (the two lines Auden uses as a motto are there, lines 43-44 of the source file); the Don's own song about the list (lines 840-843); the recitative couplet that first rhymes conquest with list (lines 236-237), which the Rhyme lens of the second passage points to.

## The scansion of the first passage, and the other ways of hearing it

Eight lines, the Italian ten-syllable line (decasillabo) with fixed accents on the third, sixth and ninth syllables; rows 3.0 and 4.3 stop on the ninth (tronco). I counted each line by word number against the tool's listing and then against the checker's proof, where every syllable is printed with its mark. Thirteen pairs of vowels meet between words and count as one (the mark `~` is on the first of each pair, as the three Dante pieces and La traviata mark it).

- Row 1.0, words 3 and 4. The verb ends in a stressed vowel and the article after it begins with a vowel; the two make the sixth syllable. I kept the stress dot on the verb's last syllable and marked the article as the uncounted one. The drawing ties an uncounted syllable to the syllable after it, so the arc from the article runs forward, which is not where the article leans. The other way is to mark the verb's last syllable as uncounted and put the stress on the article: every arc is then true, but a stress is drawn under a word that has none. I chose the first because the test is reading aloud, and remark 2 says in words what happens. Paradiso 47.1 has a precedent for an uncounted article tied forward. If the lead prefers the second way, the change is `"x~ /"` for words 3 and 4 in place of `"x/ ~"`.
- Row 1.0, word 5. A clipped noun stressed on its last syllable, right before the stress on the ninth. Marked as a light stress. A reader could give it a full stress (the page would then show a red dot on the eighth syllable) or none.
- Row 0.0, word 3, and row 2.0, word 3. The word for is. In row 2.0 it carries the sixth accent. In row 0.0 it stands on the eighth syllable, merged with the vowel before it, and I left it unstressed; a light stress would also be fair.
- Row 4.2, word 0. The hundred at the head of the line: its first syllable is the first of the line, where the measure has no accent. Marked light. A full stress is possible and would be drawn as a stress off the measure, which is what remark 5 says in words.
- Row 4.2, word 4. Counted as two syllables, the last two vowels taken as one (sineresi). With the vowels apart the line would have eleven syllables, so the count needs it.
- Row 4.3, words 4 and 5. The sixth accent falls on the word for already, and the thousand follows with a full stress on the seventh. A reader who stresses the thousand and lets already go light would be hearing the sense against the measure; I marked both as full, and the page shows the second in red as a stress off the measure.
- The whole scansion is of the verse as read. In the music words are repeated (the English libretto prints some repeats) and Auden and Kallman say sung rhythm is not spoken rhythm (p. 485). The second caveat says so. Nothing was checked against a score.

The second and third passages are not scanned. Their lines are eight-syllable lines (the close) and seven-syllable lines (the duet); I read the entries Ottonario (Enciclopedia Italiana, 1935) and Settenario (Enciclopedia dell'Italiano, 2011) but make no claim from them in the reading.
- Two more small differences in the duet, seen when I read the Rullman Italian again: in row 1.3 it has another preposition before the word for here, and in row 3.2 another spelling of the verb. Before the last three lines Rullman's Italian prints Duetto and its English prints TOGETHER, where this printing has the direction made of the letter a and the figure 2. My gloss of that direction (both together) rests on those two, not on a dictionary.

## Where each fact was read

All pages were fetched and read on 2026-10-03. Saved copies are in the scratchpad under `dg/`. Treccani addresses have the form `https://www.treccani.it/vocabolario/<word>/` or `https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/<entry>/`.

**The verse line and the count (first passage, Beat).**
- Treccani, Enciclopedia on line, "Decasillabo": accents on the 3rd, 6th and 9th, no caesura, very catchy (*molto orecchiabile*).
- Enciclopedia Italiana (1931), "Decasillabo": a rising rhythm in threes with fixed accents on the third, sixth and ninth; the regularity of the cadence makes it monotonous but effective.
- Claudio Ciociola, "Versificazione", Enciclopedia dell'Italiano (2011): the last stressed position names the line, plus one; unstressed syllables after it do not change the name; a line ending on a stressed syllable is tronco.
- Sergio Bozzola and Luciano Romito, "Sinalefe", Enciclopedia dell'Italiano (2011): the final vowel of one word and the first of the next are counted as one metrical syllable so that the measure is kept; both vowels are still sounded; it can happen across a comma or semicolon. The same article has a section on sineresi. Treccani, Enciclopedia on line, "Sineresi": two or more vowels inside a word counted as one metrical syllable.
- That no woman is named in the aria: I read lines 333-365 of the source file with `original.mjs show` and searched them for capital letters inside lines; the only proper names are the five countries.
- The five totals: 640, 231, 100, 91, 1,003; sum 2,065 (rows 4.0-4.3). The eighteen hundred: Leporello's bracketed aside at line 268 of the source file; hosted English block 177.

**Rhyme (all three passages).**
- Claudio Ciociola, "Rima", Enciclopedia dell'Italiano (2011): two lines rhyme when identical from the last stressed vowel on (this is how I set the `tails`); a rhyme may be inside the line, at the end of a half-line (row 9.0); the clipped rhyme in a consonant, of the type that ends the duet, is called characteristic of the ode-canzonetta. I did not use that last point in a caption; it would fit Auden and Kallman's remark that libretto verse was convention.
- Paull F. Baum, The Principles of English Versification (1922), chapter 4 (`.cache/handbooks/baum-versification-pg21342.txt`, lines 3208-3211): the quick return of the rhyme makes the couplet felt as a unit holding one idea. Baum writes of English verse. I use the sentence for what a couplet does, and the caption does not say it of Italian in particular.
- The earlier rhyme of conquest and list: lines 235-237 of the source file (Leporello to the Don); hosted English block 152, which adds a request for the lady's name that the Italian does not have. My caption follows the Italian.
- Row 9.3 rhymes with row 5.5 (line 349 of the source file): both stop on a stressed final vowel.
