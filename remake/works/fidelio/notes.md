### The Dyer's Hand (1962), "Notes on Music and Opera", pp. 465-74

Read through in the scan (research/auden/dh.txt, lines 21893-22327); pp. 469-73 read
again line by line on the last pass. Page map by the running heads, which stand at the
top of the page here too (the research notes verified p. 472 on the page image): p. 469
from line 22066, p. 470 from 22110, p. 471 from 22157, p. 472 from 22204, p. 473 from
22253, p. 474 from 22301. In my own words:

- p. 469: he quotes Shaw, who takes Beethoven's side against Mozart for giving high
  feelings to low characters, and disagrees with the two of them: such feelings are
  everybody's, and opera, unlike the spoken play, can show it. Beethoven is named, the
  opera is not. Not used.
- p. 471: drama rests on a mistake (among his examples a supposed chambermaid who is in
  fact a nobleman, young and disguised); a good play has two phases, the mistake made
  and the mistake found out. What is sung is so: self-deception cannot be sung. A spoken play
  may uncover the mistake slowly; a libretto must make the recognition abrupt. The
  sentence ends in the seven-word clause that the third plate's Beat caption quotes
  (dh.txt lines 22191-22192).
- pp. 471-72: a libretto needs a melodrama for a plot, one that puts its people in
  situations beyond what words can carry; nobody sings in a sensible mood.
- p. 472: where a libretto has sensible passages, he glosses them as talk and not song.
  An opera's orchestra plays for the singers and not for the listeners; an interlude is
  borne, even liked, so long as the singers must rest or the scene is being shifted, and
  any other playing by the orchestra alone is time wasted; Leonora No. 3, fine music at
  a concert, is acutely boring for its twelve minutes if it comes between the two
  scenes of Act 2 of Fidelio. This is the only place where the scan has the name
  Fidelio: the whole scan (25,138 lines) was searched for it with allowance for misread
  letters, and for Leonor-, Florestan, -delio, Pizarr-, Rocco and Marzell-; Beethoven
  is named on two other lines of the scan (the passage on Shaw above, and a mention of
  his last quartets in another essay), neither about the opera. Poetry reflects and
  will not stop at the cries of feeling; music is immediate; so what is written to be
  sung is not poetry.
- p. 473: the librettist's verses are meant for the composer, as a private letter is,
  and their great moment is the one in which they give him a melody. He believes a
  listener to song hears syllables and not words; the syllable values settle the vocal
  line; so he is on the whole against opera in translation.

The reading's two quotations of Auden: the fifteen-word sentence in `view.quote` (LS,
p. 225) and the seven-word clause in the Beat caption of the third plate (DH, p. 471).
Everything else of his is paraphrase.

## 5. The plates: why these passages

Auden left nothing on this opera but one comparison, made in passing in 1947, and one
complaint about the overture. The piece is built on the comparison, and on what he
wrote about librettos in general, each time said to be general.

1. **Leonore's answer** (Act 2, Scene 5; o-fidelio/answer rows 4.0-15.0, with the
   hosted English). This is the one place in the opera Auden pointed at. Shown in the
   uncut German against an English made from the shortened text, it lets a reader see
   three things at once: the answer Auden's class heard is the shortened one (compare
   lens); the place is the seam between speech and song, with every reasoning sentence
   on the spoken side and six verbless exclamations on the sung side (sentence lens,
   with his remark that a libretto's sensible passages are talk, and that words for
   song are not poetry); and the first words sung say of themselves that they cannot
   name what is felt (word lens, with Adelung on the two words and Auden on situations
   beyond words).
2. **Leonore alone** (Act 1, Scene 6; o-fidelio/aria rows 0.0-4.0 and 12.0-20.0). The
   one place where she gives the ground of what she does, and she gives two: a drive
   and a duty. That is the pair of Auden's sentence on Cordelia (a love wanted free and
   yet called a duty), and it is why this passage stands beside the answer. The verse
   can be seen to change its measure and its rhyme pattern at the two places where she
   turns, and the score divides there too (beat and rhyme lenses, with Auden on verses
   written for the composer). The English beside it keeps neither the measure nor the
   two nouns (compare lens, with Auden on syllables and translation).
3. **She says who she is** (Act 2, Scene 3; o-fidelio/recognition rows 1.0-13.0).
   Auden's general account of drama (a mistake made, then found out) and of recognition
   in a libretto (abrupt) is shown on the opera's own recognition: three speeches
   ending on one word with one word changing before it (figure lens), eight syllables
   among four voices (beat lens), the word itself as Adelung describes its use (word
   lens), and the count of who says which of her two names (shape lens).
