## Whose verse it is

The hosted text is William and Charles Archer's translation (Project
Gutenberg no. 66239, the revised text of 1907). The verse on every plate
is theirs. William Archer's Introduction says how it was made: Ibsen's
play is rhymed from first to last; the translation keeps his measures,
gives up his rhymes, and follows him line for line. So the beats and
the line ends on the plates are the Archers' after Ibsen, and the rhymes
are Ibsen's alone. Where a caption speaks of Ibsen's own words or rhymes
it says so, and the fact was read in the University of Oslo's edition
(see "Sources").

One thing found on the way. Auden gives the human motto in the wording
of the Everyman translation by R. Farquharson Sharp, the edition named
on his Michigan sheet. But the lines of the Button-Moulder and the Lean
One that he quotes on p. 439 of The Dyer's Hand are word for word the
Archers' (scan lines 20781-20798 against the hosted a5s9 rows 33.0-33.3
and a5s10 block 101, rows 1 to 7). The Everyman wording of the first of
these is different (scan of that edition, lines 14456-14463). So the
verse shown here is verse Auden himself quoted. This is said in the
first `auden` entry and in the Word caption of plate 3.

## The point of view

Auden's view leads the piece (`view`). Three things he said, and where:

- The play keeps asking one question, who am I, and answers it in
  various ways. The Dyer's Hand (1962), "Genius & Apostle", p. 436. Read
  in the scan, `research/auden/dh.txt`, lines 20646-20648. The scan
  misprints two words of the sentence; the quotation follows the
  research notes, section 17, where it is marked as checked at the page.
- In February 1941 he wrote that every answer is provisional, Solveig's
  included, and that Peer has the Button-Moulder still to meet. "The
  Wandering Jew", The New Republic, 10 February 1941 (Prose II, pp.
  110-13). Read only as an extract in
  `remake/research/C_auden_on_works.md` (section 17, and line 1201 of
  the appendix). The book is not among the scans. This is in `open`.
- Peer is Ibsen's picture of the poet, and almost everything he does is
  a part he plays. The Dyer's Hand, p. 438, scan lines 20730-20734.

The three plates are three answers to the one question: the troll
king's (two mottos, and his verdict in Act 5), Peer's own (the onion),
and Solveig's, with the Button-Moulder's unfinished sentence after it.
The title's two riddles are both called riddles in the text (a2s6 row
36.1; a5s10 rows 211.0 and 222.0).

Auden's pages were pinned from the running heads of the scan, which
stand at the top of each page: p. 110 (head at line 5191; passage
5199-5206), p. 194 (head 9177; passage 9196-9213), p. 436 (head 20612;
sentence 20646-20648), p. 437 (head 20661; passage 20709-20712), p. 438
(head 20719; passages 20730-20734, 20747-20751, 20759-20765), p. 439

## Plate 1, "two-sayings": a2s6 rows 42.2-49.1, beside a5s8 rows 71.1, 75.0, 79.0-79.1, 82.0

Why this passage. It is the place Auden points at (p. 439): the king
says what separates trolls from men, in two mottos. The two sayings are
built alike, so the eye can lay one on the other. The same words come
back in Act 5, which lets the plate show the return.

The lead said "one word differs". That is true of the Everyman
translation, where the two commands differ by one word (scan of that
edition, `research/texts/peer_gynt_sharp_everyman_1921_ia_ocr.txt`,
lines 4186-4187). It is not true of the hosted text. In the Archers the
men's command is two words, and the trolls' has those two turned round
after a "to", then a dash and one more word. In Ibsen the trolls'
command is the men's three words with a fourth added (Oslo edition, Act
2). The captions describe the hosted text and Ibsen; the Everyman
wording is in the first `auden` entry.

Rows. The stretch is continuous (42.2 to 49.1) because the build shows
every speaker name and stage direction that lies between two shown
blocks; a broken selection would pile up names. The Act 5 lines are the
`pair`. Pair rows show no speaker names, so the pair's label says that
the last word is Peer's. The pair has 5 rows and the main text 10; equal
numbers would make the build set them line under line.

Marks. The token at 42.7 word 6 joins the verb and the added word across
the dash, so the part tagged "added" and the gloss for the added word
both sit on that joined token. The label "after the dash" says where to
look.

Facts and where they were read.

- The riddle and its name: a2s6 rows 36.0-36.1 and 38.0. Peer sees no
  difference: 40.0. He lets the motto pass: 56.0.
- Act 5: the Old Man now begs (a5s8 block 4 and row 7.0); Peer wants him
  to swear in court (57.1-57.8); "the opposite way" (67.0); which motto,
  which word (73.0, 77.0); recoils (block 81); lived by it since
  (84.0-84.1); lived as a troll (89.1).
- Ibsen's rhyme words at the two line ends, the noun carried over the
  line end, the line shared by the courtier and Peer, the missing dash
  in Act 5, the same two rhyme pairs in both acts: Oslo edition, Acts 2
  and 5.
- The Act 5 line is literally about writing the motto behind the ear:
  the translators' footnote to a5s8 row 71.1 (footnote 135 in the
  Gutenberg text). So the shield that comes back in Act 5 is the
  Archers' and not Ibsen's; the second tie says so.
- enough, sunder, escutcheon: Online Etymology Dictionary. For the cry
  the entry says: as an interjection, meaning that is enough, from about
  1600. An earlier draft glossed the cry as "stop"; that was my word and
  not the entry's, and it is gone. The Oxford English Dictionary's
  public pages were read for enough and escutcheon (origin and earliest
  date only).
- nok means sufficient, and also having reached the limit of what can
  be accepted: Bokmålsordboka, under nok.
- Auden on the two mottos, on the poet being "enough" to himself and on
  Peer outside Norway: The Dyer's Hand, p. 439, scan lines 20775-20779
  and 20803-20809.

Scansion: the other hearings.

- 42.2: word 4 is counted as three syllables; in quick speech it has
  two, and the beats stay where they are. The first beat is on word 2;
  it can be heard on word 1.
- 42.3: the first beat is on word 1; it can be heard on word 0. Four
  beats either way.
- 42.4: word 0 can be stressed beside the first beat.
- 42.5: the beats are marked on word 1, the first syllable of word 3,
  word 5 and the second syllable of word 7. Word 1 is set against the
  trolls of the next line, which is why it has the beat. The line can
  also be heard with the beat on the second syllable of word 0 and word
  1 light, which makes it run like 42.7. Word 4, before the colon, can
  be stressed as well; that gives five stresses.
- 42.6: word 2 can be stressed beside word 1.
- 42.7: word 2, before the colon, can be stressed; that gives five.
- 45.0 and 47.0: two beats each. Ibsen prints them as one line shared
  by the two speakers.
- 49.0: word 5 can be stressed; then the line has five stresses. It has
  thirteen syllables and ends on two light ones.
- 49.1: word 1 can be stressed. The last word is divided by hand into
  three syllables (`div`); the rule finds four.
- Because of 42.5 and 42.7 the Beat caption and its points do not number
  the beats. They say "the last beat" and "the last beat but one", and
  those hold on every hearing listed here. Archer's Introduction says
  that Ibsen now and then has a line of three or of five accents.

## Plate 2, "onion": a5s5 rows 5.1-5.2, 7.0-7.5, 9.0-9.1, 11.0-11.2, 13.0

Why this passage. It is the lead's first choice and the clearest thing
to see in the play: one figure carried through a speech, coat by coat.
It is also where the text bears out Auden's "role": each coat is a part
Peer has played, and Auden names two of them himself (p. 438). Peer's
own phrase for one of them, in Act 4, is that he was playing the prophet
(a4s9 row 6.14).

Rows. A plate holds 14 lines, and the speech is longer. The plate shows
the first three coats, then the handful, the search for the kernel and
the throwing away. It leaves out rows 7.6 to 7.17, twelve lines that
name six more coats. Row 7.17 uses a racial slur and is not shown; the
table gives the coat without it. The build does not mark a gap between
rows, so the plate's heading says "three of nine coats shown" and the
Layers caption says what is left out.

Marks. In the Figure lens the "man" and "onion" parts pair up by order
(the build joins the first with the first, and so on) on the same or the
next line: the man and the onion in 5.1; the outermost layer and the
shipwrecked man; the passenger and his layer; the next coat and the
gold-digger. The later onion words have no partner, which is the thing
to see.

The table (Layers). Every row was checked in the hosted text; the
`source` key of each entry gives the rows. The order of the staged coats
is the order of the scenes run backward: Act 5 scene 2, Act 5 scene 1,
Act 4 scenes 13, 11 and 12, 6 to 8, and 1. Two coats were never staged
(the gold-digger, told of in a5s1 row 50.1 and a5s4 row 133.0; the fur
hunter, named nowhere else: `show.mjs --grep "peltry|Hudson"` finds only
this speech). The trade in slaves, idols and missionaries is told in
a4s1 rows 64.0-64.2 and 70.28-70.29.

Two entries of the table are inferences, and the caption and `open` say
so:

- The coat that looks like a crown (a5s5 rows 7.8-7.9). Peer names no
  man for it. It comes off between the two parts never staged and the
  archaeologist, which is where the last scene of Act 4 falls, and in
  that scene a straw wreath is pressed on Peer's head and he is hailed
  as emperor of the self (a4s13 blocks 221-224). An earlier draft gave
  this as a fact. The caption now says "appears to be".
- The coats pulled off without a name (block 8). By the order they
  appear to be Acts 1 to 3. The text does not say so.

Other facts and where they were read.

- Act 4: Peer strips off the Turkish costume bit by bit (a4s9 block 1),
  throws the turban away (block 3) and says the line that begins like
  a5s5 row 7.0 (a4s9 row 4.0). In Ibsen both lines begin with the same
  two words (Oslo edition). He calls it a matter of clothes (4.2) and
  speaks of his prophetical way of life and of playing the prophet (6.9,
  6.14). An earlier draft said he throws the turban away "with nearly
  the same words"; only the first three words are the same, and the
  caption now says the line begins like the first coat's.
- Kaiser for Ibsen's word for emperor: the translators' footnote 76, at
  Act 4 scene 1. Peer's boast to his mother: a1s1 row 144.0.
- Ibsen's words: jeget for the gold-digger; Lag where the Archers say
  swathings; Svøb elsewhere in the speech; Kjærnen for the kernel; the
  line end that rhymes with Gynt; the split line and the rhyme of its
  second half with the next line: Oslo edition, Act 5. Their meanings:
  Bokmålsordboka, under jeg (noun), lag, svøp, kjerne, tynn.
- kaiser, ego, swathe, kernel: Online Etymology Dictionary. Oxford
  English Dictionary public pages for ego, swathing and kernel. The two
  dictionaries date ego differently (by 1707; 1824), so no date is
  given for it.
- The measure, the rhymes given up, "rub-a-dub", comic and sad speech,
  thirty of thirty-eight scenes: William Archer's Introduction in the
  Gutenberg volume. Act 5 scene 5 is not among the eight scenes he
  lists as being in other measures; nor are Act 2 scene 6, Act 5 scene 8
  and Act 5 scene 10.
- Auden on the empire of one's own consciousness: p. 437, scan lines
  20709-20712. On the role, and the two parts he names: p. 438.

Scansion: the other hearings.

- 5.1: word 0 can be stressed beside the first beat. The last word is
  given two syllables (`div`).
- 5.2: word 7 can be stressed beside the last beat.
- 7.0: word 5 can be stressed beside the last beat.
- 7.2 and 7.3: the contracted words (word 0; word 5) are one syllable
  each (`div`); the rule finds two. In 7.3 word 9 can take a stress,
  since the name has two; that gives five.
- 7.5: word 3 can be stressed.
- 9.0: the first beat is on word 0, an exclamation. It can be heard
  light, which leaves three stresses in the line.
- 9.1: word 0 or word 4 can be stressed.
- 11.0: the last word is given two syllables (`div`); the rule finds
  one.
- 11.2 and 13.0 are two half lines, two beats each, with the stage
  direction between them. Ibsen prints them as one line split the same
  way.
- The Beat caption leans only on the first line (beats on words 1, 3,
  6 and 9) and on the split line. Word 4 of the first line is the same
  word as word 3, so the caption says "the first".

## Plate 3, "answer": a5s10 rows 222.0-226.0, beside rows 241.0-247.1

Why this passage. It is the last answer to Auden's question, and he
quotes it (p. 441). The riddle and the answer can be seen: the same
words begin his two lines and her three phrases, and her line is the one
line here that the translators took out of the measure on purpose. The
six lines beside it are the last of the play: the Button-Moulder's
sentence that stops, and the lullaby coming back in the opposite order.
Auden's remark of 1941 is about exactly this.

Rows. 222.0 is included so that the line Solveig and Peer share can be
seen, and so that the word "riddle" stands on the plate. Main text 5
rows, pair 6.

Facts and where they were read.

- The riddle is set at a5s10 rows 211.0 to 219.3. Peer's reply to her
  answer: block 228 (stage direction) and rows 229.0-229.1.
- The name of the figure. Silva Rhetoricae defines anaphora by clauses,
  sentences and lines. Solveig's three are phrases, so a second source
  was read: Merriam-Webster, under anaphora, which names phrases as
  well.
- The three reprieves: a5s7 row 122.1, a5s9 row 61.0, a5s10 row 174.0
  (this one also breaks off). The first meeting is in scene 7; scene 9
  is set at a cross-road (block 0) and in scene 10 Peer comes on the
  cross-roads again (block 141). An earlier plan of mine counted "three
  cross-roads and a fourth promised"; the text does not say that, and
  the caption says only that he has three times let Peer go on.
- The last stage direction: a5s10 block 246. Block 247 is the last block
  of the hosted play.
- The Button-Moulder on being oneself: a5s9 rows 33.0-33.3. Auden
  quotes those lines on p. 439, in the Archers' wording (see "Whose
  verse it is").
- The translators gave up the meter of Solveig's line, and Ibsen's word
  for love also means charity: their footnote 146, to a5s10 row 226.0.
  It prints Ibsen's line.
- Ibsen's four lines rhyme first with last; the same word, selv, in the
  sayings of Act 2 and in Peer's question; Stempel for sigil; the
  Button-Moulder's sentence breaks off in Ibsen too; the lullaby lines
  reversed with one word added: Oslo edition. Meanings: Bokmålsordboka,
  under selv and stempel.
- sigil: the Online Etymology Dictionary gives three nouns for it:
  sign, mark and seal. An earlier gloss said "a stamped mark";
  "stamped" was mine, taken from Ibsen's word, and the gloss now keeps
  to the entry's three nouns.
- Faith, hope, charity: 1 Corinthians 13:13, King James Version
  (bible-api.com).
- Auden on the exchange and on the doubt Ibsen leaves: p. 441, scan
  lines 20883-20890. His remark that the English word "myself" covers
  two things: p. 438, lines 20747-20751.

Scansion: the other hearings.

- 222.0: word 1 can be stressed in place of the first syllable of word
  2. Two beats either way.
- 224.1: the first beat is on word 2, before the comma. It can be heard
  on word 0 or on word 1. Words 8 and 11 are marked light after their
  adjectives; each can be stressed.
- 224.2: the voice is marked with a stress on word 4 that falls between
  two beats, so the plate shows five stresses against four beats. The
  caption and a point on the plate say so. The beat on the second
  syllable of word 6 is a light one.
- 226.0: three stresses. The measure's fourth beat is drawn on word 7,
  where the voice gives none; it could as well be drawn on word 6. The
  caption says the line has three when read plainly, and gives the
  translators' own note as the reason.
- The Button-Moulder's two lines (244.0-244.1) are not scanned. Five
  stresses compete in each, and no lens needs them.

## Rule three: what was cut or changed

The first draft was written before rule three. In the rewrite every
caption and `why` was taken a sentence at a time, and these went:

- that the difference between the sayings is small enough to miss, and
  that nothing of the men's saying has to be given up to become a troll;
- that the last beat is the strongest place in a line;
- that the peeling goes to the gait of a comic turn, and that Peer has
  dismissed the finding before the verse has closed;
- that the figure tells Peer what he would not have said, and that the
  wrappings are round nothing;
- that the kernel was in another person's keeping, and that Peer asks
  for proof and is given trust;
- that the play closes on two voices that do not answer each other.

None of these stood on the plate, the lines, a source or Auden. Where a
reason was wanted in their place it is now Auden's, with the page, or a
plain fact of the text (Peer's own reply at a5s10 rows 229.0-229.1; the
Act 4 lines in a4s9).

A last pass on 2026-10-03 opened every source again and mended these:

- The essay on p. 110 of The Dyer's Hand was cited by the name of its
  part of the book. It is "Balaam and His Ass".
- A paraphrase changed the grammar: Peer does not say he refused "and
  so remained himself"; he says he refused for the sake of remaining
  himself (a5s8 rows 57.4-57.7).
- "A fourth beat could fall only on" one small word in Solveig's line
  was an "only" that did not hold; two small words could take it.
- Wording that stayed too near Auden's own sentences was moved further
  off (the poet and the word "enough"; the role).
- The numbering of beats in the two sayings, the gloss of the cry, the
  gloss of sigil, the crown, and the turban line, as said above.

Two hearings are flagged in the reading: Peer's one-word reply in Act 5
("can be heard" as the cry or as the motto's word said back), and the
stress on word 4 of a5s10 row 224.2. Two inferences are flagged with
"appear to be" (the crown; the unnamed coats).

A measure of stresses is named by its beats, by its syllables where the count matters, and by "the line beginning off the beat" or "on a beat". In verse measured by count an accent is a place the count fixes and a stress is what the voice does. "Light syllable", "weak", "the pattern", "the meter" and "scanned" give way to "unstressed", "where the measure has no beat", "the measure" and "marked".
