## 2. The view

Auden left no essay on the Comedy (research notes C, section 1). His one piece wholly on Dante is the review of 1947, which is mostly about translation and declines to offer criticism (section 7). On the Inferno itself he speaks at some length in two places: of the speech of Ulysses in The Enchafèd Flood (section 5), and of the writing over the gate in the Kierkegaard introduction of 1952. The shorter things are in section 7: Hell’s open gates (1939), the souls just inside the gate (1943), the gate’s lines and the teaching on Hell (1946), Francesca (1946). The passage of 1952 is the `view`, because it says what Hell is in the poem and so bears on more than one passage: Forewords and Afterwords p. 176, in the scan `research/auden/fa.txt`, lines 8435-8440. He is explaining the wrath of God, says it describes no state of feeling in God but what the words quoted in the `view` say, and goes on (his word is "So") that Dante wrote over Hell that Power, Wisdom and Love made it. He quotes the gate's fifth and sixth lines in English. Then comes the Landor remark. That is the `view`. It bears on the first plate directly and on the second by the Landor remark.

The passage of 1939 (section 7) says the second half of it in his own voice, thirteen years earlier and with no Landor: the gates are open, and the lost know the reason they stay. I kept the passage of 1952 as the `view`, because it is the one that cites the gate’s own lines, and set the passage of 1939 where it meets a mark: between the lost people of the gate’s third line and the address to those who enter in its ninth (the Figure tab).

What his words there do not cover: in that passage he says nothing of Francesca, of the rhyme, or of the form of the poem. On the form he speaks elsewhere, in his review of 1947 (section 7), and the Rhyme and Compare tabs use it. On Francesca he speaks in a lecture of 1946 (section 7), and the Word tab of the second passage uses it.

Page convention, checked by the sequence of numbers: in `fa.txt` and `ef.txt` the page number stands at the foot of its page (176 at line 8444; 10 at line 516). In `dh.txt` the running head stands at the top. In `pearce.txt` the number stands at the foot, before the next page's heading.

## 3. Plate "gate": Inferno 3, lines 1 to 12 (rows 0.0 to 3.2)

**Why this passage.** It is the passage Auden cites (the view), and Inferno 3 heads the list of cantos for the 1941 examination (Pearce p. 153). In twelve lines it shows four things that can be seen: Dante's chained rhyme, the threefold opening, the names of the Trinity, and one word used in two senses at two rhymes. I took twelve lines and not nine so that the rhyme the gate leaves open is seen to close, and so that the traveler's word stands on the plate. Longfellow is beside it because his version keeps the lines one for one.

**Rhyme.** Marks: letters a to e; tails read in the proof. Facts:
- The chain is Dante's invention, most likely from the stanza of the serventese, and rests on three (three lines, each rhyme three times, thirty-three cantos, three parts): Enciclopedia Dantesca, "terzina" (Baldelli), sections 1 and 2. The same passage sends the reader to Vita Nuova 29 for three as the number of the Father, the Son and the Spirit.
- The chain keeps each group "closed and at the same time open": the same entry, section 6.
- Counted on the plate: b, c and d each come three times; e comes once on the plate and again at lines 13 and 15 (read in the hosted text). The first rhyme of a canto comes only twice, and so does its last middle sound, so the caption no longer says how many times a sound comes.
- Baum, chapter 4 (end), has terza rima as the meter of the Comedy, riming aba bcb cdc. He says nothing of what it does, so the reasons stand on the Enciclopedia. The caption now gives the form its name, which is the name Baum and Auden use.
- Auden's sentence (Prose, vol. 2, p. 324; section 7): to an Italian it is unthinkable to part what Dante says from his terza rima. He says it of an Italian reader, as against an English or American one, and the caption keeps "To an Italian". This is where the tab meets him: the rhyme that joins pain, maker and love, and the rhyme the gate leaves open, are things said by the form.
- "What the gate leads to, and who made it": the three words on the second rhyme are the last words of rows 0.1, 1.0 and 1.2. Row 0.1 says the way through the gate goes into eternal pain; rows 1.0 to 1.2 name its maker and say that power, wisdom and the first love made it. The caption says no more than that of the three words. It is the claim of the gate that Auden cites (the view), and the caption leaves that link to the view and to the Word tab.

**Figure (anaphora).** Rows 0.0 to 0.2, words 0 to 3. Facts:
- The name: Silva Rhetoricae under anaphora.
- In the Comedy anaphora is "above all" part of the grave and sententious style, and this threefold opening is the first example given: Enciclopedia Dantesca, "anafora" (Tateo). I put "stile grave e sentenzioso" as "the grave style of pronouncement".
- Gate inscriptions: Bosco and Reggio on 3.1-15 (many cities of his time bore one); Chiavacci Leonardi on 3.1-9 (like the verse inscriptions on the gates of medieval cities; the caption that says where the words are comes after them, in lines 10-11).
- "Only the ninth line turns to anyone": lines 1 to 8 have no form of you; line 9 has a command in the plural and the word for you (row 2.2 w0 and w3). Chiavacci Leonardi says the same on 3.9.
- The English for the opening words is Carlyle's (hosted Temple text, Inferno 3, first sentence).
- "Each line changes only where the way leads": in rows 0.0 to 0.2 the words from 4 to the end are all that differ: into the city, into the pain, among the people.
- Auden's sentence (Prose, vol. 2, p. 428; section 7). He writes there of Hell's gates and of the lost in general, having just spoken of people one can only feel to be damned. He names the Inferno a few sentences later, for the good pagans. The caption gives the sentence as his, with its year, and does not say he wrote it of Dante's gate; the second caveat tells the reader so. It stands after the three places the way leads to, the last of which is the lost people, and before the ninth line, which speaks to those who enter. The caption draws no conclusion from that order.
- The caption no longer says that the canto opens on the nine lines and tells afterwards where they stand. The plate ground keeps the clause, because "The me is a gate" rests on the same two lines (rows 3.0 and 3.1).

**Word.** Facts:
- Power, wisdom, love as the Father, the Son, the Spirit: Singleton on 3.5-6 (with Convivio 2.5.8), who calls them established; Bosco and Reggio on 3.5; Chiavacci Leonardi on 3.5-6 and Hollander on 3.5-6, who call them the attributes of the three persons. The caption's "each by its established attribute" joins the two words.
- The gate's last word in row 2.1 is the verb "I last": Enciclopedia Dantesca, "durare", which cites the line; Lewis and Short under duro (to make hard; to hold out; to last), from durus.
- The traveler's word in row 3.2: Enciclopedia Dantesca, "duro"; Singleton and Chiavacci Leonardi on 3.12. They differ on whether it is hard to understand or hard to bear, and both cite the Latin Gospel (John 6:61 in the Vulgate, read at Perseus). The note gives both senses.
- The pair is listed among the poem's equivocal rhymes: Enciclopedia Dantesca, "rima" (Baldelli), section 2.6, which defines the device as one form whose senses stand in contrast.
- "Dark color": most take it as black letters, others as dark threats (Enciclopedia Dantesca, "oscuro"; Bosco and Reggio prefer the second).
- "Forever" as an adverb, and the third "eternal" of the nine lines (rows 0.1 w5, 2.1 w2, 2.1 w5): Singleton on 3.8; counted on the plate. Some editions read the feminine form in row 2.1; the hosted text is followed.
- Virgil's use of the same verb five lines on (line 14): hosted Italian and Temple texts.

**Compare.** Facts:
- Longfellow's journal, 7 May 1864: something must be given up, and it must be the rhyme, to keep fidelity. Samuel Longfellow's Life, vol. 3, p. 35, in the archive.org scan `worksofwadsworth14longiala` (running heads at the top of the page). His letter of 8 May 1867 to Robert Ferguson, p. 88: he tried to make it rhythmic and also as close to the letter as a translation in prose.
- His note on line 1 (the canto opens on repeated sounds that toll like a funeral bell, and he gives the two words that end the first two lines): read at the Dartmouth Dante Project.
- dolent and dole: Wiktionary (both marked archaic); Online Etymology Dictionary under doleful for Middle English dole. The Oxford dictionary's pages would not open.
- "Nearly the same word order": the exceptions are the ninth line, where the English puts what is left before the command, and the tenth and eleventh, where the words for "I beheld" move up a line. Both are in the grounds.
- Auden's two sentences (Prose, vol. 2, p. 324; section 7). He is weighing a prose translation against a verse translation that reproduces Dante's form, which in that review is Binyon's. A language with few rhymes, he says, drives even the best rhymed version to a wording and an order of words that are falsely poetic, and that troubles a reader of English. Then he sets the prose and Binyon's verse of one passage one after the other and says that something is lost either way. Longfellow's version is a third thing, verse without rhyme, and Auden says neither sentence of it. The caption therefore says what he was weighing ("an English Dante in prose against one in Dante's rhyme") and then, as a fact of the passage shown, what is absent here. It does not say that he judged Longfellow's version. What he does say of it, that it is among the best known and that readers can judge it against Binyon's, is in the `auden` list.
- The two sentences on the bell and on dolent and dole are out of the caption and remain in the notes of the first two ties.

## 4. Plate "francesca": Inferno 5, lines 100 to 108 (rows 33.0 to 35.2)

**Why this passage.** Inferno 5 is the second canto on the examination list. The nine lines hold three sentences built on one frame, a line with one root three times, and Dante's line at its plainest and at one marked departure. The ninth line is the narrator's; it is kept so the last rhyme closes and the speech is seen to stop. They are also the lines with which Auden begins when he ends his lecture on Romeo and Juliet with her story (section 7), though I chose them before I found that. I did not take the line about the book and the day (line 138): it needs the story of the reading before it, which is longer than a plate. Auden quotes it with the rest of the canto and says nothing of it.

**Sentence.** The eleven finite verbs of her eight lines and their subjects, so the "no I or we" can be checked:
- 33.0 w5: Love (through w1). 33.1 w0: Love. 33.2 w2-3: the fair body (33.1 w4-5). 33.2 w8: the manner (w6).
- 34.0 w5: Love (through w1). 34.1 w1: Love. 34.2 w2: you, the traveler. 34.2 w5: Love (Bosco and Reggio on 5.105, who say the subject is Love and not Paolo; the third caveat tells the reader of the other reading).
- 35.0 w1: Love. 35.1 w1: Caina. 35.1 w6: the one who (w2), her husband.
Love is the subject of six. The lovers are 33.1 w1, 34.1 w0, 35.0 w2 and 35.1 w5, all objects.
- What the repeating does: Peacham (1593) under Epanaphora, "The use of this figure": chiefly to repeat a word of importance, as the cause before each of its effects. The Enciclopedia Dantesca, "anafora", names this threefold Amor as an example of the grave style and says Francesca is recalling the doctrine of love.
- "Still" comes twice in the canto and both are on the plate (searched in the hosted text).
- Line 102 is read two ways: the manner of the death (Singleton) or the force of the love (Sapegno, Bosco and Reggio). Hollander says the wording allows either. Nothing on the page chooses; the caption says only that she twice adds what still holds, and that the second is the love.

**Figure (polyptoton).** Row 34.0, words 0, 3 and 4. Silva Rhetoricae under polyptoton and paregmenon. Peacham's name for it is Paregmenon; its use is to please the ear and to move the mind to consider the near affinity of the things. The sense of the line: Enciclopedia Dantesca, "perdonare", which says this is the verb's one use in the Inferno (a search of the hosted text agrees). Purgatorio 22.10-12 read in the hosted text; Bosco and Reggio on 5.103 call it Dante's correction of the idea.

**Beat.** See section 6.

**Word.** Facts:
- The noble heart and the verb for love's fire are Guinizzelli's: Enciclopedia Dantesca, "gentile" (the line is cited in Convivio 4.20.7) and "apprendere"; the song read on Italian Wikisource. Chiavacci Leonardi and Grandgent on 5.100 say the same.
- Dante's sonnet in the Vita Nuova: Enciclopedia Dantesca under its first line.
- Persona as body: Enciclopedia Dantesca, "persona", which cites the line.
- Piacer as charm, beauty: Singleton, Chiavacci Leonardi and Bosco and Reggio on 5.104. Singleton adds that it may mean Paolo's pleasure in her and that neither sense can be ruled out; that is in `open`.
- Caina: Singleton on 32.58; Grandgent on 5.107; Bosco and Reggio on 5.106-107.
- The verb of row 35.1 w6: Enciclopedia Dantesca, "spegnere".
- Auden's lecture on Romeo and Juliet (Lectures on Shakespeare, p. 51; section 7). The caption gives two things from it and keeps them apart, as he does: that Romeo and Juliet do not tell romance from love, and that her story holds a warning. He does not say what the warning is, and the caption does not join the two for him. "Ended it with her speech": the quotation with which the lecture ends begins at her three sentences and runs to the end of the canto. The lecture is a reconstruction from students' notes; `stands` and the first caveat say so.
- Auden's quotation of Purgatorio 17.94-96: The Dyer's Hand p. 130, with his gloss on p. 131 (`dh.txt` lines 6277-6296). He quotes it against The Tempest, as Dante's view. He does not apply it to Francesca; `open` says so. "The other love is the mind's" is from Purgatorio 17.91-93 in the hosted text.

## 5. Plate "ulysses": Inferno 26, lines 118 to 126 (rows 39.0 to 41.2)

**Why this passage.** Auden quotes the first of these three tercets and says what he thinks the speech is (The Enchafèd Flood, pp. 9-10; `ef.txt` lines 474-508). The next two tercets are the speaker's own account of what his speech did, and they hold the line with the word the research notes had already checked (webfacts no. 8).

**Antithesis.** Rows 39.1 and 39.2. Enciclopedia Dantesca, "antitesi" (Tateo): two words or statements of contrary sense in parallel; at times the sense of a word is lit by its contrary. Sapegno on 26.120 names the antithesis. Where the speech is made: lines 106-109 in the hosted text. Genesis 3:4-5 read in the Vulgate at Perseus. Auden's words: p. 10. "Auden quotes this sentence": he quotes it in Carlyle's prose, which the hosted Temple text confirms.

**Metaphor.** Row 41.1. Enciclopedia Dantesca, "metafora" (Tateo): flight is the poem's fitting figure for the movement of the spirit, and this line is a metaphor of the ship's swift course and of a moral going astray. Virgil's phrase: Aeneid 6.19 at Perseus; Chiavacci Leonardi on 26.125 says Dante uses it in reverse. The word for mad: Enciclopedia Dantesca, "folle" (Favati); Treccani's Vocabolario, folle (1), sense 1b, which quotes the line.

**Word.** Facts: "semenza", "virtù", "orazione", "acuto", "mancino" in the Enciclopedia Dantesca, each citing the line; Lewis and Short under virtus and follis; Treccani folle (1) and (2); Online Etymology Dictionary under fool. The word for virtue comes twice in the canto, at lines 22 and 120 (every word of the canto that holds the letters virt, counted again on 2026-10-03). The word for little comes three times in twenty-one lines, at 102, 114 and 122 (read in the hosted text; Hollander's note on 26.121-123 counts the same three).

## 6. The three scansions, and the other ways of hearing them

The rule: eleven syllables, the tenth always accented (Enciclopedia Dantesca, "endecasillabo", section 4). Vowels across a word boundary fuse ("sinalefe") or stay apart ("dialefe").

- **Row 33.0 (line 100).** Accents on 2, 4, 6, 7, 10. No fused vowels. Both the sixth and the seventh syllables carry a word accent. A reader can lean on either; some would call the seventh the main accent and the line one of the 4, 7, 10 kind with a lighter sixth. The caption needs only that the two meet. The rule that such a neighbor accent "often" brings out its word is the Enciclopedia's ("endecasillabo", section 6, with examples of an accent after the sixth). Applying it to this line is my step; I found no printed scansion of the line.
- **Row 34.0 (line 103).** Accents on 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. Two fusions (w2 into w3, w3 into w4), marked `~`.
- **Row 35.0 (line 106).** Accents on 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. The vowels of w2 and w3 stay apart, which the Enciclopedia ("dialefe") calls most regular after a falling diphthong. The other way is to split w2 in two and fuse its second vowel with w3; the count and the accents come out the same. The hosted text prints two dots where a vowel is split (rows 1.2 w2 and 3.1 w1 of the first plate) and prints none here. The accent on w4 is that of the numeral "one".
