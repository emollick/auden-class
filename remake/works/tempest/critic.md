## 4. Doubted and not changed, for the lead

Each of these needs a fact added, or touches something I was told
to leave. The words offered are mine, for you to take or not.

1. **`about`, the fourth sentence.** Ferdinand is "a young prince"
   and Ariel "a spirit he cannot see", but Caliban and Prospero come
   in by name alone, and nothing above the list says who Prospero
   is. A reader who does not know the play reaches the second
   passage without knowing who is speaking. To say who they are adds
   two facts, so I left it. I would write: "The second passage is
   spoken by Caliban, a slave, when Ariel’s unseen music frightens
   the butler and the jester he is leading to kill his master,
   Prospero, the magician who rules the island. The third is
   Prospero’s own, spoken to the audience when the play is over."
   With that `about` is 90 words, the limit; without the last seven
   words of the first sentence it is 84.
2. **`view.says`, the second and the last sentence.** Both follow
   Auden's own sentences on pp. 525 and 526 closely, in his order and
   with runs of his words (I set them beside the scan), outside
   quotation marks: the one about a son at his father's grave, and
   the one on the past, the future and Miranda. The quotation
   printed over them is 25 words, the most the house allows at a
   time, so the head of the page is closer to his text than a
   paraphrase should be. I may not
   touch `view`. I would loosen both, for instance: "The feelings of
   a mourning son are no part of it." and "Once Ferdinand accepts
   that his father belongs to the past, his future is in front of
   him: Miranda."
3. **"Tabor", twice in the Word caption of the second passage.** It
   is the stage direction's word and is not said plainly. "On a
   tabor, a small drum, and a pipe" would do it (the caption would be
   113 words), but that is a fact with no ground in the lens.
4. **"The line rising to each beat"**, the measure named on the Beat
   tab of the second passage. A phrase of the handbooks. "The line
   beginning off the beat" would answer the first passage's "the
   line beginning on a beat" and uses the key's own word. I left it
   because the approved passage has the same phrase in a remark
   (section 5), and the two should change together or not at all.
5. **"Under the sense music"**, Word caption of the second passage.
   The sense is not set off, and it cannot be: the kit lights in the
   passage any word a caption puts in italics, and here that would
   light the last line's "music", which is another speaker's. "Takes
   the word here to mean music" would read more easily, but "puts
   this line under" is the examiner's exact statement of what the
   lexicon does, and I did not loosen it.
6. **The last sentence of the Scene caption.** It is true to Auden
   (p. 130, which I read). But the tab shows who speaks verse and who
   prose, and his sentence answers another question: what the two
   men bring to Caliban. The reader has to make the join alone. The
   caption would be whole without it, ending on Prospero, Miranda and
   Ferdinand. It does not pose, pad or pronounce, so it was not mine
   to cut.
7. **Change 18** drops "A handbook of verse says" from the Rhyme
   caption of the third passage. If that reads to you as a stronger
   claim, put the four words back at the head of the sentence.
8. **Change 37** puts Auden's plain word for what Caliban is accused
   of where the page had "the attempt on Miranda".
9. **Two titles that cannot be marked.** "Henry IV, Part 2" (a note
   on the Word tab of the second passage) and "A Midsummer Night’s
   Dream" (a note on the Word tab of the third) stand in notes, which
   are plain text set all in italics. Asterisks there would print as
   asterisks, so I left both bare.

## 5. The approved plate

Nothing in the plate `song` was edited. It reads better than the
other two did: its notes and remarks seldom say the caption over
again, and its four captions bring Auden in four different ways.
Seven things in it failed a test. They are set out in the order I
would mend them.

1. **Beat, the second numbered remark, second sentence.** It says the
   line ends on three stresses in a row and that "two of them are
   the bell". The third of the three is the word for the bell itself
   (row 227.1, word 6), so a reader counts three and stops. I
   would write: "It ends on three stresses in a row. The first two
   are the sound of the bell: ding-dong is a word made from it."
2. **Beat, caption, fifth sentence** ("They part most in the two
   lines that say what is happening to the body."). The reader cannot
   tell which two. The second and third lines, on the bones and the
   eyes, say what is happening to the body more plainly than any, and
   in them voice and measure hardly part; the red marks are in the
   fourth and fifth. I would write: "They part most in the fourth and
   fifth lines, which say what is happening to the body."
3. **Word, the note on row 225.4, word 2** ("The same lexicon
   puts this line under to undergo or experience ..."). "Under to
   undergo" cannot be read at speed, and a note is all italics, so
   nothing sets the sense off. I would write: "The same lexicon puts
   this line under the sense: to undergo or experience, in a good or
   a bad sense." The note on row 225.3, word 5 has the same build in a
   milder form ("under the sense to be perishable") and would take
   the same colon. These are changes 5 and 6 of section 2 over again.
4. **Rhyme, caption, last sentence** ("... and **s** four times
   across the couplet."). This is the page's first "couplet", and it
   is not said in plain words. I would end the fourth sentence "makes
   its two lines one unit, a couplet." (96 words), as change 18 now
   does for the third passage. That last sentence is also the only
   one on the plate that names something and gives no reason for it.
   I have no words to offer there that would not be a new claim, and
   the dotted marks need the sentence.
5. **Beat, the first numbered remark** ("... and rises to its
   beats."). A phrase of the handbooks, not said plainly, and the key
   beside it has the plain word. I would write: "The first line has
   eight syllables and begins off the beat." With it would go item 4
   of section 4.
6. **Beat, caption, last sentence** ("In **Into something rich and
   strange** voice and measure agree again."). "In Into" reads as a
   slip of the hand. I would write: "In the next line, **Into
   something rich and strange**, voice and measure agree again."
   With this and item 2 the caption is 120 words, the limit.
7. **"The Oxford dictionary" and "*A New English Dictionary*".** The
   Word caption and its note say the first; `stands` on the Beat and
   Word tabs and the list of sources say the second. Nothing on the
   page says they are one book. The mend is outside the plate, in
   `sources`: after the title, "(the first edition of the Oxford
   English Dictionary)". It adds words to a citation, so it is yours.

Weighed and passed:

- Figure, caption, the last word (", twice."). It says again what
  "These two lines" says, and is there for the fall of the sentence.
  One word, and the sentence before it earns the close.
- Figure, caption, "Neither line says *like*." Short, but it is the
  observation the last sentence rests on.
- Word, caption, last sentence ("The words are those of a dirge, and
  Auden's point is ..."). It tells the reader what Auden's point is
  and does not conclude for them.
- Word, the notes on row 225.0, word 1 and row 225.4, word 4. Each
  says in part what the caption says (the arm-span; the dictionary's
  first example), and each adds a fact the caption does not have.

about the words as they are set.
