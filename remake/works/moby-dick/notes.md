# Moby-Dick: the poet's notes

Piece `moby-dick`, title "Ishmael and Ahab". Three plates, eleven lenses.
The text is the hosted `moby-dick` (Project Gutenberg no. 2701), parts
`c001`, `c037` and `c128`.

`node build/tools/check-reading.mjs moby-dick`, run after the last change
on 2026-10-03, ends with "no problems", "no warnings" and "the printed
notes are fit to print". No warning was left to answer.

In these notes the novel is pointed at by block and word number, and no
more than a phrase of six words is typed. Nothing of Auden's is quoted
here. The reading has the two quotations the brief allows: `view.quote`
(22 words, The Enchafèd Flood, p. 137) and the quote in the fourth `auden`
entry (20 words, p. 140). Both were compared with the scan word for word.

Short names used below: EF is The Enchafèd Flood, read in the scan
`research/auden/ef.txt`; CTH is "The Christian Tragic Hero" (1945); C is
`remake/research/C_auden_on_works.md`.

## 1. How the work went

The rules changed six times while this piece was being written, and the
reading was gone through again each time.

1. About 02:20 UTC rule three ("nothing is made up") came in, with `view`
   and `grounds`. The captions drafted before it were thrown away and
   written again, so that each sentence stands on the plate, the text, a
   source opened in this session, or a page of Auden.
2. At 02:51 to 02:52 BRIEF.md, POET.md and EXAMINER.md were revised, with
   the list of what the model got wrong. The reading was then audited one
   sentence at a time against that list. What the audit changed:
   - The railroad figure. The caption had turned the grammar of the
     sentence round. It now says what the words say: the path is laid with
     rails, and the soul is grooved to run on them.
   - General statements. Each "only", "alone", "first", "all" and each
     number was counted again in the proof or the text. Mended: the
     narrator is silent in four chapters, 37 to 40, not in "three
     speeches"; 25 of the crew speak under their own headings in Chapter
     40; Ahab's question is given in his own words at six meetings, in the
     same five words at the last four; two ships ask something of him, not
     one.
   - Auden made to say more than he said. Three places were narrowed. EF
     p. 119 says that Ishmael and the reader have only to record, and the
     caption now says that and no more. The sentence on EF p. 140 about a
     god that is the hero's will is conditional, which the ground and a
     caveat now say. Pearce reports two examples, Ahab with the whale or K.
     with the Castle, and the reading gives both.
   - Glosses. Every gloss was taken again from the entry itself (see the
     lists under each plate).
   - The term. The crossing on the third plate was called chiasmus. The

   - Pages. Where the page number stands in each scan was proved (section

   - Three inexact phrases: "his own verb" is now "the main verb"; "what he

## 2. Auden's view, and where it was read

**CTH (The New York Times Book Review, 16 December 1945, pp. 1 and 21).**
Read only in C, section 21 and appendix H4, which give its sentences from
search snippets of the reprint in Corrigan, *Tragedy: Vision and Form*
(1965), pp. 143-47. No page of the essay was seen by me. The reading cites
the essay with no page and quotes nothing from it; the first caveat and the
first `open` entry say so. What the reading takes from it:

- Greek tragedy is of necessity, Christian tragedy of possibility (`view`).
- Ahab at the start is what a Greek hero is at the end, greatly
  unfortunate; the misfortune tempts him and gives him a choice; he chooses
  wrongly and goes on doing so (`view`).
- His end becomes necessary only after the last offer is refused. C says in
  two places that the offer is the Rachel's appeal (`view`; third plate,
  first lens).
- Ahab does not repent and goes to a death he chose, as the tragic heroes
  of Shakespeare do; Macbeth and Ahab are named together (second plate,
  first lens; second `auden` entry).
- Ishmael is no character: he has consciousness and no will, knows and does
  not act, and ends as an orphan who has still to choose (first plate,
  first lens; second `auden` entry).
- The book is epic, tragedy, quest and religious allegory at once, and an
  American novel of its century (second `auden` entry).

## 3. Plate "whenever" (Chapter 1, block 0, words 56 to 149)

**Why this passage.** Auden prints part of this very sentence (EF p. 35) as
a report of the solitary's condition, and his Ishmael is the man with
consciousness and no will (CTH), pushed toward the sea by spleen and
powerlessness (EF p. 112). The sentence can be seen doing what he says:
four clauses headed by one word, each longer than the last; the main clause
kept to the end; verbs that make Ishmael the one things happen to; and
three adverbs whose roots are will, weighing and a road followed.

**Why the first three words of the book are not shown.** They are words 0
to 2 of the same block. The page and the checker keep a plate's rows by row
id, so one row can be shown only once on a plate. I tried listing the row
twice: the checker printed both ranges and reported no problem, but the
page's table of rows holds one entry for an id. The reading was put back as
it was. `next` sends the reader to `moby-dick/c001:0`, where those words
are.

**Counts.** The tools count tokens. Token 127 is two words joined by a
dash. The first sentence is tokens 56 to 141: 86 tokens, 87 words. Its
clauses: tokens 56-64 (9 words), 65-74 (10), 75-93 (19), 94-127 (34, with
the first half of token 127), and the main clause, 15 words, from the
second half of token 127 to token 141. 9 + 10 + 19 + 34 + 15 = 87. The
sentence after it, tokens 142-149, has 8 words. The mark for *then* sits on
token 127 because its two words cannot be parted.

**Sentence lens.**

- "Kept to the end, it holds the sense open": Wikipedia, *Periodic
  sentence* (the sense of such a sentence is complete only at its close;
  the article quotes William Harmon's *A Handbook to Literature* on keeping
  an idea waiting), and Merriam-Webster, *periodic sentence*. It is also
  one of the craft facts POET.md names. The term itself is not used on the
  page: Merriam-Webster's definition allows nothing to trail after the
  principal clause, and here five words do (tokens 137-141).
- *Find oneself*: Wiktionary, *find oneself*, sense 2 (to begin a thing
  without meaning to); Merriam-Webster, *find*, transitive sense 3b.
- *Account*: Merriam-Webster, verb, transitive sense 1 (to think of as, to
  consider).
- Who acts in each clause is on the plate: tokens 57-59 and 76-78, 97-105,
  107-115.
- Auden: EF p. 112; CTH.

**Figure lens (anaphora).**

- The term: Silva Rhetoricae, *anaphora*; Merriam-Webster, *anaphora*,
  sense 1; Encyclopaedia Britannica, *anaphora*. All three: the same word
  or words at the beginning of successive clauses. The fourth *whenever*
  has two words before it, and the caption says so.
- That the repetition makes the clauses one list is the craft knowledge
  POET.md names. No handbook opened says it in those words (Britannica says
  the device is used for emphasis), so the ground says where it comes from.
- *Whenever*: Merriam-Webster, conjunction (at any or every time that).
- The sentence before, with *a way I have*: block 0, tokens 41-55.
- Auden: EF pp. 34-36, as in section 2.

**Word lens.** Online Etymology Dictionary: *hypo* (noun, 1711, short for
hypochondria, depression of the spirits), *hypochondria*, *involuntary* and
*voluntary* (Latin *voluntas*, will), *deliberate* and *deliberately*
(Latin *deliberare*, to weigh well; *libra*, a pair of scales; the adverb
glossed as with a set purpose), *method* and *methodical* (Greek
*methodos*, at first a following after; *hodos*, a way). Merriam-Webster:
*hypo*, noun (1) (first known use 1709); *involuntary*; *methodical*;
*upper hand* (mastery); *account*; *ball*, noun (1), sense 1c. Wiktionary,
*hypo* (obsolete: melancholy, a fit of hypochondria), which cites this very
sentence. Which adverb goes with which act can be read off the plate:
token 79 with tokens 80-93, tokens 117 and 123 with the two acts that the
principle of tokens 109-112 prevents. The last gloss leans on the next
sentence, block 0, tokens 150-165 (Cato). Auden on Dürer: EF p. 36.

## 4. Plate "rails" (Chapter 37: block 1, and block 4, words 225 to 269)

**Why this passage.** It is the first paragraph of Ahab's speech alone and
its last 45 words. Auden quotes this speech twice on EF p. 137: the vow
(block 4, tokens 106-132) and the iron-rails sentence, which he prints
under the remark used as `view.quote`. The remark is about the defiant man
who says that fate acts, and the passage shows how the sentence says it:
two verbs with no doer, after verbs that were Ahab's own. The speech also
shows a thing that can be heard and counted, prose that keeps a rising
beat, in a chapter set as a play. The lead in pieces.json said that Ahab's
speeches scan as blank verse. The text bears that out for the foot, and not
for the five-beat line (below). Chapter 132 was read and not used: its
speech is too long for a plate and its rhythm is looser.

**Whose craft.** Melville's. In the hosted text block 0 is a stage
direction and blocks 1 to 4 are speech with no narrator. Chapter 16, block
42, is the narrator on the Nantucket whaleman of great natural force who
learns *a bold and nervous lofty language* and is *formed for noble
tragedies*; he adds there that he is not yet speaking of such a man, and
the ground says so. The caption quotes the last two words of the first
phrase and all of the second. Chapter 33, blocks 5 and 6, where the narrator speaks
as a tragic dramatist would and turns to Ahab, was read and left out for
room.

**The measure.** One rising foot repeated: an off-beat, then a beat. The
prose is not laid out in five-beat lines, because it does not fall into
them. The phrases of block 1 run 4, 5, 6, 1 and 2 feet between stops, 18
feet and 36 counted syllables. The caption claims only the foot of blank
verse, and the first numbered remark says that only the second phrase has
the five feet of a line of it. The shown part of block 4 has 63 counted
syllables: 2, then 21, then 26, then 14. From *is laid* (tokens 233-234) to
*I rush* (tokens 258-259) there are 7 feet and then 13, twenty in all.

Where the marks part from the rule inside the two stretches the caption
counts, read off the proof one foot at a time:

- Block 1, eighteen feet: a light stress off the beat at token 7; the foot
  at tokens 23-24, marked turned round. The middle syllable of token 15 is
  marked as not counted, so the foot it stands in keeps the rule.
- Block 4, the twenty feet from token 233 to token 259: the foot at token
  245, turned round; the beat on the last syllable of token 257, left
  unstressed. Token 248 has a light stress, and it is on its beat.

The caption names these four and no others. Before token 233 and after
token 259 the caption claims nothing; two numbered remarks say what happens
there.

Blank verse, iamb, pentameter: Encyclopaedia Britannica, *blank verse*;
Merriam-Webster, *blank verse*, *iamb* and *pentameter*.

**Other ways of hearing it.** Each of these can fairly be heard otherwise.
The caption says "can be heard" of the one foot it gives that way, and the
second caveat names the rest for the reader. The caption lists the
departures as the marks have them. Two places inside the stretches it
counts would add to that list if heard the other way: token 15 said as
three syllables, and token 248 said with no stress. The caveat names both.

- *pale* (1.0, token 7): marked as a light stress off the beat. A full
  stress is as good, which sets three stresses together across the stop; so
  is none.
- *envious* (1.0, token 15): marked as two syllables, the middle one run
  into the last, as often in verse. Said as three, the foot has one more
  light syllable and the count of feet is the same.
- *let them* (1.0, tokens 23-24): the stress can fall on either word. With
  it on the first, the foot is turned round, and that is how it is marked.
  The caption says it "can be heard turned round".
- *Swerve me?* (4.0, tokens 225-226): marked with both stressed. Either can
  be the stronger. The third remark says two stresses and a stop, and no
  more.
- *my fixed purpose* (4.0, tokens 230-232): marked with *fixed* and the
  first syllable of *purpose* both stressed and *my* light, which leaves
  the rule. A reader who stresses *my* keeps the rule and bends the sense.
  The rule is given one extra off-beat at the second syllable of *purpose*,
  because the beat plainly starts again at *is laid*; strict alternation
  carried through would set every stress of the next seven feet off the
  beat. The third remark says that the two stresses fall together and that
  the voice leaves the rule here.
- *through* (4.0, token 248): a light stress on a beat. Said with none, the
  beat is passed over.
- *unerringly* (4.0, token 257): the rule puts a beat on the last syllable.
  It is marked unstressed, and the caption says the beat is left
  unstressed; a light stress there is as good, and then the voice keeps
  the rule.
- The last sentence (4.0, tokens 260-269): it begins on a stress, against
  the rule, twice over, and its seventh word is a light syllable where the
  rule has a beat. A reader can stress that word and keep the rule to the
  end. The fourth remark says the sentence starts against the rule and
  ends in step.
- *whereon* (4.0, token 238) is marked with the stress on its second
  syllable, and *where'er* (1.0, token 11) as two syllables.
- Pauses are marked only at full stops, question marks, exclamation marks
  and semicolons.

"Stresses that fall together slow the voice": Paull F. Baum, *The
Principles of English Versification* (1922), chapter 5, on lines that are
metrically overweighted, which he says retard the tempo (lines 6971-6984 of
`.cache/handbooks/baum-versification-pg21342.txt`); chapter 4 says anapests
are often slow because weighty monosyllables stand in light places (lines
2796-2801). Chapter 2 has a passage on metrical prose, with Dickens and
Blackmore as examples (lines 830-838 and 1063-1076). It does not name
Melville and was not used.

*Where'er*: Wiktionary (a poetic contraction of *wherever*).
