## 6. The second passage (chapter 19): what was found and what was changed

Proof: every mark on its word; no word here is joined by a dash.

Who (sentence).
- "each with a capital letter": all three nouns begin a sentence, so the
  capital shows nothing. Now "each at the head of its sentence".
- "Society puts on" changed the grammar (the text has could). Now "can".
- The last sentence drew a line from this paragraph to the theory in
  chapter 33 ("the same arrangement"). The book does not draw it; cut, with
  its ground. In its place the paragraph after (block 10), which says such
  impressions are felt and not reasoned.
- Labels: "a power" was Auden's word laid on Adams's nouns; now "an
  abstract noun".

Pair (antithesis).
- `plain` followed the handbook less closely than it could ("often, not
  always, in parallel structure"); mended.
- "the Prayer Book" spelled out. The American book was opened too (an
  edition of 1847): same lesson, 1 Corinthians 15:20-58.
- "what such comforts became for him" and, in the ground, "remedies for
  grief": the text has anodynes, which the dictionary gives as what eases
  pain, and it names stoicism and religion in the next sentence. Caption
  and ground now say that.
- "leaves the mortal man the bare noun": now the two words of the text.
- Labels "the many", "the one", "can choose", "can only feel" were glosses
  of the poet's; they now name the parts of the frame. "no match below"
  supposed a layout the page does not have (the artist's note); now "only
  in the first sentence".

Nature (personification).
- "calls what he saw an insanity of force" and "concludes": brought closer
  to block 10. Last sentence ("thirty years before the dynamo, a force is
  given a woman's face") was the poet's summing up; cut.
- Auden's two sentences given with their hedges (tend to; a power they
  suppose in charge), p. 62 and p. 63.
- The definition is printed beside the caption from `plain`, so it was
  taken out of the caption.

Word.
- features: the gloss decided for the face. The dictionary has both the
  lines of a face (fourteenth century) and any distinctive part (1690s),
  and the sentence can be read either way. Gloss and note now give both;
  the caption no longer says Death takes the parts of a face.
- "so both senses are at work" was an inference; cut. The caption now
  points at what the sentence itself joins (horror to charm; smothered,
  with caresses).
- winning: the dictionary's second sense is successful in contending;
  "victorious" replaced. "in 1904" is not in the paragraphs read (the
  chapter's title has 1903-1904); now "on the afternoon he reads of an
  assassination" (chapter 32, blocks 17 and 18).
- charm: note brought into line with the entry (an incantation, about

## 12. Still doubtful

- Everything of Auden's from 1941 and 1946 was seen only in search
  snippets (caveat 1).
- Whose hand wrote Omit, and whether the class read the book (caveat 2).
- "Features" may mean no more than aspects; the gloss gives both senses.
- "runs, or ran": the reading says only that both tenses stand. Why Adams
  amended a verb that was still true in 1905 is not known.
- The year the Hancock house came down is given by the guide as 1863 on
  its title page; the text of the guide says only that the land was sold
  in February 1863 and the house pulled down some while after.
- Word histories come from the Online Etymology Dictionary and
  Merriam-Webster, not from the Oxford English Dictionary.
- The hosted text may differ in small things from the Modern Library
  edition that Auden set; this was not compared.
