## 6. Sentences doubted and not changed

For the lead to decide. I may not weaken or strengthen a claim, so
none of these was touched.

1. **born / Book**, sentence 3: "The preface gives the reason." The
   preface says what the self has become since Rousseau and what the
   figure is for. It does not say in so many words that this is why the
   book says *he*. That step is the caption's, and it is the usual
   reading, but it is stated as the preface's own. The sentence is the
   hinge of the caption (rule two), so it was not cut. A lighter verb
   is the lead's to choose.
2. **sister / Word**, last sentence: Auden on Adams taking the Virgin
   and Venus for one person, set straight after the fact that one
   adjective is used of Nature here and of the Virgin in chapter 32.
   The sentence makes no claim of its own, but by standing there it
   lets the reader think the adjective shows what Auden said. Nature
   in 1870 is not Venus, and Auden's remark is about another chapter.
   The caption would end soundly one sentence sooner.
3. **dynamo / Level**, sentence 3: "So the machine is likened to the
   Cross and the Virgin's force to X-rays." "So" makes Adams's reason,
   given between the two passages, the cause of both likenesses. The
   first likeness comes five paragraphs before he gives the reason.
   The caption's order (the reason, then "So") is the site's reading
   of him, on his own words. "So" could be dropped at no cost.
4. **dynamo / Force**, the sentence that begins "Force is Auden's word
   too". The hook is the caption's: Auden's sentence has the word
   once, in the phrase the caption then gives. True as far as it goes.
5. **The two short closers**: "Here Adams reports the prayer himself."
   (Likeness) and "The book tells neither." (Book). Each is a short
   sentence at the end, which is fault 2 by its shape. Each states
   something the reader can check, in the lines or in the drawing, and
   neither draws a moral, so both stay. The first is what the tab pays.
6. **The view at the head**, sentence 3: "Praying to the first is the
   religion of an illusion." Hard at first reading: the reader has not
   yet been told of the two illusions, which the first entry under
   "More from Auden" lays out far below. `view` is not mine to touch.
7. **Under "The passage"** (`about`), sentence 1: the hundred copies
   "printed privately" follow "in 1905" and read as printed that year.
   The examiner's open note has the book's own three dates. A fact,
   not mine.
8. **Remark 5**, sentence 1: "Here the dynamo is force." A little
   gnomic. It says what the mark on the left stands on (the last
   words of the passage), and the second sentence carries the remark.
9. **Entry 1 under "More from Auden"** opens "The essay names two
   illusions." Which essay is said at the head and in the source line
   under the entry. Left.

Every tab pays something; none only names things. Likeness: the one
