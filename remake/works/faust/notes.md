## The view, and why these three passages

Auden's view of the work is in two places that I read in the scans.

- The Dyer's Hand, "Balaam and His Ass", p. 115 (dh.txt lines 5475 to
  5497): full of poetry and wise sayings, not exciting as drama, like a
  variety show, scenes of interest one by one with no real continuity.
  p. 118 (lines 5637 to 5650): the Devil of an age that idolizes
  possibility appears as Mephisto, an actor, and an actor has no name of
  his own.
- Forewords and Afterwords, "Italian Journey", p. 131 (fa.txt lines 6330
  to 6345; the essay begins on that page, numbers at the foot): Goethe
  resists translation; a reader of a translated Faust will think him too
  intellectual and short of passion, because no translation shows his
  command of every style of poetry. The view's quotation (20 words) is
  from this sentence, checked letter by letter.

So the piece shows the German with the English under it, and each plate
is a different style and measure, which is what the variety show and the
"every style" remarks are about. Auden names no passages. The choice of
the three, and the matching of the actor remark to the names and
costumes of Mephistopheles, are mine. The first caveat says so.

The course's reading list has "Omit" written by hand against Faust
(research notes A and C section 14). I built the piece anyway: Auden
wrote about the work in February 1941 and named the Devil by a line of
it in the poem he published that year.

**Plate 1, "heaven"** (German lines 267 to 270 and 275 to 282; the cut
is `o-faust/himmel`). The last four lines of the archangels' hymn and
eight lines of Mephistopheles' first speech. Chosen because the change
of speaker is a change of measure that can be seen in one glance (four
beats, then five, then six), the rhyme changes from alternate to pairs
at the point where he reaches his subject, and his last line here takes
back the hymn's closing words with one adjective changed. The plate
leaves out his first four lines (271 to 274, five beats, rhymed by
turns); the cut's label gives the line numbers and a numbered remark
says he is four lines into his speech.

**Plate 2, "name"** (lines 1334 to 1344; `o-faust/studierzimmer`). The
lead in pieces.json asked for Mephistopheles naming himself. It is also
the place Auden quotes twice: the riddle couplet in The Dyer's Hand,
p. 117, and the line about the spirit who denies in New Year Letter.
What can be seen: three six-beat lines with a break in the middle (one
of them shared by the two speakers), the antithesis inside the riddle's
line, and the drop to four beats when the answer comes.

**Plate 3, "wheel"** (lines 3394 to 3405; `o-faust/gretchen`). Stanzas
six, seven and eight of Gretchen's scene: the list of what is his, and
the return of the opening stanza. Chosen for the shortest line in the
play, the repeated possessive, and the refrain coming back after the
kiss. The lead said "two-beat lines with a returning stanza"; the text
bears it out (count below).

## Whose verse

The marks are on Goethe's German. Taylor's English is the pair, and a
compare lens on plates 1 and 3 says what he kept and what he loosened.
Taylor's title page says "in the original metres"; his preface says he
very nearly kept every foot, line and rhyme (Project Gutenberg
no. 14591, title page and preface, read).

## Scansions: the other ways of hearing them

All three plates are scanned by German word stress against an iambic
(rising) pattern. A light stress is marked where a small word carries a
beat. Places where another hearing is fair:

- heaven 0.1, word 2: marked light on the beat. It can be said with no
  stress at all.
- heaven 1.1, word 1: light on the beat; a reader may stress word 2
  instead, against the meter.
- heaven 1.3: marked with the stress on word 0 and the first beat
  (word 1) passed over. The regular reading stresses word 1. The
  numbered remark gives both.
- heaven 1.7: words 1, 4 to 5: light stresses; the third beat falls on
  the last syllable of word 3, which is weak. The pause after word 3
  (six syllables into the line) is the alexandrine's break.
- name 0.0: word 1 light on the beat; a speaker may stress word 2. The
  break after word 3 is where the comma stands.
- name 0.1: word 5 marked light; a speaker may stress word 2 and pass
  over word 3.
- name 2.0: word 1 light; a speaker may stress word 0.
- name 3.4: words 1 and 4 light; a speaker may stress word 5 (the
  pronoun) against the meter.
- name 3.6: only two full stresses in a four-beat line; the second and
  third beats fall on weak syllables of words 1 and 2, marked light.
- wheel 0.2, 0.3 and 2.0: marked with two unstressed syllables before
  the first beat. Each can also be heard with a stress on its first
  syllable, which gives three beats (0.2 and 0.3) or a falling start
  (2.0). The second caveat says so. The caption says "can be said with
  two beats", not "has".
- wheel 1.0: the possessive (word 1) carries the first beat, marked
  light.
- wheel 1.1, 1.2, 2.3: the last syllable of a compound carries the
  second beat with secondary stress, marked light.

Divisions given with `div` where the checker's syllable counter divided
a German word differently (final -e, and four compounds).

## Facts, and where each was read (all on 2026-10-03)

- Calvin Thomas, Goethes Faust, vol. I (Boston: Heath, 1892), Internet
  Archive scan: introduction p. xxxviii (Hans Sachs's Knittelvers the
  prevailing meter of the earliest scenes); notes on the Dedication
  (the eight-line stanza), on the Prelude (rimed iambic lines of four
  or five feet with occasional alexandrines), on the name Mephistopheles
  (first in the Faust-book of 1587, origin unsettled), on 275 (fine
  phrases, as the archangels have been making), 282 (the line parodies
  250 and 270; wunderlich 'singular'), 339 (Schalk 'wag'), 1334 (the
  three names and their places in the Bible), 1336 (the riddle is the
  devil's own opinion, no admission of a higher power), 4023 (Voland),
  and on Gretchen's room (a lyric monologue rather than a song). Thomas
  calls the archangels' lines "chants"; "hymn" is my word for a song of
  praise to the Lord, which is what the lines are.
- Schiller to Goethe, 15 October 1799 (Briefwechsel, at Projekt
  Gutenberg-DE): the alexandrine parts into two equal halves, and with
  the couplet it puts everything in the French plays under the rule of
  opposition. He is writing about Goethe's version of Voltaire's
  Mahomet. The caption sets this beside Goethe's six-beat lines and
  does not say Goethe meant it so.
- Meyers Großes Konversations-Lexikon, 6th ed., at Wörterbuchnetz:
  Alexandriner (twelve or thirteen syllables, break in the middle,
  French, ruled German verse from Opitz to Klopstock and Lessing),
  Knüttelverse (four beats, rhymed in pairs, the form of the sixteenth
  century, revived by Goethe after Hans Sachs), Stanze (ottava rima,
  the epic measure of the Italians).
- Baum, Principles of English Versification, chapter 4: the quick
  return of the rhyme makes the couplet a unit holding an independent
  idea; the six-stress line is the standard line of classical French
  verse and tends to split three and three.
- German Wikipedia: Madrigalvers (rhymed lines with no fixed number of
  beats; liked in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries after the
  French mixed verse of La Fontaine's fables; German fables of Gellert,
  Hagedorn, Lessing; four to six beats in Faust I); Faust. Eine
  Tragödie (all verse except one scene); Gretchen am Spinnrade (two
  beats, ten stanzas, refrain; Schubert 1814). English Wikipedia,
  Gretchen am Spinnrade (19 October 1814). The article on Madrigalvers
  also names Molière's comedies; I left that out because most of them
  are not in mixed verse.
- Dictionaries: Digitales Wörterbuch der deutschen Sprache with

- Auden: The Dyer's Hand pp. 115 to 118 and Forewords and Afterwords
