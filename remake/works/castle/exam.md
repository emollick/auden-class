## 3. The terms

- Anaphora (yard, Figure). Opened: Silva Rhetoricae, the page for
  anaphora (rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/A/anaphora.htm), 2026-10-03. Its
  definition speaks of successive clauses, sentences or lines; its
  example is John of Gaunt's speech in Richard II, where the same
  demonstrative stands before one noun phrase after another, three in a
  row in one line. The three last nouns of the passage are that pattern.
  The name and the plain gloss stand. That a word repeated at the head
  of each member makes a list is given in POET.md as common knowledge of
  the craft.

## 4. The words, and Kafka's German

Opened by me on 2026-10-03, with curl:

    The street is one that holds fast. All as the poet said.
  - Chapter 2, the letter: the salutation has no name or title; the

  - Chapter 5 (not read by the poet in German): the Superintendent's

  - Chapter 8, last sentence: when-clauses first, then one verb of

    clause about touching hangs on the second without a new one, so the
    Muirs' third "as if" is added; nothing twice with two comparatives;
    three nouns each with a demonstrative; the remark between dashes.
- DWDS (dwds.de/wb/...): weiter- (sense 1: the continuation of a
  movement along a way), weitergehen (to go on one's way, not to stand
  still), schwer (2a of great weight; 2e with great effort, not easy;
  2e alpha, with bodily effort), festhalten (1: to hold back, not let
  go), Unverletzlichkeit (unverletzlich + -keit), unverletzlich (un- +
  verletzlich; defined by one word, unantastbar), verletzlich (verletzen
  + -lich), verletzen (1: to wound). All as the reading says.
- Grimm, Deutsches Wörterbuch, through dwds.de/wb/dwb/ (the poet could
  not open Grimm at Trier; this address works): verletzen, sense 1, of
  outward harm, with an old gloss "to make wounded"; unverletzlich, in

  the sources. There is no entry for the noun.
- Online Etymology Dictionary, inviolable and inviolability: mid-15c.,

  1570s; from Latin words for not and for what may be injured, from
  violare; the noun 1660s. The caption's gloss (what cannot be injured)

  dares touch him). It stands.
- Edwin Muir's Introductory Note and Max Brod's Additional Note, in the

## 5. Auden

Read by me in the scans on 2026-10-03: The Dyer's Hand, "The I Without a
Self", scan lines 7655-7880 and 8010-8062 (running heads 160, 161, 162,
163, 167); Pearce at the places for pp. 134-36 and 154-56 (page numbers
at the foot of each page). The 1941 review and the 1946 essay are not in
the scans: read only in the extracts of C_auden_on_works.md (section 23;
appendix H12 and H17b), which the researchers took from snippets.

- The view (pp. 162-63). He says of a typical story that it turns the
  formula of the heroic Quest upside down; the older hero is told the way
  by others; in a typical Kafka story no one the hero meets knows it; for
  K the question is what he ought to do; in The Castle the letter wants
  to become a word and is not allowed. The view says no more than that.
  It stands. The paragraph before is on the late stories (the poet says
  so under open); the next begins "in a typical Kafka story" and p. 163
  names The Trial and The Castle, so the reach is fair.
- The lead asked: does each caption claim more of Auden than the page
  bears? Sentence by sentence:
  - Clauses. He gives the aphorism of the goal and the way as putting
    his point that none of those the hero meets knows the way. The
    caption had that sentence too; in this passage K. meets no one, so
    it was cut from the caption (it is in the view). What is left says
    only that he cites the aphorism, writing of a typical Kafka story.
    The research notes record that in 1941 he gave to The Castle an
    aphorism that is not in it. Opened: Mendelson, Early Auden, Later
    Auden (2017), p. 491, in the text at archive.org. That one is on
    places of refuge and of salvation, not this one.
  - Stop. He says, with an "I think", that in no other imagined world is
    everything so heavy, and that one step uses up the strength (p.
    163). The caption says that and no more. Its last clause stood close
    to his own words; put further into other words.
  - Reading. He says of K that he cannot read absurd or contradictory
    instructions and advice as evidence of malice or guilt in others,
    and that it "may well" be proof of his own (p. 163). The why said K.
    "ends at his own uneasy conscience" and that the instructions "may
    prove K.'s own guilt": that set the text's uneasy conscience beside
    Auden's guilt and left out the text's own correction (uneasy, not
    bad; c02, block 10, words 518 to 524). Mended: the caption gives the
    correction, the why keeps to "hardly thinks the Castle undecided"
    and to his "may".
  - Words and Title. Both use the one sentence that names The Castle (p.
    163). Said as he says it. They stand.
  - Turn. His "perhaps" stands before both halves: prison as the state
    the hero was made for, and freedom as what would destroy him. The
    caption and the why said the second half flatly. Mended: "may" now
    covers both.
  - Figure has no sentence of Auden's, and claims none.
- The list of what else he said:
  - [0] Pearce pp. 134-35: true to the scan. Pearce says he wrote after
    a lecture, in a nearby room; "after each class" was more than he
    says. Now "after the lectures".
  - [1] Pearce p. 136: true to the scan, but for his proviso that music

  - [3] pp. 159-60, [4] pp. 160-61, [5] pp. 162-63: true to the scan.
  - [6] pp. 166-67: he is "inclined to believe" one should read Kafka

  - [7] and [8]: every sentence is in the extracts; not seen in full.
