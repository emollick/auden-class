# Notes on the piece "castle"

Poet's notes for `reading.json`, written on 2026-10-03. Places in the hosted
text are given as part, block and word number (the numbers `words.mjs` and
the checker print). Nothing of the novel or of Auden is copied out here
beyond single words and short phrases.

## 1. What the piece shows

Auden's view (the `view`): a typical Kafka story turns the heroic quest
upside down. The old hero knows what he must do and is told the way;
Kafka's hero meets nobody who knows it; K.'s problem is what he ought to
do; in The Castle the letter K. wishes to be a word, land-surveyor, and
is not allowed. Read in the scan of The Dyer's Hand
(`research/auden/dh.txt`, lines 7789 to 7875; running heads 162 at line
7778 and 163 at line 7831).

Three passages, seven lenses:

| Passage | Where | Lenses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The street to the Castle | c01, block 34, words 0 to 81; beside it words 130 to 178 of the same block | Clauses (sentence), Stop (compare) |
| Klamm's letter | c02, block 8, whole; beside it c02, block 10, words 0 to 92 | Reading (compare), Words (word), Title (shape) |

This is prose in translation. No lens draws a meter. Where a caption leans
on the build of a sentence or on a repeated word, I checked that Kafka's
German has it, and the caption says so, or says that the word is the
Muirs'.

## 2. Why these passages

**The street (chapter 1).** The lead in `pieces.json` asks for a sentence
that takes back what it has just given. This is that sentence: the second
of the paragraph, words 10 to 58, with one subject and five clauses that
qualify each other by turns. It is also K.'s first attempt to reach the
Castle, on his first morning, and it fails without any person stopping
him. Auden's p. 162 (none of those the hero meets knows the way; the
aphorism on the goal and the way) bears on exactly this. The end of the
same paragraph is set beside it because the verb of walking that the
passage ends on (words 80 to 81) comes back denied as the paragraph's
last words (175 to 178), and because the lines in between say what
Auden's p. 163 says of Kafka's world: it is heavy, and a step costs
strength.

**The letter (chapter 2).** Also a lead in `pieces.json`. It is the first
thing the Castle says to K. in writing, it is short enough to show whole
(102 words), and the book itself reads it twice: K. at once (c02, block
10) and the Superintendent in chapter 5 (c05, block 34). K.'s reading
shows him sorting its phrases two ways and ending at his own conscience,
which is the case Auden describes on p. 163 (instructions that seem
contradictory are no evidence against others). The letter does not use
the title K. claims, which is the point of Auden's sentence about the
letter and the word. The shape lens sits on this plate because the
letter is the place in the book where the missing title can be seen.

**The yard (chapter 8).** The last sentence of chapter 8. K. has done the
one thing he can do against the Castle, which is to stay where he was
told to leave. The sentence gives what he has gained and its worth under
one verb. Auden's p. 163 ends on the same pair of ideas (the hero as
prisoner; freedom that would destroy him). The passage is the part of
the sentence from its main clause to the end; the sentence begins at
word 192 of the block with the coachman's last movements and the lights
going out, which the `about` tells in my words.

**Considered and not made plates.**

- The first paragraph of the book (c01, block 0), a lead in
  `pieces.json`. What can be marked there in the English is partly the
  translators': the capital letter that tells Castle from castle cannot
  be Kafka's, since German capitalizes every noun, and the adjective at
  word 61 is the Muirs' choice among several. Auden's sentences bear more
  closely on the three passages chosen. The paragraph is where `next`
  sends the reader.
- Olga on the roads to the Castle (c15-4, block 4, words 53 to 142). I
  thought of setting it beside the street. It is fourteen chapters away
  and needs its own story told first. Not used.
- The Superintendent's reading of the letter (c05, block 34). Used as a
  ground for the Words lens and linked from its printed note. It could
  not be the pair of the letter as well as K.'s reading.
- The two telephone calls (c01, blocks 14 to 16; c02, blocks 4 to 6).
  Auden's remark about the telephone exchange (Pearce, pp. 155-56) is
  about them. They are dialogue spread over many paragraphs, with nothing
  one plate could mark. They are in the Title lens as turns, and Auden's
  remark is in `auden`.

## 3. Facts, plate by plate, and where each was read

### The `about`

- K. arrives late in the evening at a village deep in snow: c01, block
  0. He says who he is: c01, block 13, words 52 to 65.
- The walk is on the morning after: c01, blocks 18 to 34. When he gets
  back it is dark, and the text says it was morning when he left: c02,
  block 0, words 12 to 53. The messenger brings the letter that evening:
  c02, blocks 7 and 8.
- The third passage is on K.'s fourth day. He stays in bed a whole day
  and night, and the text counts the morning after that as his fourth
  day in the village: c03, block 3, words 1338 to 1379. Chapters 4 to 7
  follow on that day with no night between them, and chapter 8 opens
  with K. leaving the room of chapter 7 as darkness begins to fall: c08,
  block 0. An earlier draft said "a few days later"; the letter comes
  on the second day, so the yard is two days after it.
- The yard: under "The yard" below.

### The street

- The marks: read against the proof of the checker. The subject span
  begins at word 11 (word 10 is the conjunction).
- English between the two parts shown (words 82 to 129): K. is unwilling
  to leave the street; the village seems to have no end; a list of what
  he passes, joined by "and".
- Kafka's German: zeno.org, Kafka, Romane, Das Schloß, Das erste Kapitel
  (http://www.zeno.org/Literatur/M/Kafka,+Franz/Romane/Das+Schloß/Das+erste+Kapitel).
  The page names its source as Gesammelte Werke, Band 2, Frankfurt a.M.
  1950 ff., pp. 5-28, and marks the text as in the public domain. The
  paragraph has 147 words there. What I checked in it:
  - the five clauses about the street stand in the same order as in the
    English, with a two-word hedge (*wie absichtlich*) before the turn
    aside;
  - *weiter* is its 72nd word (where he goes on) and its last word
    (where he can go no further);
  - the lifting of the feet is *schwere Arbeit*;
  - the street he leaves is *dieser festhaltenden Straße*; the Muirs'
    noun at word 138 of the English is their own turn for it;
  - the lane takes him in (the Muirs: he escapes into it);
  - the Muirs' three words at 59 to 61 stand for a German word meaning
    always.
- Dictionary: Digitales Wörterbuch der deutschen Sprache
  (https://www.dwds.de/wb/ followed by the word), read on 2026-10-03.
  *weiter-*, sense 1: marks the continuation of a movement along a way,
  with verbs of motion. *weitergehen*: to continue on one's way, not to
  stand still. *schwer*, sense 2a: of great weight; sense 2e: with great
  effort, laborious. *festhalten*, sense 1: to hold back, not to let go.
- Edwin Muir on Kafka's sentences: his Introductory Note, printed with
  the translation and in the Standard Ebooks edition
  (https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/franz-kafka/the-castle/willa-muir_edwin-muir),
  last paragraph. He says the sentences rarely need a semicolon, that
  Kafka is partial to the comma, and that he loves a winding sentence
  that moves forward, comes back on itself and moves forward again. The
  caption gives the last point in my words. The second sentence of the
  passage has in fact only commas in the English; I did not put that in
  the caption because I did not compare the German punctuation mark by
  mark.
- Auden: dh.txt lines 7805 to 7816 (p. 162) for the way and the
  aphorism; lines 7867 to 7875 (p. 163) for the heavy world and the
  single step. He gives the aphorism as one of Kafka's aphorisms and
  does not say which book it is from.

### The yard

- The scene (c08): K. waits by the sledge in the dark (block 8, 0 to
  30); he gets into the sledge and drinks the brandy (block 8, 230 to
  652); the lights go on and a gentleman comes out, who is not Klamm
  (block 9); he tells K. to come with him and K. refuses (block 11, 0 to
  95); K. will miss the man whether he goes or stays (141 to 152); the
  horses are to be unyoked (252 to 256); the sledge and the gentleman
  leave (block 12); K. holds the field without joy (block 13, 23 to 42);
  the coachman shuts himself in and the lights go out (block 13, 192 to
  252). Chapter 9 begins with K. going back into the house.
- The sentence shown (block 13, 277 to 385): main clause 277 to 280; "as
  if" at 281-282, 295-296, 342-343 and 365-366; the turn at 359; the
  remark between dashes 359 to 365; the three last nouns at 381, 383 and
  385. The caption's sentence that the freedom and its hopelessness are
  both what seemed to K. stands on these marks: all four clauses hang
  on the one verb at 278, and the remark between dashes is the only
  thing in the passage that does not.
- Kafka's German: zeno.org, Das achte Kapitel (source line: the same
  volume, pp. 145-158). The last paragraph has 328 words there. Checked:
  one verb of seeming before the clauses; *als* with a subjunctive verb
  three times (the Muirs' third "as if", at 342-343, is added); *freilich
  freier* where the Muirs have "in reality ... freer"; the word for free
  or freedom three times (the Muirs' "at liberty", 309-310, is added);
  *nichts* twice before the two comparatives; the three nouns (*Freiheit*,
  *Warten*, *Unverletzlichkeit*), each with *diese* or *dieses* before
  it; the two dashes round the remark on the second
  conviction.
- Anaphora: Silva Rhetoricae (rhetoric.byu.edu, the page for anaphora),
  read on 2026-10-03. Its definition speaks of clauses, sentences or
  lines. Its example is John of Gaunt's speech in Richard II, where
  "this" stands at the head of one noun phrase after another. That is
  the pattern of the last three nouns here, so the name fits. That a
  word repeated at the head of each member makes the members a list is
  given in POET.md as common knowledge of the craft.
- *inviolable*, *inviolability*: Online Etymology Dictionary
  (https://www.etymonline.com/word/inviolable and /inviolability), read
  on 2026-10-03. Mid-15th century; from the Latin for not and for what
  can be hurt, from *violare*; the sense of what cannot be injured is
  dated 1520s; the noun 1660s.
- *Unverletzlichkeit*: DWDS, read on 2026-10-03. The entry divides it as
  *unverletzlich* with *-keit*; *unverletzlich* as *un-* with
  *verletzlich* (and defines it by *unantastbar*); *verletzlich* as
  *verletzen* with *-lich*; *verletzen*, sense 1, is to wound. I also
  read *unantastbar* (fully secured, not to be violated) and *antasten*
  (to touch; in a figurative sense to attack). The caption uses only
  the line through *verletzen*.
- Auden: dh.txt lines 7872 to 7875 (p. 163).

## 6. Warnings of the checker

The checker ends with no problems and one warning.

- `plate yard lens 0 (sentence) caption: "as if"`. The checker takes the
  two words for a claim about what the passage stands for. Here they are
  quoted: they are the words of the passage at 281-282, 295-296, 342-343
  and 365-366, they are what the lens marks, and the caption sets them in
  bold and in italics. The sentence about them rests on the plate and on
  the German. I left it.

Warnings that came up earlier and were mended: a filler in one `auden`
paraphrase; "as if" and "mirror" in two others, reworded; "offered" in
the Reading caption, reworded; `about` one word too long.
