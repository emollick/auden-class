# Antigone: the poet's notes

Piece `antigone`. Written 2026-10-03. The reading is `reading.json` in this
folder. No words of the play are typed here: places are given as part,
block.row and word number, as `node build/tools/check-reading.mjs antigone`
prints them. Auden is paraphrased; the only two quotations of his are the
two in the reading (`view.quote`, 18 words, and `auden[0].quote`, 13 words).

## What the piece is built to show

Auden said two different things about this play, and the piece gives both.

- In March 1941, the year of the course, Antigone is his instance of the
  hero who accepts a tragic choice: she cannot both obey the law and do
  her duty to her brother, so she chooses and wills her death; the
  ambiguity is in her situation and not in her. ("James Joyce and Richard
  Wagner".)
- In the lectures of 1967 the chief figure, for Sophocles, is Creon;
  Antigone's part is to bring out his hubris, and his ruin is punishment
  for hubris toward the gods. (*Secondary Worlds*, pp. 18-19.)
- Between the two, in 1948, he describes the Greek tragic hero as a
  warning to the chorus, lists Antigone among heroes for whom every
  course is wrong, and names the moderate law-abiding citizen as the
  model. (*Forewords and Afterwords*, pp. 19-20.)

The `view` gives 1941 and 1967 and takes its gloss of hubris from 1948.
Where each passage meets these: the second passage (her answer) bears on
1941 (tabs Sides and Knowing) and on 1967 (tab Greek: what Creon's order
is called); the third (Creon and Haemon) bears on 1967 (tabs Exchange and
Play); the first (the elders' song) bears on 1948 (tabs Greek and Song).
The tabs Beat, Rhyme (first passage) and City (third) show whose verse
and whose words these are, Storr's or Sophocles', and meet Auden only in
what he says about reading Greek in translation (*Forewords and
Afterwords*, pp. 5-7).

## The three passages, and why these

They stand in the order of the play.

1. `elders`: antigone/p2, rows 73.0 to 73.5. The fourth and last stanza
   (headed Ant. 2 in the text) of the elders' song on man. The famous
   stanza is the first, and I did not take it, for these reasons. The
   fourth is where the song turns from what man can do to what he does
   with it. It names in one clause the two things the play then sets
   against each other, the laws of the land and the justice of the gods.
   Its Greek has the pair of opposites hypsipolis and apolis side by
   side, which the word tab can show under Storr's English. And its six
   lines show Storr's measure and his double rhyme whole. The first three
   stanzas are told in the Song tab, with links.
2. `answer`: antigone/p3, rows 32.0 to 36.7, then words 4 to 9 of row
   36.10, then rows 36.11 and 36.12. Thirteen rows, under the limit of
   fourteen. Antigone's one-line answer to Creon's first question, his
   second question, and the head of her speech. Rows 36.8 and 36.9 and
   words 0 to 3 of row 36.10 are left out: there she says that no fear of
   any man's will would make her answer to the gods for breaking these
   laws. The cut keeps the plate to size and still reaches the place where
   she says she knew she must die and counts an early death as gain,
   which is what Auden's sentence of 1941 turns on. The page shows dots at
   the cut, and `about` says that lines are left out. Rows 36.13 to 36.19,
   the rest of the speech, are not shown.
3. `quarrel`: antigone/p4, rows 24.0 to 42.0: ten single lines, blocks 24
   to 42 by twos. The middle of the exchange of single lines between Creon
   and Haemon (twenty-eight lines, blocks 20 to 74), from Creon's question
   about Antigone to Haemon's reply about the woman. Chosen because the
   three words taken up from speaker to speaker (the city, ruling, woman)
   all fall inside these ten lines, and because Creon's claim about whose
   the city is falls here.

Passages considered and left: the first stanza of the song (see 1); the
scene of the two sisters (nothing of Auden's bears on it); Antigone's last
song (rhymed stanzas, but the first plate already shows Storr's song
verse); Creon's lament at the end (told in the Play tab).

## The beat of the first passage: how each line was heard, and the other ways

Storr's line here has six beats with one or two light syllables between
them, and the internal rhyme falls on the third beat of each line. The
whole song (blocks 67 to 73) is in this line. The lines before it (block
64) and on the other two plates are blank verse, and block 74 is in a
shorter rhymed measure, mostly of four beats.

| row | syllables | beats fall on | stop after third beat | other way of hearing it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 73.0 | 14 | w0 (first syllable), w2 (first syllable), w3, w5, w8 (first syllable), w10 | no: the phrase w2 to w5 runs across it | none that changes anything; w6 is light |
| 73.1 | 14 | w1, w3, w6, then w8, w10 (first syllable), w12 | yes, comma | w2 (*man*) can be stressed and w3 left light: still three beats in the half, but two beats then touch and the first *now* loses the stress that the contrast with w8 asks for. I marked w2 as a lesser stress off the beat |
| 73.2 | 17 | w2 (first syllable), w4, w7, then w9 (second syllable), w11, w14 | yes, comma | none; two light syllables open each half |
| 73.3 | 16 | w0 (first syllable), w2 (first syllable), w4, then w7 (first syllable), w8 (first syllable), w10 | yes, semicolon | the second syllable of w8 (*outcast*) has a lesser stress; it does not take a beat because w10, the rhyme word, does |
| 73.4 | 14 | w0 (first syllable), w1, w4, w7, w9, w11 (second syllable) | no stop | **the doubtful one.** If w0 (*Whoso*) is slid over as two light syllables the line has five beats. Merriam-Webster gives the word with its main stress on the first syllable and a lesser one on the second. The other five lines have six beats, and in each of them the word that carries the inner rhyme is the third beat; here that word is w4, so I hear six. The second syllable of w0 could also take the lesser stress |
| 73.5 | 14 | w0, w3, w6, then w8, w10, w13 | yes, comma | w0 light and w1 stressed: the half keeps three beats |

What the caption leans on, and whether it is safe. "Six beats" leans on the
first syllable of row 73.4: the fourth caveat tells the reader so. "Four of
the six have a stop after the third" is punctuation, not ear (rows 73.1,
73.2, 73.3, 73.5). "Half against half" in rows 73.1 and 73.3 is a matter
of the words on each side of the stop. The pinned remark on row 73.1 says
that *man* can also be heard stressed.

The checker's syllable division agreed with my marks in every row; no
`div` was needed.

The handbook. Baum has two sentences near each other in chapter 4, part 1
(The Line). One is general: lines of one, two and three feet tend to become
jerky, and those of more than five to break up into smaller units (line
2355 of `.cache/handbooks/baum-versification-pg21342.txt`). The other is
about the six-stress iambic line, the alexandrine, which he says tends to
split into three and three (lines 2961 to 2966). Storr's line is not iambic
throughout, so the caption gives only the general sentence, and what is
seen on the plate (the stop after the third beat) is left to show where
this line breaks. An earlier draft gave the second sentence as if it
covered all six-beat lines; that was more than the handbook says.

## The terms

- **Antithesis** (tab Sides). Online Etymology Dictionary, antithesis:
  in rhetoric, the bringing of contrary ideas or terms into close
  opposition. The marks sort ten phrases into the king's side and the
  gods' side; the Greek of lines 450 to 457 sets the same things against
  each other.
- **Polyptoton** (tab Knowing). Merriam-Webster: repetition of a word in
  a different case, inflection or voice in the same sentence. Silva
  Rhetoricae: repeating a word in a different form. In row 32.0 the verb
  stands three times, twice in one form and once in another (w1, w3, w9);
  the figure is named for the change of form, and the plain-words gloss
  says "in a changed form". It returns in rows 36.7 (w5) and 36.10 (w5).
  In the Greek the verb stands once in line 448 and again in 457 and 460,
  so the triple in row 32.0 is Storr's, and the caption says so.
- **Stichomythia** (tab Exchange). Liddell and Scott: conversation in
  alternate lines. The Century Dictionary, as the Online Etymology
  Dictionary quotes it: usually one speaker opposes or corrects the other,
  often with partial repetition of his words. The caption paraphrases
  that sentence.
- The text's own labels Str. and Ant. (strophe, antistrophe) are kept only
  in the names of the stanzas in the Song tab's facts. The captions say
  stanza.

**The Greek, by the standard line numbers** (`numcount.py`). The TEI file
carries a number at every fifth line and at the head of each section. The
script gives each stretch between two numbers to its speaker. No stretch
is shared by two speakers unless every line in it has its own number, so
the count is exact for this text: Chorus 362, Creon 358, Antigone 215,
Guard 112, Messenger 82, Teiresias 76, Haemon 65, Ismene 60, Second
Messenger 14, Eurydice 9; total 1,353. Antigone's last line is 943, and
410 follow.

**The Greek, by printed lines** (`grcount.py`): 1,256 printed lines,
because the songs are printed in long lines; Creon 350, Chorus 294,
Antigone 194.

What this means for the caption. In Storr's English Creon has more lines
than the chorus (339 to 319). By the Greek numbers the chorus has four
more than Creon (362 to 358). So the caption does not say that Creon has
the most lines in the play. It says that he has the most of the single
speakers, which holds in all three counts, and it compares him with
Antigone: about two-thirds more lines than hers (339 to 205 in English,
358 to 215 in Greek). The third caveat gives the Greek numbers.

## The rules that changed while this was being written

POET.md, EXAMINER.md, the checker and `build/lib/printed.mjs` were changed
between 04:11 and 04:33 UTC, after the reading had first passed. The new
rules ask for a `stands` on each lens and for `caveats`, and say that
nothing printed may name files, blocks, rows, research notes or tools, or
call a part of the page a plate or a lens. I rewrote the printed notes to
those rules: `view.source` and each `auden[].source` give the book, year
and page only; `sources` has one entry for each book, article or
dictionary; each lens has `stands`, with links into the copy printed here;
there are five `caveats`. The working record (each ground's `source`, each
`checked`, `open`, the working keys in the facts, this file) still uses
block and row numbers. The examiner should read the printed notes as
closely as the captions.

Other things mended in the same pass, after reading every source again:

- The Play caption no longer says that Creon has the most lines (see The
  counts).
- The Greek tab of the second passage no longer says that Antigone keeps
  to the herald's word. She takes it up three times, and says laws once,
  in the corrected line.
- The Beat caption no longer gives Baum's sentence about the alexandrine
  as if it covered this line.
- `about` and the Song caption no longer say that no one knows who buried

- Paraphrases of Auden were moved further from his wording, so that the
