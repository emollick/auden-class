## 2. The ear (scanned by me before I looked at the marks)

Counted by the Italian rules as Ciociola gives them (a line is named from its last stressed position plus one; a vowel running into a vowel between words counts once).

- First passage, all twelve rows are sevens (last stress on the sixth). 0.3, 1.3, 4.7, 5.3 end on the stress (six heard); 1.0 and 5.0 end sliding (eight heard); 1.1, 1.2, 5.1, 5.2, 3.0, 3.1 end plainly. - Around it: Alfredo's eight lines (238-245), Violetta's eight in reply (246-253) and all twenty-four lines of the slow song (304-315, 317-328) are sevens. The recitative before the slow song (296-303) goes 7, 7, 11, 11, 11, 11, 7, 11 and ends in a rhymed couplet; the recitative before the fast song (330-337) goes 11, 7, 7, 7, 7, 7, 11, 11 and ends in a rhymed couplet.
- Second passage: rows 9.0 and 9.1 are elevens (last stress on the tenth; 9.0 is stressed on 2, 4, 6, 8, 10). Rows 10.0 to 10.7 are eights, every one stressed on the third and the seventh; 10.3 and 10.7 end on the stress. The rhymes are a a for the couplet and b c b d c e e d for the song, as marked. The line-end word of 10.0 counts three syllables at the end of the line and two inside row 9.0, as the marks have it.
- The guests' chorus (283-290): eight eights, each stressed on the third and seventh, plain and cut endings by turns; the rhyme words of 286 and 288 are the cut forms of the words for rejoicing and for pleasure.
- Third passage: the last eight lines of the libretto are sevens in the order sliding, plain, sliding, plain, sliding, cut, sliding, cut. The first needs no run of vowels across the speakers. The sixth, seventh and eighth come to seven only if the vowel that ends one speaker's piece runs into the vowel that begins the next speaker's; the seventh also needs the first two vowels of the name said as one. The fifth needs the first exclamation kept apart from the pronoun after it. The reading says the main point in a caveat and the rest in `open`.

## 5. Sources read for the facts

- First printing (Wikimedia Commons, `File:La traviata.djvu`, pages fetched as pictures at 1280 px). Title page: Piave's libretto, Verdi's music, written for La Fenice, for the Carnival and Lent season of 1852-53, Venice, Teresa Gattei. P. 9: Alfredo's eight lines as two groups of four, the first line of each hanging. P. 10: the guests' chorus with every second line set in; the four returning lines set deeper in than her eight, the dieresis printed, no comma between the two adjectives. P. 11: the second stanza the same; a question mark after the words for what must I do; the fast song as two groups of four. Pp. 34-35: the divided lines set in steps, the names shortened in the margin, the last line with two small words one over the other and given to all.
- Seen on p. 10 and not shown on the page: the print differs from the transcription in one word of line 301 (a cut infinitive) and one of line 284 (a verb with another prefix). Neither changes a count. Recorded in `open`.
- Ciociola, "versificazione", "metrica e lingua", "settenario", "rima" (Enciclopedia dell'Italiano, 2011): as the reading reports them. The article on the seven says the ode-canzonetta mixes plain, cut and sliding rhymes and became the typical form of the opera aria, and that recitative is a free run of elevens and sevens.
- Pelaez, "ottonario" (Enciclopedia Italiana, 1935): a doubled four, normally accented on third and seventh; found in the oldest Italian verse; in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries in dance songs, laudi and carnival songs; rare in the sixteenth; again in the canzonette of the seventeenth. The caption had kept the dance and carnival songs and dropped the laudi: see 8.2.
- Della Corte, "cabaletta" (Enciclopedia Italiana, 1930): an easy piece whose effect lies in the uniform return of the rhythmic design. Treccani's Vocabolario and Enciclopedia on line under the same word; this song is the example given.
- Rossi, "melodramma, lingua del" (Enciclopedia dell'Italiano, 2011): archaism, high words, distance from speech, predictability; the cross-and-delight phrase still proverbial.
- Basevi (1859), pp. 233-34 and 236, in the saved scan text: Alfredo's tune on the four lines is called characteristic and is said to come back several times in the act; in Violetta's slow song the phrase proper to Alfredo returns with the clarinet; the fast song follows, and the tenor sings the phrase from behind the scenes. Of the last scene he says only that it has some pathetic phrases, not well joined.
- Kobbé, *The Complete Opera Book* (1919; Project Gutenberg no. 40540, lines 14666-14685), found by me: the words of Alfredo's line are set to a phrase that Violetta repeats in her slow song, "in the nature of a refrain". A second witness for the return of the tune. He does not describe the music of the last lines.
- The sung words (Ditson's edition with the words under the notes; Rullman's Italian column, which I read again at lines 896-920 of the saved text): Alfredo sings the heartbeat noun; the fast song keeps its first and fifth lines and has other words in the rest; all the rhyme sounds are kept and six of the rhyme words. Rullman's Italian column keeps the verb for ending in the couplet and has the question mark of the first printing.
- The cover of the Rullman libretto, seen as a picture (the Internet Archive's leaf n4 of `latraviataloston00verduoft`) and in the saved text (lines 1-30 and 78): price 50 cents, Metropolitan Opera House, Grand Opera, Edward Johnson general manager, "the original Italian, French or German libretto with a correct English translation", Fred. Rullman, Inc., "the only correct and authorized edition". The Archive's record gives the date as "[19--?]" and reports no notice of copyright; the library's label on the binding reads 1900.
- *The Canadian Encyclopedia*, "Edward Johnson" (section "Opera Administrator 1935-1950"), and Wikipedia, "Edward Johnson (tenor)": general manager of the Metropolitan Opera from May 1935 until he retired in 1950. So the copy scanned was printed in those years. The dating is my inference from the cover; nobody had made it, and the poet and the hosted note say only that the libretto gives no date.
- Liddell and Scott at Perseus, and the Wikipedia article through its API, for antilabe.
- Counts in the Italian file by search: the "strange" exclamation at 296 (twice), 426 and 1258, each time Violetta's; the "oh joy" exclamation at 300 and 1266; the word for free at 338 only; the old must-form at 336, 338 and 1193 (the last is Alfredo's); the illness named at 1029; a year, friendship only, the flower and "tomorrow" at 237, 247 and 261-269, for the `about`.

## 7. The reason and its ground, tab by tab

First passage.
- Refrain. Every sentence stands: the plate, the Italian file, the page images, the dictionary, Basevi, DH p. 471. The reason is true of the passage: she says his words are carved in her heart, and both stanzas of her song then close on them. Unchanged, but for a second witness in the grounds and the printed note.
- Measure. Stands. The convention Auden and Kallman speak of was settled, they say, before Wagner and the Verdi of the middle years; the caption says "settled before Verdi's middle years" and claims no more. Unchanged.
- Words. Stands. The fit to pp. 469-70 is close: Auden's point there is that in a play a character's range of speech is tied to social character, and in opera it is not. Unchanged.
- English. Stands. One note reworded (8.8). The sentence that follows the one used on p. 493 is recorded in `open`.

Second passage.
- Measure. Two faults: the history of the eight-syllable line was reported selectively, and the `why` said the song takes two rhyme words of the guests when only one is in the song (the other ends the first row, in the recitative). The `why` also made the verse stress the same thing as the musical rhythm the encyclopedia speaks of. Mended (8.2, 8.3).
- Rhyme. Stands, with Auden's word restored (8.5) and the handbook citation changed to one that fits (8.4).
- Words. Stands. "A duty she lays on herself" rests on the text: she is alone and asks herself. One gloss mended (8.7).
- Scene. Stands. One printed fact reworded nearer the Italian (8.6).

Third passage.
- Words. The `why` ended with a rule of the poet's making: that by Auden what she sings is so "until the Doctor's two words". Auden sets no such term. Cut and replaced with what he says (8.10). Two notes made exact (8.13, 8.14).
- Shared. The caption ended "Here it happens inside one line", which asserts that the last lines are the finding out of a mistake in Auden's sense. Cut. The fact it leaned on is now stated (in the fifth line she sings that she returns to life) and Auden's sentence stands last, as his (8.9).
- English. The caption brought Auden in "for another reason", his belief that a listener hears syllables. That is no reason for this tab. In its place: the fact that the English comes from an opera-house libretto with the Italian and an English translation, and what he and Kallman say on p. 484 about such librettos (8.15). "Its reader cannot tell" was more than can be known; it now says the reader does not meet the same words twice.

## 8. What was changed, and why

1. **Third passage, the label over the English** said "the speeches, without its stage directions". The page prints the directions, and prints each one under the name of the next speaker, because the build hands a direction to the row after it. In this libretto each direction follows its speech. The label now says "each stage direction goes with the speech above it". The order can only be put right in the kit; reported in `open`.
2. **Second passage, Measure caption**: "an old line of dance songs and carnival songs" is now "an old line, used in dance songs, religious songs and carnival songs". The source names all three.
3. **Second passage, Measure `why`** rewritten: the song is in the guests' measure, two of their rhyme words end lines "here" (on the passage, not in the song), all eight lines are stressed alike, and a cabaletta's effect lies in a rhythm that returns unchanged.
4. **Second passage, Rhyme ground**: Baum on the short couplet (said of the English line of four beats, and not used by the caption) replaced by Baum, chapter 4, section 2: lines grouped and held together by rhyme, the couplet the simplest stanza. The list of sources follows.
5. **Second passage, Rhyme caption**: "opera rests on a human habit" is now "opera imitates human willfulness" (DH p. 470).
6. **Second passage, Scene facts**: "alone in the crowd" is now "lonely amid the tumult" (lines 304-307; tumulto 1, solingo).
7. **Second passage, Words**: the gloss on row 9.0 words 0 to 3 left out the fourth word; now "what more do I hope now".
8. **First passage, English, first note**: "Both stanzas end on the word for love" could be taken for the two stanzas of her song; now "Both versions end this line on the word for love".
9. **Third passage, Shared caption**: last sentence cut, the fact about the fifth line put in, "the last word of the opera" made "the libretto's last word". The plate ground follows.
10. **Third passage, Words `why`**: the invented clause cut; now ends on what Auden holds, that what is sung is so and only the orchestra can hint otherwise.
11. **`auden`, the entry from p. 470**: "His list runs ... Violetta is not on it" is now "His examples run ... and Violetta is not among them".
12. **`auden`, the entry on the librettist's verses**: page set to 473; `checked` says why.
13. **Third passage, Words, note on row 2.6**: she did not say no man had kindled "her"; line 300 is addressed to her soul. Now exact.
14. **Third passage, Words, note on row 2.7, and the last note of the English tab**: "the word of her second line" is the fourth row on the page; now "her own word for her pain".
15. **Third passage, English**: caption, `why`, grounds and printed note rewritten as said in section 7; DH p. 484 added to the list of sources. The sentence "It keeps the speeches in order and loses two returns" went to make room for the date (item 21); the two returns are still told in the sentence that follows it.
16. **`auden[0].source`**: "not seen in full" taken out of the printed source (it stays in the list of sources and in the first caveat).
17. **First passage, Refrain**: Kobbé added as a second witness in the grounds, the printed note, the sources and the last caveat.
18. **Antilabe**: Liddell and Scott added in the ground, the printed note and the sources; the last caveat reworded.
19. **`open`**: nine entries added, each beginning "From the examiner". The poet's thirteen are left as they were; the last of them (antilabe sourced only to Wikipedia) is answered by one of mine.
20. **Working record kept in step**: the grounds of the tabs changed now name what I opened (the lauda, the tumult and the solitary word in the dictionary; Baum's lines; the libretto's cover; DH p. 484), and the dictionary list under `sources` has the three new words.
21. **The English libretto dated**: the poet and the hosted note say the libretto gives no date. The cover of the copy scanned names Edward Johnson as general manager of the house, and he held the post from 1935 to 1950 (section 5). The third passage's English caption now says the copy was printed between 1935 and 1950, its ground and printed note say where that comes from, the first entry of `sources` says the same, and *The Canadian Encyclopedia* is entered there. It is the date of this printing only; the translation may be older and its maker is not named. The hosted text's own note and its empty year are outside this folder and were left; reported in `open`.
