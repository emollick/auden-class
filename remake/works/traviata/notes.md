## Why these passages

Auden left one sentence on this opera's music and a general account of opera. I chose three places where the libretto bears that account out and where the thing can be seen in the words.

1. **`refrain`: Alfredo's four lines and the same four in Violetta's song** (act-1 rows 0.3 to 1.3, 3.0 to 3.1, 4.7 to 5.3). She says his words are carved in her heart, and two stanzas later the words she sings about love are his, three lines of four to the letter. That is the last clause of the sentence in `view.quote` made visible: the same words stacked. The first printing itself sets the four lines apart in her song by a deeper indent (pp. 10 and 11), so the return was meant to be seen on the page. The measure shows why they fit (her song is in his seven-syllable line and reaches his rhyme word). The words show a courtesan given the old poetic vocabulary of love, which meets his remark that such feelings belong to everybody and that only opera can show it. The English shows what Auden and Kallman say an aria's translation owes (the emotion) and what this one drops (everything the four lines say love is).
2. **`cabaletta`: the couplet that ends the second recitative, and the fast song** (rows 9.0 to 10.7). The measure changes under the reader's eye from eleven to eight; the eight is the line of the guests' chorus; the couplet rhymes the verb for taking joy with the verb for ending; the question's verb comes back as the first line's verb, joined to the only use of the word for free in the libretto. This is Auden's willfulness, feelings insisted on whatever they cost, in the words themselves, and the scene's build (the `shape` tab) answers the words quoted in `auden[6].quote`.
3. **`end`: the last thirteen printed lines** (act-3 rows 1.0 to 2.7). Her last speech opens and breaks off with the two exclamations of her first speech alone; the stage direction states her revival as fact; the eight lines of verse are divided among the voices so that her joy and their cry that she dies are one line. Auden's one sentence on this opera is about this very scene (the orchestra at Germont's entrance), and his rule that what is sung is so explains why the libretto can let her sing that she lives.

What I did not use: the leads in pieces.json speak of Alfredo's voice heard from outside between the two songs. It is not in the libretto as first printed (lines 329 to 345 have no words for him); Basevi (p. 234) reports it of the score, after the fast song. The second lead (a line ending on a stress closes a stanza) is true of every stanza in these passages, and I found no source that states it as a rule, so the captions say only what each line does.

## Scansions: the count, and the other way of hearing

The count is by the Italian rules as Ciociola gives them: the line is named by its last stress plus one; a vowel ending a word runs into a vowel beginning the next (marked `~` on the syllable that is run in); two vowels inside a word may count as one or as two.

Passage 1 (lines of seven):
- 0.3: word 0 is marked stressed on its first syllable, where the word's own accent is. The measure needs only the stresses on the fourth and sixth positions; a reader may leave the first light.
- 1.0 and 5.0: word 2 (the relative with the verb) is marked unstressed, next to the stress on word 3. It can be given a light stress; the count does not change.
- 1.2 and 5.2: word 0 counts five syllables because the text prints a dieresis on its third vowel. Without it the line would be a syllable short.
- 1.3 and 5.3: word 2 counts three syllables (its last two vowels are one). Two runs of vowels, after word 0 and after word 2. No other count gives six positions.
- 3.0: the two runs of vowels cross exclamation marks (after words 1 and 3). The voice pauses there; the count does not, and without them the line has nine syllables. Words 0 and 2 (the verb) are marked unstressed; the first can be heard stressed.
- 4.7: word 0 has four syllables, stressed on the second; its last vowel runs into word 1.

Passage 2:
- 9.0 (eleven): word 6 counts two syllables inside the line, stressed on the second (the pronoun). Stressing its first syllable instead would set three stresses together on positions 6, 7 and 8. In 10.0 the same word ends the line and counts three, as a final pronoun of two vowels does at a line end. The first printing has a question mark after word 6 where the transcription has an exclamation mark; the count is the same either way.
- 10.2: word 0 has its own accent on its last syllable, which runs into word 1 and falls on the second position, beside the stress on the third. I left it unmarked; it can be heard as a light stress.
- 10.5 and 10.6: the pronoun (10.5 word 1, 10.6 word 3) is the stressed form and takes the stress on the third and fifth positions.
- 10.7: word 4 is one syllable inside the line; in 10.2 the same word ends the line and is two.
- All eight lines of the song have stresses on the third and seventh positions whichever way the lighter syllables are heard.

Passage 3 (no beat tab; the count stands behind the "Shared" tab). The eight lines of verse:
- first: 1.0 words 1-2 (three syllables), 1.1 word 1 (one), 1.2 word 1 (four, stressed on its second): last stress on the sixth, sliding. No run of vowels is needed.
- second 1.3, third 1.4 (sliding), fourth 2.0, fifth 2.1 words 0-4 (sliding; words 0 and 1 are kept apart after the exclamation, and word 2 runs into word 3).
- sixth: 2.2 words 0-2 and 2.3 words 1-3. A seven that stops on its stress only if the last vowel of 2.2 word 2 runs into 2.3 word 1 across the change of speaker. Counted without that run it has its last stress on the seventh and is an eight, out of the pattern.
- seventh: 2.4 word 1 and 2.5 words 1-3. A sliding seven if the name is three syllables (its first two vowels as one), its last vowel runs into 2.5 word 1, and 2.5 word 2 is one syllable. In line 1207 of the file the same name must be four syllables to make the eleven; both counts are allowed in Italian verse.
- eighth: 2.6 words 1-2 and 2.7 words 1, 2 and 4. A seven on the same condition as the sixth. Word 3 of 2.7, in brackets, is left out: it stands for word 2.
- Why I take the run across speakers: with it all three lines fall into the pattern (sliding, plain, sliding, plain, sliding, cut, sliding, cut) of the two stanzas before, lines 1240-1247 and 1248-1257; and lines 1213-1214, an eleven shared by Germont and Violetta, need the same run (five syllables and seven make eleven only with it). I found no handbook that states the rule for a shared line. The fourth caveat tells the reader.
- One line of the stanza before does not sit in that pattern without help: line 1250, the third of Germont's four, ends in a word in *-zio*, which is sliding only if its last two vowels are counted apart, and plain otherwise. Alfredo's eight (1240-1247) have all four odd lines sliding. The ground of the "Shared" tab says exactly this and does not claim that every odd line slides.
- That the thirteen printed lines are eight lines of verse is no longer only my count: the first printing sets the pieces of each divided line in steps (pp. 34 and 35, seen in the page images; see below). What remains mine is that each of the eight is a seven.

## Facts about the verse, and where I read them (treccani.it, 2026-10-03)

- Claudio Ciociola, "versificazione", Enciclopedia dell'Italiano (2011): the name of a line depends on the number of its last stressed position, plus one; the line is the same whatever unstressed syllables follow; the ottonario has its last stress on the seventh and the scheme third and seventh prevails; after Dante and Petrarch it is used only in religious verse or verse for music.
- Ciociola, "settenario": cut and sliding sevens are taken up in the ode-canzonetta, which likes short lines and a mixture of plain, cut and sliding rhymes; it became the typical form of the cantata and of the opera aria, whereas recitative is a free discourse of elevens and sevens.
- Ciociola, "rima": the cut rhyme in a consonant, of the types *amor : cor*, is characteristic of the ode-canzonetta through the nineteenth century.
- Ciociola, "metrica e lingua": two vowels meeting between words generally count as one syllable; a meeting of vowels inside a word may be counted as two (dieresis). Two more articles were read and nothing in the reading stands on them, so they are not in `sources`: the Enciclopedia dell'Italiano's "canzone" (2010) and Andrea Della Corte's "cavatina" in the Enciclopedia Italiana (1931).
- Treccani, Enciclopedia on line, "sdrucciolo": sliding sevens have eight syllables.
- Mario Pelaez, "ottonario", Enciclopedia Italiana (1935): eight syllables, a double four, normally stressed on the third and seventh; in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries the line of dance songs, laude and carnival songs.
- Andrea Della Corte, "cabaletta", Enciclopedia Italiana (1930): a short aria; an easy piece whose effect lies in the uniform return of the rhythmic design. Treccani, Vocabolario, "cabaletta": simple and catchy, fast and insistent, placed as a rule at the close of an aria or duet, characteristic above all of Italian opera of the first half of the nineteenth century. Enciclopedia on line, "cabaletta": gives this very song as its example.
- Fabio Rossi, "melodramma, lingua del", Enciclopedia dell'Italiano (2011): the language of librettos is archaism, courtly words, anti-realism and predictability; the phrase of line 245 is still proverbial.
- Paull F. Baum, The Principles of English Versification (1922), `.cache/handbooks/baum-versification-pg21342.txt`: chapter 5, feminine rhyme defined (lines 6078-6084) and the refrain (line 6491); chapter 4, the couplet felt as a unit (lines 3208-3211); the glossary under Refrain (line 7532).

## The names of the two figures

- *refrain*: Baum's glossary. In Alfredo's mouth the four lines are not yet a refrain; they become one in her song, at the close of each stanza. The `plain` says both.
- *antilabe*: one line of verse divided between speakers. The only source I opened for the word is the Wikipedia article "Antilabe" (fetched 2026-10-03), which defines it as here and says the verse usually keeps its metrical integrity. Treccani has no entry. The fifth caveat tells the reader; `open` tells the lead. If the term is thought too thinly sourced, the lens stands as well under the plain name "shared line".
