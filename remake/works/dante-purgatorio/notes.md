Poet's notes to go with `reading.json`. Rows and words are named as the tools print them. No lines of the poem, of the translations or of Auden are copied here.

## 1. What changed when rule three arrived

BRIEF.md and POET.md were changed while I was at work (rule three, `view`, `grounds`). My first reading had no `view`, no `grounds`, and three plates: canto V, 130-136 (La Pia), canto XVII and canto XXVIII, with Longfellow beside the Italian. I rebuilt it.

- **The view.** Auden's view of this part of the poem is on record, so the piece now leads with it: the lines of canto XVII that he copied out in 1941 and set against The Tempest in The Dyer's Hand.
- **Plate 1 was replaced.** Auden never wrote about La Pia. He copied out canto XV, 73-75 in 1941, quoted the same three lines again in The Dyer's Hand (p. 230), and put two lines of the same exchange (XV, 55-56) at the head of a section there (p. 139). So the first plate is now canto XV, 67-75. It also answers the lead about a simile built with "as" and "so". The La Pia work is kept in section 8 in case the lead wants it.
- **The pair is now Okey's prose.** The Temple prose is the version Auden set, the version the class had to write from memory, and the wording Auden himself quotes for canto XV and canto XVII. Longfellow is named in ties and notes where he differs.
- **Sentences cut as my own invention** (they stood on none of the four grounds): that Dante in the garden thinks at once of the moment innocence was taken; that the sentence does what the sight did; that the lady's coming is heard in the meter; that in sound error is love with a different start; "the one real stop"; a closing contrast between what the damned did and what the souls of the mountain loved (now a plain statement about Inferno XI with its line numbers); that leaving out the second verb is what brings the two words for the girl together (the word order does that; the caption now says only that they meet at the comma).
- **Mended in the last pass**, after every source was opened again: the pages of Pearce (section 5); "plan of Hell" to "plan of lower Hell"; the lady's "last speech" to "the end of the canto"; "the first sight of the garden" to "the first sight of the lady"; `about` cut from five sentences to four; several paraphrases of Auden moved further from his wording; the scan line numbers in four `checked` fields; "a subject kept back holds the sense open" replaced by the plain fact that for three lines the sentence does not say what appeared.

## 2. Why these passages

**Plate "share": Purgatorio XV, 67-75 (rows 22.0 to 24.2).** One of the two passages of the poem that Auden copied into the notes of New Year Letter in 1941. Something can be seen in it: two comparisons (rows 22.1-22.2 and 24.2); three statements built as proportions (rows 23.0 to 24.1); words that belong to wealth as well as to love; and one verb (row 24.0, word 6) on which the two English versions and the Dante encyclopedia part company. It is spoken on the stair above the terrace of envy, so it shows a right aim for love before plate 2 names the wrong ones.

**Plate "love": Purgatorio XVII, 91-96 and 103-105 (rows 30.0 to 31.2 and 34.0 to 34.2).** The lines Auden kept returning to, cut as he cut them in his 1941 note: the first two tercets and the conclusion, with the six lines between left out. They hold an antithesis that turns on one verb, a rhyme that ties error to love, words whose history is a picture (straying, seed), and the three terms that sort the seven terraces.

**Plate "garden": Purgatorio XXVIII, 37-42 and 49-51 (rows 12.0 to 13.2 and 16.0 to 16.2).** Canto XXVIII is the one Purgatorio canto Pearce names among those announced for the examination from memory. The sentence keeps its subject back for three lines; one verb serves two clauses in the Proserpina line; the wording recalls Cavalcanti and Ovid; and it is the one plate where I scan the Italian line. Auden left nothing on these lines, so the captions keep to the plate, the text and sources. Pearce is the one ground from Auden's side.

**`next`.** It points at `dante-temple/pur-15:13`, where the talk on the stair begins in the prose Auden set. A reader who starts there goes through cantos XV to XVIII, which hold the first two plates and Virgil's whole account of love. If the lead wants every Dante piece to start at the head of its part of the poem, change it to `dante-temple/pur-01:0`.

## 3. The scansion (plate "garden", lens "Beat"): other ways of hearing

The measure is the Italian eleven-syllable line. The `meter` string of each row is the frame that row fills: stresses on 4, 6 and 10, or on 6 and 10. Rules read at Treccani. The Enciclopedia dell'Italiano (endecasillabo) gives a fixed stress on the tenth syllable and at least one main stress on the fourth or the sixth. The Vocabolario (endecasillabo) lists the common patterns: 6 and 10 with lighter stresses on 2 and 4 or 8; 4, 8 and 10; 4, 7 and 10. Sinalefe and dialefe are from the Vocabolario: two vowels that meet between words are said as one syllable, and they stay apart when the first or both are stressed. I said each line aloud and counted it again in the last pass.

- **Line 37 (row 12.0).** Marked 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. Word 3 can be heard weak; the line is then 2, 4, 8, 10. The fourth-syllable stress stands either way.
- **Line 38 (row 12.1).** 4, 6, 10. The long adverb also has a lighter stress on its first syllable, which I leave unmarked.
- **Line 39 (row 12.2).** Marked 4, 6, 7, 10. It can as well be heard 4, 7, 10, with word 2 weak and the weight on word 3. The caption leans only on the fourth syllable, which is firm (word 1).
- **Lines 40 and 41 (rows 13.0, 13.1).** 3, 6, 10 and 3, 6, 8, 10. I hear no other way. The fourth syllable is weak in both.
- **Line 49 (row 16.0).** Marked 3, 6, 7, 10. Word 4 can be heard weak after the stress before it; word 0 can take a stress if the address is spoken with emphasis; word 6 can take one too. Stresses on 3, 6 and 10 stand either way.
- **Line 50 (row 16.1).** The name is stressed on its second syllable in Italian.
- **Line 51 (row 16.2).** The count needs the vowels of words 2 and 3 kept apart (a comma, and the first vowel stressed). Run together the line would have ten syllables.
- **Rows 12.1, 13.0, 13.2.** The last word of each ends in two vowels that count as two syllables at the line end. Inside the line (row 13.2, word 5) such a pair counts as one.
- **Vowels run together** four times: row 12.0 words 5-6, row 12.2 words 2-3, row 13.1 words 1-2, row 16.0 words 4-5. The uncounted syllable is marked `~` on the first of the two words.
- `div` is given wherever the checker's rule of thumb, made for English, divides an Italian word wrongly.

- rendere: Enciclopedia Dantesca, rendere (to give back to another what is owed him because it was lent or taken; the verb is also used of a surface that returns an image); Treccani, rendere, senses 1a, 2, 3a; Lewis and Short, reddo.
