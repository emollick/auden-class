## In short

What was wrong, in order of weight:

1. **Auden made to say more than he said** (section 5). The review of 1941 does

## 2. The ear (third passage, the Beat tab)

I counted the nine Italian rows myself before reading the poet's marks, by the
rules of the Italian eleven-syllable line (the tenth syllable always stressed; a
second main stress on the fourth or on the sixth; a vowel ending one word and a
vowel beginning the next count once, unless the first is stressed).

My count, by syllable number of each stress:

    12.0  2 4 6 8 10      12.1  4 6 10         12.2  4 6(or 7) 10
    13.0  3 6 10          13.1  3 6 8 10       13.2  2 4 6 10
    16.0  3 6 7 10        16.1  2 6 10         16.2  2 4 6 10

Every row has eleven syllables. Vowels of two words run together four times
(12.0 words 5 and 6; 12.2 words 2 and 3; 13.1 words 1 and 2; 16.0 words 4 and
5), and I looked at every other meeting of words in the nine rows to be sure
there is no fifth: where an apostrophe has already cut a vowel (12.0 word 4,
13.2 word 0) nothing meets. In 16.2 the vowels of words 2 and 3 are kept apart:
word 2 ends on a stressed diphthong, and without the break the row would have
ten syllables. The last words of 12.1 and 13.0 and of 13.2 each count their two
final vowels as two syllables, as the rhyme requires.

This agrees with the poet's marks in every row. Where two readings are open
(12.2, the seventh syllable; 16.0, the sixth and seventh together) the caption
does not lean on them, and the fifth caveat tells the reader.

The stated meter is the real one. Read for it: Claudio Ciociola,
"endecasillabo", *Enciclopedia dell'Italiano* (Treccani, 2010): the canonical
line has the tenth and either the fourth (the lesser type, whose first half
equals a five-syllable line) or the sixth (the greater type, whose first half
equals a seven-syllable line; a line with no stress on the fourth is of this
type, with a lighter stress on the second or third). Treccani, *Vocabolario*:
endecasillabo, sinalefe, dialefe, terzina.

Finding. The caption said only that the stress "moves" in the two rows that
bring the lady and called that a change of "step" (the poet's own reason, with
no handbook behind it). The handbook supports a firmer and plainer statement.
Rows 12.0 to 12.2 are of the lesser type: their first halves are 12.0 words 0 to
2, 12.1 word 0 and 12.2 words 0 and 1, five syllables each, stressed on the
fourth. Rows 13.0 and 13.1 have no stress on the fourth and are of the greater
type, and their seven-syllable first halves are filled exactly by the subject
(13.0 words 0 to 2) and by two of the things she is doing (13.1 words 0 to 3).
The caption and the reason now say this, on Ciociola. "Her two actions" would
have been a general statement that row 13.0 breaks (words 3 to 5 there are a
third thing she does), so the caption says "two things she is doing".

## 3. The terms

- **Simile** (first passage). *Silva Rhetoricae*, simile: an explicit
  comparison, often made with "like" or "as". The passage has two, each with
  its marker: rows 22.1 word 4 and 22.2 word 0; row 24.2 word 1. The name and
  the plain-words gloss are right.
- **Antithesis** (second passage). *Silva Rhetoricae*, antithesis: contrasting
  words or ideas set side by side, often in parallel structure. Rows 31.0 and
  31.1: two subjects (31.0 word 1; 31.1 word 1), two verbs (31.0 words 2 and 3;
  31.1 word 2), the same root on both sides (31.0 word 5; 31.1 word 3), and the
  turn on 31.1 word 0. The name and the gloss are right.
- **Zeugma** (third passage). *Silva Rhetoricae*, zeugma: one word, usually a
  verb, governs two or more parts of a sentence; prozeugma: the verb is said in
  the first clause and understood in the others. The verb is row 16.1 word 4;
  row 16.2 holds a first pair (words 1 and 2) and a second (words 4 and 5) that
  has no verb of its own. The name and the gloss are right. Bosco and Reggio
  call this line a chiasmus. Its grammar does not cross: each half is subject
  then object. What crosses is the person (the girl is the object of the first
  half and the subject of the second), and the caption says that in plain
  words. Zeugma stays; the point is recorded in `open`.
- **The chained tercet** (Rhyme tab). Treccani, *Vocabolario*, terzina: the
  middle line of each group of three gives its rhyme to the first and third of
  the next. The marks agree: row 30.1 ends the first rhyme, rows 31.0 and 31.2
  answer it.
- **Proportion** (Sentence tab of the first passage). The poet wrote "ratio".
  Chiavacci Leonardi's note on lines 70 to 72 uses the Italian for proportion,
  and the reason already said proportion. Mended to one word throughout.

**First passage, canto XV, lines 67-75**

- *Simile.* False: "the reply ends on that picture". The argument ends on a
  second comparison (row 24.2), and Virgil's speech runs on to line 81; at line
  76 he calls what he has said his reasoning. Invented: "the thing shared is
  changed from goods ... to light", "so the kind of good is changed". What the
  comparison is for is said by Landino on these lines (also quoted by Sapegno):
  light is no less in one body because more bodies receive it. The caption now
  gives that, with his name and date, and says that the reasoning ends on a
  second comparison.
- *Sentence.* Stands. The count was made again by script: the word for "more"
  comes ten times in lines 55 to 75 (lines 55, 56, 57, 58, 60, 62 twice, 73, 74
  twice). Hollander, citing Barolini, counts the seven of lines 55 to 62 and
  notes those of 73 and 74. "A sum in division" was reworded: the question takes
  sharing for division, which lines 49 to 51 say of earthly goods.
- *Word.* The reason was the poet's: "the picture of wealth is kept and its kind
  is changed", resting on the root sense of 23.1 word 3 (a high price) and on
  23.2 word 4 (worth). No source reads the lines so, and the dictionary gives
  23.2 word 4 here as a name for God. Cut. What the lines and the sources bear
  out: the question is put in three words of property (rows 20.0 word 5, 20.1
  words 2 and 5); the nine lines of the answer hold no word of owning (I read
  every word of them for this); their vocabulary centers on light and love
  (Chiavacci Leonardi; Hollander); two of the verbs are of giving (23.0 word 2;
  24.2 word 6); Virgil has just told Dante that his mind is fixed on earthly
  things (lines 64-66). "Its verbs are of giving" would have been a general
  statement the other verbs break, so the caption says two.
- *Translation.* Stands. The two commentators who read the verb as love are now
  named, and "the two lines" became "lines 73 and 74".

**Second passage, canto XVII, lines 91-96 and 103-105**
