## The drawing (plate `bee`, tab "Book")

**What it is.** Two small drawings, one under the other, each under a
heading in small capitals. Both are made of the same 103 squares, a
square to an ode.

1. *13 measures, a square for each ode.* A row of squares to each
   measure, all from one left edge, under four headings. The headings
   are the four totals the caption names, each number in the display
   face: 37 in the Alcaic stanza, 25 in the Sapphic, 34 in the five
   Asclepiadic stanzas (five rows, each with its count: 3, 9, 12, 7,
   3), 7 in the six other measures (six rows, each with its name).
   Beside the two long rows stands whom each stanza is named for, and
   beside three rows which passage of this page is in that measure.
2. *4 books, the odes in their order.* The same squares in four rows,
   Book I to Book IV, each square in the color of its measure, with a
   dot under the three odes quoted on the page.

Colors: red for the Sapphic stanza, because the passage of this plate
is in it (red is "this, here"); ink for the Alcaic; then blue and
ochre, in the kit's order, for the other two headings. Four kinds.

**Why this form.** The caption's claim is a count: how the 103 odes
are shared among thirteen measures, and that the two stanzas named for
the poets of Lesbos hold most of them. A square to an ode makes that
plain without an axis: rows from one edge are lengths from zero, and
a reader who doubts a number can count it. Squares, not solid bars,
because the same marks can then be set out a second way. The tab is
called "Book" and the facts give the place of every ode, so the second
drawing puts the squares in the order of the books: it shows where
each measure falls (the black and red that take turns through the
first half of Book II need no words), and it is where a reader finds
"the last ode of his third book", which the caption speaks of: the
last square of row III. One idea each; the first is the key to the
second.

**What it is drawn from.** Only the lens's `facts`, read by key at
run time: `measures` (each one's `measure`, `odes`, `in`, `family`,
`named for`, `shown here`, `read`), `books`, `odes in all`,
`measures in all`, and the four totals whose names begin "in the".
These are the two rows the plain table left empty. Before it draws,
the code checks that every measure's lists add up to its `odes`, that
every book's odes run 1, 2, 3 to its number with none missing or
twice, that the totals are the sums of the rows under them, and that
all of it comes to `odes in all` in `measures in all`. If anything
does not add up it draws nothing and throws `facts: ...`, and the kit
shows the plain table (tried with one count made wrong in the built
copy). No name is written into the code: the red measure is found by
matching the plate's own source to a measure's `read`. One more key
is read if it is there, and is not there yet: `spoken of` (proposed
below).

**The three labels open the book.** Three measures carry a `read`
key, the place of the passage of this page that is in that measure.
The README says such a key is there to send the reader from a mark to
its place in the book printed here, so each of the three labels ("the
third passage, from Odes IV.2" and the two like it) is a link to
`#/read/` and that place. It is ruled under with a hairline, as the
page rules its links and as the drawings of Dante, Karamazov and
Kierkegaard rule theirs; under the pointer the rule turns red and
the row stays lit. A click or a tap opens the ode at the stanza
quoted, where the reader marks where the passage begins and offers
the way back. For Odes I.3 it is the page's only way into the ode,
and for IV.2 the only way to the stanza quoted. A finger that touches
the square of a quoted ode is told which ode it is, and the name of
the passage in that line is a link too. In a private preview of the
one piece, which has no book to open, there is no link and no rule.

**What the pointer adds** (a tap on a phone). A heading or a row says
how many of its odes stand in each book and lights them in both
drawings. The number of a book says how its odes are shared among the
four headings. A square says which ode it is and in what measure, and
lights its twin in the other drawing. The numbers, folded under the
drawing, list every ode of every measure by book.

## The Measure tabs: can a reader with no Latin tell from the key what each mark means?

**Which mark is which: yes. What the two chief marks mean: no. And on
a phone the key is not there when it is wanted.** I went through both
tabs mark by mark at 1280, 390, 360 and 320, and measured them in the
browser. The marks themselves are right: 24 long and 15 short on
`ship` (39 syllables), 48 and 28 on `bee` (76, with one more that is
not counted), each under its syllable, and they agree with the measure
the reading names.

The key has four rows on `ship` and five on `bee`: a dash, "a long
syllable"; a small curve, "a short syllable"; a dot, "a stress in the
voice"; on `bee` a wide gray curve, "a syllable that is not counted:
it runs into the next"; two upright hairlines, "the break in the
line". Row by row:

1. **Long and short are named, not explained.** Nothing in the key,
   and nothing in either caption, says that length here is time: that
   a long syllable is held about twice as long as a short one. A
   reader with no Latin can as well take "long" for a syllable of many
   letters, or a loud one. The caption of `ship` says a dash marks a
   long one and a curve a short one, that Latin verse is measured by
   that pattern, and that English verse goes by accent; the caption of
   `bee` names neither dash, curve nor dot. A few words in the key
   would do it ("a long syllable: twice the time of a short one"). The key is the
   kit's (lens-beat.js lines 163 and 164); a sentence in a caption
   would be the critic's.
2. **The dot is said plainly, but in words the page uses for something
   else, and half of it is in no key.** The caption of `ship` calls
   the dots the accents of the Latin words, and the first caveat at
   the foot of the page says the grammar also teaches a beat of the
   voice within each measure, "which is not drawn here". The key's "a
   stress in the voice" sounds like that beat. For a measure of long
   and short the row would be truer as "the accent of the word"
   (lens-beat.js line 168 already chooses its words by the kind of
   measure). And the accent is shown twice: by the dot, and by the
   letters of that syllable, which are set heavy and dark (weight 700
   against 300 for the rest: atlas.css lines 269 to 271). The heavy
   letters are the first thing the eye meets on the tab and the key
   has no row for them.
3. **The bar is now right.** The examiner noted that the key called it
   "a pause" where five of the eight bars mark only the regular break
   of the measure. The lead has mended that: for a measure of long and
   short the key says "the break in the line" (lens-beat.js line 173),
   which is true of all eight (`ship` rows 0.0 and 0.1, `bee` rows 6.0
   to 6.2 and 7.0 to 7.2) and is the captions' own word.
4. **The tie** (`bee`, row 6.2 only) is said rightly. As a mark it is
   the short mark over again, a curve open upward, only wider and
   gray, and it touches the short mark under its right end: see the
   faults.
5. **On a phone the key and the marks are never on one screen.** The
   key's first row stands 962 px under the first marks on `ship` at
   390 (977 at 360, 1,031 at 320) and 1,354 px under them on `bee`
   (1,358; 1,376). The order on a narrow plate is: the passage, the
   caption, the name of the measure, the numbered remarks, and only
   then the key (atlas.css lines 393 to 395 put the remarks first on
   purpose). On `ship` the caption's first sentence does the key's
   work. On `bee` nothing does until the foot of the plate. At 1280
   the key stands beside the first lines (334 and 309 px under the
   first marks), and on `bee`, which is 1,457 px tall there, the
   caption and the key follow the reader down beside the second
   stanza (tried in a window of 1280 by 800:
   `look/look-bee-1-1280-second-stanza-in-a-window.png`). So at 1280
   all is well. On a Measure tab the key is four or five short rows
   that nothing above them can be read without: on a narrow plate I
   would set it straight under the passage, before the caption.

Two more things on these tabs are in no key and no caption, and both
blur what the tab is there to show:

6. **The dash that means "long" is itself of many lengths.** Its
   length follows the width of the letters over it (lens-beat.js line
   98), from 7 px to 16. At 1280 nearly every dash reaches 16 and it
   does not show. At 390 the 24 dashes of `ship` are of seven lengths
   and at 320 of nine; on `bee` the 48 are of nine and ten. A reader
   told that a dash is a long syllable will take a dash twice as long
   for a longer one. One length would say what the measure says.
7. **The hairline says the opposite of the marks.** When the tab is
   drawn a red hairline crosses each line. It gives every syllable
   the same time (333 ms on `ship`, 171 ms on `bee`: lens-beat.js
   lines 137 to 139) and lifts each accented syllable as it passes
   (line 146). So the one thing that moves on the tab acts out the
   accent at an even pace, under a caption that says the measure is of
   long and short. If it runs here it should take twice as long over
   a dash as over a curve, and leave the accents alone; or not run
   for a measure of this kind. `look/look-ship-0-1280-hairline.png`
   shows it under way.

## Proposed for the reading

Each was tried in the built copy only, which was written again from

**1. The plate `crossing`: set the two versions as two blocks.**

    "beside": true,
    "turn": { "p:5.3": 2 }

tab shows is the order of its words from line to line. Set line under
