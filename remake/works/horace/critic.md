## 5. The four places the artist found thin, and the words of the key

### 5.1 What a long syllable is (both Measure tabs): for the finisher

**What the page fails to tell.** That long and short are lengths: how
long a syllable lasts in the saying, with one long counted as two
shorts. The first Measure caption names the two marks and goes straight
on to say that Latin verse is measured by them. A reader with no Latin
can take "long" for many letters, or for loud. The whole tab, and
Auden's sentence in it, turns on this, and one clause would do, once,
in the caption of passage 1: it is the first tab the page shows.

**The words I would use**, in place of the caption's second sentence
("Latin verse is measured by that pattern."):

> A long syllable takes the time of two short ones, and Latin verse
> is measured by that pattern.

The caption is 116 words and would then be 127. Room for it: the
sentence "Such measures began in Greek lyric poetry and the Romans
adopted them" (12 words) can go, leaving 115 (counted by script). The
Book tab of passage 3 tells where the
measures came from, at length and with a drawing. If it goes, `why`
loses its first clause and `stands` its "and where the measures came
from".

**The ground** is not mine to open. Two places already in the reading
carry the claim: the lens's third ground (Allen and Greenough,
sections 607 and 623, note: a long counts as two shorts), and the

### 5.5 The words of the key: which the page should use throughout

The captions and the first caveat already agree with each other. The
kit's key is the odd one out, in one row.

- The dash. Key: "a long syllable". Captions: "a dash marks a long

- The curve. Key: "a short syllable". Captions the same. **Use: a
  short syllable.**
- The dot. Key: "a stress in the voice". Captions: "the accents of the

- The wide gray curve (passage 3). Key: "a syllable that is not
  counted: it runs into the next". Caption: "whose last syllable runs

- The two upright strokes. Key: "the break in the line". Captions:
  "breaks after its sixth syllable", "stops at the break", "at that
  break". **Use: the break in the line.** One thing to know: in
  passage 3 the caption claims only a word-end after the fifth
  syllable of the lines shown, and then calls it "that break". The
