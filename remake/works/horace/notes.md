## What Auden said, and where I read it (all read on 2026-10-03)

Scans are in the scratchpad, research/auden/. EF = The Enchafed Flood
(1950), DH = The Dyer's Hand (1962), FA = Forewords and Afterwords
(1973), PEARCE = Donald Pearce's memoir (1985).

1. EF p. 7 (ef.txt lines 351-386): the ship as a figure for the state
   comes in early, he says, but only at times of danger to society; a
   ship's proper place is in port. He quotes the opening of Odes I.14
   (as far as the first word of its third line) with a prose version.
   In ordinary times society is pictured as a City or a Garden, and a
   voyage is an evil to be put up with (lines 372-386; p. 8 from line
   389 adds the Ship of Fools).
2. EF p. 9 (ef.txt lines 439-477): the sea is a place to keep out of,
   and the attempt to cross it is rash, close to hubris (lines
   458-460; this is the sentence the view quotes eleven words of). He
   then quotes Odes I.3, lines 21 to 24, with a prose version.
   Page numbers in this scan: the number stands at the foot of the
   page (10 at line 516), even pages are headed with the book's title,
   odd pages with the chapter's. Counting back, p. 7 = lines 351-388,
   p. 8 = 389-438, p. 9 = 439-477. This agrees with research note C.
3. DH p. 485, "Translating Opera Libretti" (written with Chester
   Kallman), dh.txt lines 22789-22800: a quantitative prosody, like
   Greek and Latin verse, has syllables long or short, one long equal
   to two shorts; an accentual prosody like English ignores length and
   distinguishes accented from unaccented syllables.
4. FA pp. 5-6, "The Greeks and Us" (1948), fa.txt lines 280-311:
   translating poetry always costs a great deal; verse by length
   without rhyme and rhymed verse by stress share only that both are
   patterns of rhythm (p. 5); Greek is inflected, so its meaning is
   not tied to the place of a word in the sentence, as English
   meaning is (p. 6). NOTE: he says this of Greek, not of Latin.
5. DH p. 6, "Reading", dh.txt line 629: the language of his Eden
   would be a mixture by origin, as English is, and heavily
   inflected. Not used in the reading.
6. DH p. 297, "Marianne Moore", dh.txt lines 14083-14103: verse that
   counts syllables and nothing else is hard for an English ear; he
   knew it first from Bridges (lines of six or twelve syllables).
7. FA p. 124, "A Civilized Voice" (on Pope), fa.txt lines 6039-6044:
   with age, and in darker times, Horace and Pope are the kind of
   poet he goes back to more and more for rest.
8. FA pp. 326-27, "A Worcestershire Lad", fa.txt lines 15510-15520: he
   compared three versions of the last stanza of Odes IV.7 (Johnson,
   Michie, Housman) and found Housman's the furthest from the Latin.
9. PEARCE p. 133: in the admission interview of September 1941 Horace
   is the first author Auden names for the course.
10. Not in the scans (research note C only, from snippets): the poem
    "The Horatians" (City Without Walls, 1969, pp. 26-28), and the
    first line of Odes I.38 quoted in the notes to New Year Letter.

11. Edward Mendelson, Early Auden, Later Auden: A Critical Biography
    (Princeton, 2017), full text fetched from archive.org on
    2026-10-03 (item early-auden-later-auden-a-critical-biography-
    edward-mendelson-2017, the _djvu.txt file; saved in the scratchpad
    as horace/mend.txt). The page number stands at the head of each

    - p. 416 (mend.txt lines 21040-21055): Auden took Marianne

    - p. 747 (lines 37967-37970): what Auden said to James Michie, a

    - pp. 778-79 (lines 39553-39586): "The Horatians" dates from the

## The three passages, and why these

All three are short enough to show whole, each has a tab that stands
on Auden or on his own practice, and each shows something a reader
without Latin can see on the page.

### First passage: Odes I.14, rows 0.0 to 0.3 (lines 1 to 4)

Why: Auden quotes the opening (The Enchafed Flood, p. 7) and so does
Quintilian, as his example of allegory, and both stop at the same
word, row 0.2 w0. The stanza shows the measure by length in four
lines, with the break of the long line falling on a question mark.

- Measure (beat). Tool: `node build/tools/latin.mjs horace-la o1-14 0
  0 --meter ascl,ascl,pher,glyc`. All four rows fit, and the meter
  strings in the reading are the tool's. The voice is the tool's "by
  stress" form. The tool leaves words of one syllable unmarked and
  says "decide each". My decisions: a dot on the interjection (row
  0.0 w0 and row 0.1 w1) and on the question word (row 0.1 w2); none
  on the preposition (0.0 w3), the pronoun (0.0 w5) or the
  conjunction (0.2 w3). I added the lone `|` after the sixth syllable
  of the two long rows; Allen and Greenough print the break there in
  every lesser Asclepiadic of section 626. Only in row 0.1 is it also
  a stop in the sense.
  Another way of hearing it: a reader could stress the pronoun at 0.0
  w5 (the ship spoken to) and leave the interjections light. Nothing
  in a caption depends on these dots.
  The tool lists syllables that are "long by the vowel's own length,
  which this tool cannot see". No caption leans on the length of any
  single vowel, so these were not each looked up. The headwords I did
  open agree with the tool where they mark a length: navis has a long
  first vowel, occupo a short second one, novus a short first one.
- Allegory (figure, drawn as a list). Three single words, each its
  own tag, labelled with Quintilian's three equations in the Latin
  order. The kit's map would join the ship to the waves, which says
  something else.
- English (compare). Stands on Conington's preface: the stanza after
  Milton, ten and six syllables, rhyme added.

### Second passage: Odes I.3, rows 5.0 to 5.3 (lines 21 to 24)

Why: Auden quotes exactly these four lines (The Enchafed Flood, p.
9), and they hold the interlocked order the leads asked for.

## The lead after an outside review: what Auden means by hubris (5 October, 10:12 UTC)

The owner passed on an outside reader's review of the site. One of its points was the last
sentence of the Word tab of the second passage, which called hubris "a Greek word that first
meant presumption toward the gods". Checked (`review/outside/hubris.md`, then
`facts-hubris-valour.md`): the reviewer is right. The clause came from the Online Etymology
Dictionary, which does say it. Liddell and Scott's Greek lexicon has no such sense, and in Homer
and Hesiod the word is used of outrage done to other people.

no god and no limit. The site's rule is Auden's view where there is one, so the history was cut
and his own account of the word put in its place: "The Greeks and Us" (1948), in *Forewords and
Afterwords*, pp. 19 and 21, where he spells it hybris.
