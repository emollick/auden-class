### Wording

1. **The passage** (`about`), second sentence.
   Old: "... a seer said the goddess Artemis asked the life of the king’s
   daughter, and the king spoke."
   New: "... asked for the life of the king’s daughter, and the king spoke."
   Fault: an antique turn where the ordinary idiom does the work.

2. **Passage 1, Figure**, last sentence of the caption.
   Old: "This is the choice Auden gives first for the Greek tragic
   situation, in which either course is wrong: kill his daughter or fail
   the army he leads."
   New: "This choice is Auden’s first example of the Greek tragic
   situation, in which either course is wrong: kill his daughter or fail
   the army he leads."
   Fault: "gives first for" has to be read twice. The head of the page
   says the same thing the plain way, and the caption now uses its words.

3. **Passage 1, Metaphor**, the figure in plain words (`plain`).
   Old: "one thing called by the name of another: here the compulsion of
   Fate is called a yoke, the harness of oxen"
   New: "one thing called by the name of another"
   Fault: padding. The second half repeated the caption's second and third
   sentences, which stand a few lines over it, almost word for word.

4. **Passage 1, Metaphor**, the first row of the key (the `label` of the
   first part). Old: "the harness". New: "the picture". See section 7.

5. **Passage 1, Rhyme**, fifth sentence of the caption.
   Old: "Two couplets follow."
   New: "Two couplets follow, pairs of rhymed lines."
   Fault: a word of the trade left unexplained. The next sentence says what
   a handbook calls the couplet, not what one is. The plain words stand on
   grounds the tab already lists (Baum's couplet "rimed aa", and the
   *Britannica* under couplet). The caption is now 118 words by the
   checker's count.

6. **Passage 1, Word**, first sentence of the caption.
   Old: "The glosses give the Greek behind the translator’s words."
   New: cut.
   Fault: a signpost. It tells the reader what the small notes are, and
   each of them already begins "Greek:". "Glosses" is also a word of the
   trade that the page uses nowhere else.

7. **Passage 2, Word**, fourth and fifth sentences of the caption.
   Old: "Her last line asks two things. <b>Yield</b> stands for a Greek
   verb that means be persuaded, or obey."
   New: "In her last line <b>Yield</b> stands for a Greek verb that means
   be persuaded, or obey."
   Fault: a signpost. The two things are never counted off, and three
   sentences later the caption says she asks one thing.

8. **Passage 2, Word**, the note on the king's third line (the Greek for
   the cloths).
   Old: "Poikila are stuffs woven or embroidered in many colors; ..."
   New: "Poikila are fabrics woven or embroidered in many colors; ..."
   Fault: a quaint word where the ordinary one would do.

9. **Passage 2, Word**, the note on the queen's sixth line.
   Old: "The Greek has no word for victors."
   New: "The Greek line has no word for victors."
   Fault: as it stood it could be read as a statement about the Greek
   language.

10. **Passage 3, Figure**, last sentence of the caption.
    Old: "So the robe is likened to a net, then called one, before it is
    named, and the king is its catch."
    New: cut.
    Fault: it sums up the three sentences before it, and closes on a turn
    made for the ending. The caption still goes from what the lines say
    she did (its first sentence, which is the reason) to the three
    namings and the Greek. `why` and `stands` never leaned on it and are
    as they were. The caption is now 94 words.

11. **Passage 3, Play**, fourth sentence of the caption.
    Old: "... the elders mourn their king, wound in a <i>spider-web</i>."
    New: "... the elders mourn their king, wrapped in a <i>spider-web</i>."
    Fault: beside a murdered king "wound" is read first as an injury. In
    the lament the queen weaves the web and winds it; "wrapped" says that
    without the second sense, and claims no more.

12. **Passage 3, Play**, last sentence of the caption.
    Old: "The picture of the victory is the picture of his death."
    New: cut.
    Fault: an ending that poses. A short balanced sentence, set after
    Auden's, that draws the conclusion for the reader. Without it the
    caption shows the same thing and stops: the first two nets are thanks
    for victory, the later ones are the king's and Cassandra's, the elders
    mourn him, and Auden says the hero begins in glory and ends in
    suffering. `why` still says the link in one sentence, on the same
    grounds, and I left it. The caption is now 106 words.

13. **More from Auden**, first entry, its first words.
    Old: "On the same page Auden wrote that the tragic hero is a warning"
    New: "In “The Greeks and Us” Auden also wrote that the tragic hero is
    a warning"
    Fault: "the same page" pointed at a source line three passages higher
    up. The page number is still under the entry.

14. **Passage 1, Word**, the small note under the fifth line.
    Old: "Greek: which is free of evil"
    New: "Greek: which is free of evil?"
    Fault: without the mark it reads on from the English line as a
    clause of it. The Greek line is a question, as the note beside it says.

15. **Passage 1, Metaphor**, what the caption stands on (`stands`).
    Old: "... with Liddell and Scott under *lepadnon*, which cites the
    line, *ananke* and *dyo*."
    New: "... with Liddell and Scott under *ananke*, *dyo* and *lepadnon*,
    which cites the line."
    Fault: the clause in the middle of the list made the entry seem to
    cite three things. Only the order changed.

16. **Passage 1, Word**, what the caption stands on (`stands`).
    Old: "... under *liponaus* and *lepadnon*, which cite these lines,
    *themis*, *ananke*, *barys*, *ker* and *dyo*."
    New: "... under *themis*, *ananke*, *barys*, *ker* and *dyo*, and under
    *liponaus* and *lepadnon*, which cite these lines."
    Fault: the same. Only the order changed, and "and under" was added.

17. **Where this page may be wrong**, third entry, last sentence.
    Old: "His essay of 1945 and introduction of 1947 were read only in
    snippets of scanned copies."
    New: "His essay of 1945 was read only in snippets of a scanned copy."
    Why: the introduction of 1947 is no longer on the page (see below).

18. **Passage 1, Word**, fourth sentence of the caption.
    Old: "Auden describes sparing her as a failure of his duty to the
    army."
    New: "Auden describes sparing her as a failure of the king’s duty to
    the army."
    Fault: "his" fell to Auden, the only man in the sentence. The caption
    is now 98 words.

19. **Passage 1, Word**, the notes listed under the caption. Three cuts.
    - The note on the king's eighth line (the word for what is right).
      Old: "Themis is what custom has laid down as right." New: cut.
    - The note on the first word of the last line.
      Old: "The translator’s Fate stands for ananke: force, constraint,
      necessity." New: cut.
    - The note on the sixth line (the old word for a deserter), its second
      sentence. Old: "Aeschylus’s word is liponaus, one who deserts the
      fleet." New: cut. Its first sentence stays.
    Fault: padding. Each said again, a few lines lower on the same screen,
    what the caption had just said in the same words. The two words keep
    the small note set under them in the passage; like three words of
    passage 2, they now have no line in the list. The poet's guide asks
    one sentence of such a note, and makes it optional. Nothing in `why`,
    `stands` or the grounds leaned on these lines.

20. **Passage 2, Word**, the note on the queen's fifth line (the rare word
    for what goes with good fortune).
    Old: "... then, by transfer, a natural or necessary accompaniment or
    attribute."
    New: "... then, by extension, a natural or necessary accompaniment or
    attribute."
    Fault: a dictionary-maker's shorthand where the ordinary phrase does
    the work.

21. **Passage 3, Figure**, the figure in plain words (`plain`).
    Old: "... With as in front it is a simile, a likeness said outright."
    New: "... With “as” in front it is a simile, a likeness said outright."
    Fault: a word spoken of, not used, with nothing to show it, so the

22. **Passage 3, Figure**, fifth sentence of the caption.
    Old: "Only the third line gives the plain noun, a <b>robe</b>."
    New: "Only the last line gives the plain noun, a <b>robe</b>."
    Fault: "the third line" meant the third of the three just spoken of,

## 3. Sentences I doubted and did not change

**Auden's words.** I read every paraphrase of him, in the captions, at
the head and in the list, against what he wrote: those from “The Greeks
and Us” and from *The Dyer's Hand* against the scans (pages 19 to 21,
and 173 to 174), those of 1945 against the research notes' block H4,
since no whole copy has been seen. None says more than he does. Outside
the two quotations no run of his own words is longer than five or six
ordinary ones. The two quotations are as the scans have them.

1. **Passage 2, Word, the last two sentences** ("So her argument is his
   own good fortune ..." and Auden on good fortune and hubris). This is
   where the page comes nearest to making him say more than he said. His
   sentence is about the Greek hero in general; the page sets it beside
   the queen's word. The caption does not say he said it of her, the
   ground says it is a general statement, and the third caveat says the
   page applies such sentences to these lines. Left as it is.

2. **Passage 3, Word, the last sentence** ("The robe, named in the same
   two words, is what holds him for the blow."). It is the page's own
   conclusion and it closes with a ring. I kept it because it states
   what the lines state (the two words, and that he could not flee), and
   without it the caption would end on Auden's walk with nothing to tie
   it to this passage.

3. **Passage 1, Rhyme, the last sentence** ("So the rhyme left open at
   the line of the two woes is closed by the prayer ..."). It says again
   what the two sentences before it show, and it comes close to telling
   the reader what the translator's rhyme means. I kept it because it is
   the only sentence that says what the rhyme does there, and it claims
   no more than which line opens the rhyme and which closes it.

4. **Passage 1, Rhyme, the whole tab.** It pays: a reader sees how the
   speech is built. But what they learn is Morshead's, as the caption's
   first two sentences and two caveats say, and it is the tab that stands
   furthest from the head of the page. The picture also draws a sixth
   rhyme, the pair of the elders' two closing lines, for which the
   caption has no word; "Two couplets follow" is said with three pairs
   in view. Everything the caption points at is there. A sentence for
   the sixth pair would be a new statement, so I added none.

5. **Passage 3, Play, third sentence** ("Each later net is said of the
   king or his captive Cassandra"). True: the third net is said of the
   king's rumored wounds. But it catches no one, the drawing gives it a
   line named "no one", and a reader who checks the caption against the
   drawing finds a line the caption does not account for. A clause for
   it would be a new statement in the caption. For the lead.

6. **Passage 2, Figure, two places.** "After it he walks" jolts, and "an
   honor for gods, a pride he fears" sets two things side by side as if
   they were one. The caption is at 119 words and every other sentence in
   it carries a fact, so there was no room to ease either.

7. **Passage 2, Sentence, the last two sentences.** The caption ends on
   two cautions about the Greek text. They are needed, and they make the
   tab stop on a qualification. To move them up would put the king's
   surrender last, which is a stronger close and a change of emphasis
   that is not mine to make.

8. **Passage 3, Word, the first three sentences.** "Two of the
   translator's words need their old senses" announces, and the two
   senses were already given in the Figure tab's caption and are given a
   third time in the notes. I left them because a reader may open Word
   without Figure, and the tab's own matter (the two Greek words from the
   palace door) needs the nets named first.

9. **More from Auden, first entry:** "a law of right that holds for
   everyone" is stiff. Every plainer wording I tried came nearer to his
   own words or said less than he does.

10. **The head of the page** is not mine to touch. For the record: three
    colons in four sentences, and "being placed so" is a little antique.

11. **Small things left.** "Loath" twice in passage 3 Word (old but
    current). The chorus's four names and its verb (section 2): each
    reads naturally where it stands; an editor holding to American usage
    would settle them.

**A tab that pays nothing:** none. The thinnest is the first half of
passage 3 Word (item 8).

## 4. What on the page got in the way of reading

1. **Two numberings of the lines, and nothing to say which is which.**
   Everywhere but the drawing a line number is the Greek's: "the Greek of
   lines 1380 to 1383" at the foot, "Line 211 of the Greek" in a note,

   line for no one, and the caption beside it says each later net is said
   of the king or Cassandra (section 3, item 5). The reader has to point
   at the mark to learn what it is.

3. **"The figures" under a tab called "Figure".** On this page "Figure"
   is the name of a tab on all three passages and means a figure of
   speech. Under the drawing, in the same passage, "The figures" opens
   the table of numbers. Two senses of one word a few lines apart.

4. **"Figure" beside "Metaphor".** On passage 1 the first tab is named
   for the kind (Figure, which holds an anaphora) and the second for the
   figure itself (Metaphor). On passage 3 the tab named Figure holds a
   metaphor. A reader may take it that a metaphor is not a figure. Small.

5. **The same thing three times on one screen** in the Word tabs: under
   the word, in the caption, and in the list below the caption. Less so

6. **Two specks.** In `agamemnon-page-1280-2.png` a small mark stands
