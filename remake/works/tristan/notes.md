## 1. Auden's view, and where I read it

The view is taken from his review "Eros and Agape" (The Nation, 28 June 1941),
in Prose, Volume II (2002), pp. 137-40. The class that read the libretto met
that autumn, and Pearce says it was set de Rougemont's book, which the review
is about.

- p. 138: his summary of de Rougemont. The Tristan legend, with Wagner's opera
  as its height, is the chief form of a myth of passion in which perfection
  comes only by death; to the two lovers love is fated and nothing they answer
  for, and it lasts in time only while something stands in the way; the Don
  Juan legend is its opposite; the Christian teaching on Agape stands against
  both. The nine words quoted in the view are from the sentence on the two
  lovers. The running head of p. 138 stands before that sentence and the head
  of p. 139 just after the sentence on Agape.
- pp. 139-40: found in this session and absent from the research notes. After
  a long quotation from de Rougemont, and after saying that the book's
  definition of Eros is vague, Auden speaks for himself. Eros is the basic
  will of every creature to realize itself. In man it has to make the possible
  actual by a series of decisions, and it runs from that in two ways: it hides
  in the actual alone (Don Juan), or it hides in the possible alone until,
  unless the man falls sick, the physical world breaks in on his consciousness
  and destroys it. His next sentence gives Tristan as the case: bound to
  something past, the drinking of the potion, his tie to Isolde leaves
  nothing possible. He then asks what could free man so that his Eros dares
  to decide. The running head of p. 140 falls inside the sentence on the
  first of the two ways, about a hundred words before the sentence on Tristan.

I built the second passage into the view (its last sentence), into two
captions (the Verbs tab of the first passage, the Sentence tab of the third)
and into the list, because it is the one place found where Auden, in 1941 and
in his own voice, says what is wrong with these lovers in the terms of the
course: fate against decision. Both passages were read only in overlapping
search snippets of the Internet Archive scan (see section 4). The examiner
should read pp. 139-40 again at the source.

How the view meets the three passages: in the first Isolde hands the deed and
every coming choice to Lady Love; in the second the two give up their names
and no verb is left to say who does what; in the third the one who asks
disappears from her own sentence and the last adverb is the word for
unconscious.

## 2. The passages: why each, and what each tab marks

### First passage, `work`, "Whose work"

Rows 12.0 to 25.0 of `o-tristan/minne` (source lines 2361 to 2387); the
English beside it is `tristan/act-2`, block 27, rows 12 to 25.

Why this passage.
- Auden pointed here himself. The opening words of this speech are the second
  of the two epigraphs over section VI of "Balaam and His Ass" (The Dyer's
  Hand, p. 118), the section on Don Giovanni and on these lovers with their
  servants. Its argument (p. 122) is that the servants do the deeds and that
  the exchange of potions binds the two by something outside their own
  decisions.
- The speech says this in its grammar, and it can be counted. Brangaene has
  just called Isolde's plight her own work (source lines 2315 to 2337). Isolde
  takes the deed from the maid only to give it to Lady Love. In the last
  fourteen lines there are ten finite verbs: two are Isolde's and both are
  past (rows 13.0 and 24.0); seven are Lady Love's (rows 14.0 to 23.0) and go
  from perfect and past through present to two present subjunctives, what she
  may choose and where she may lead; the tenth (row 25.0) is an order to the
  maid, to let her show obedience.
- It is where the lead "short line with alliteration and rhyme together" does
  work that can be named: the pair of rhyme words that ends Isolde's own act
  (rows 12.0 and 13.0) comes back six lines later as Lady Love's (rows 18.0
  and 19.0).
- A plate holds fourteen lines. The speech is twenty-six rows. The last
  fourteen hold the whole turn from her own act to obedience; the first twelve
  (Lady Love as queen, with life and death her subjects, rows 4.0 to 9.0) are
  named in the Words caption and linked.

The tabs.
- Verbs (a sentence lens). Long brackets show whose clauses they are, short
  ones the finite verbs. The perfect of rows 14.0 and 15.0 is one verb in two
  words, so eleven short brackets make ten verbs. The infinitive at the end of
  row 25.0 hangs on the order and is not bracketed; the caption says what the
  order asks. In rows 16.0 and 17.0 either feminine form could be the subject;
  the clause that follows (rows 18.0 and 19.0), with no new subject, has the
  work grasped in her hand, and the English too makes Lady Love the one who
  takes, so I read her as the subject. The reason given: her sentences hand
  the deed over, which is what Auden's 1941 sentence says of the tie between
  the two (fixed to a thing already done).
- Rhyme. End rhymes by letter; rows 14.0 and 16.0 have none. One alliteration
  is marked as a thread, the m that begins word 1 of row 14.0 and words 0 and
  1 of row 15.0: it ties Lady Love to the power she takes the work from. I
  marked only this one because it is the only one for which I could give a
  reason without painting sounds. Seen and left unmarked: the w that begins
  six words of rows 20.0 to 24.0 (four question words and the verbs of rows
  20.0 and 24.0), and the m that begins the stressed second syllable of the
  adverb in row 13.0 (word 2). That m would carry the thread back into
  Isolde's own line, onto her word for presuming; it is not the first letter
  of its word, the caption speaks of first sounds, and there was no room to
  explain stress, so it is left for the artist or the examiner to add if they
  think it worth the words.
- Words. Plain glosses under each phrase and nine notes. The reason given:
  Isolde's words for Lady Love are words of debt, ownership and obedience, the
  last a word made for obedience to God, which meets Auden's remark of 1948
  that the myth needs a society taught to give the self to God without
  reserve.

### Second passage, `names`, "Two names"

Rows 0.0 to 13.0 of `o-tristan/duet` (source lines 3621 to 3625, 3629 to 3633
and 3637 to 3651, the speech lines without the three headings); the English
beside it is `tristan/act-2`, block 122, rows 28 to 39.

Why this passage.
- The lead: in Act 2 the lovers talk about the word for "and" between their
  names. That exchange is source lines 3361 to 3399. I did not make it a
  plate, for three reasons. The English printed on this site leaves it out,
  so a reader would have nothing beside the German. With the answer that
  follows it runs past fourteen lines. And it is talk about grammar, where the
  close of the duet is the grammar done: two names with no "and" and no verb,
  each given to the other singer, then eight lines both sing that hold no
  finite verb at all. The Crossing caption points back to the exchange.
- It is the one place in the opera on which a remark of Auden's from the 1941
  class survives: before playing the duet he said that Wagner keeps the two
  voices from unison until its end (Pearce, p. 138). In The Dyer's Hand,
  p. 122, he writes about the duet again.
- The English has twelve lines for these fourteen: it gives the names four
  lines, not six, so the crossing cannot be seen in it.

The tabs.
- Crossing (a figure lens, chiasmus). Pronoun and name, then name and pronoun,
  in rows 0.0 and 1.0 and again in rows 3.0 and 4.0; rows 2.0 and 5.0 drop the
  singer's own name. The strokes are set with `join` as pairs of part numbers,
  [[0,3],[1,2],[6,9],[7,8]]; the checker does not test them; in my private
  build they drew two crossed pairs as meant. The reason given: each addresses
  the other as the singer's self, which meets what Pearce reports of the class
  (romantic lovers as narcissists who confirm each other).
- Grammar (a sentence lens). Every one of the sixteen words of rows 6.0 to
  13.0 is marked by kind: two prepositions, four infinitives written as nouns,
  eight describing words (one of them a participle), two nouns. The reason
  given: with no finite verb there is no doer and no time, only a state named;
  Auden in 1969 on Wagner's strings of appositions and synonyms in this opera,
  and in 1962 on a passion with nothing before or after it.
- Rhyme. Each name rhymes only with itself; the one rhyme her three lines and
  his share is the pronoun for I (rows 1.0 and 4.0); the lines both sing begin
  on one rhyme four times over. The reason given: the words keep the two apart
  and then join them, as Auden said the voices do.
- Words. Glosses and six notes. Row 8.0 has the verb that Luther's Bible uses
  for a man's knowing a woman. Tristan's sentence earlier in the scene (source
  lines 3405 to 3423) has the two words that the close takes up. Its main verb
  is in the past tense in the German printed here and in the 1906 Textbuch,
  and Isolde's repetition (line 3427) is in the subjunctive; the English makes
  it a wish. I first wrote "wishes" and changed it in the last pass to say
  only what the German says: he speaks of dying so as to live undivided and
  nameless.

### Third passage, `end`, "The last twelve lines"

Rows 43.0 to 54.0 of `o-tristan/song` (source lines 5771 to 5793); the English
beside it is `tristan/act-3`, block 265, rows 44 to 55.

Why this passage.
- The lead (Isolde's last song: questions never answered, short lines, one
  long climb) and the record on the page (the song as sung in 1915). The whole
  song is fifty-five rows, far past a plate.
- The last twelve lines are where the change can be seen: the last verb with
  a person (row 43.0), the last pronoun (row 46.0), the last of thirteen
  question marks (row 46.0), then eight lines with none of these; and the
  measure changes at the same line. The "long climb" is the music's; nothing
  on the page claims it.
- No remark of Auden's on this song was found. What meets it is his 1941
  sentence on Eros hiding in the possible until the physical world destroys
  consciousness, with Tristan as the case (p. 140), and his sentence of 1962
  that what the two are after is not each other but a oneness before anything
  was many (The Dyer's Hand, p. 122).

The tabs.
- Sentence. Marks the verb with its person and the pronoun, the five
  infinitives, the four phrases that begin with the word for in, the two
  describing words and the last cry. The first sense of the verb of row 43.0
  in the DWDS is that a will other than the subject's requires the act.
  Whether the infinitives of rows 51.0 and 52.0 still hang on that verb or
  stand free cannot be settled and does not matter to the caption: no pronoun
  and no question mark come with them. The caption gives Auden's sentence with
  his own word, Eros, and keeps his proviso about sickness.
- Rhyme. Nine of the twelve lines rhyme. The three rhyme words of rows 47.0,
  48.0 and 50.0 are one word losing first one consonant and then another; the
  thread marks the three letters that rows 47.0 and 48.0 share at the head of
  the rhyme word. The last rhyme is that of the first lines the two sing
  together in Act 1 (source lines 1967 and 1969; the first heading for both
  voices is at line 1939) and of the end of the duet (lines 3647 to 3651), and
  the last line is the line that ends both of those, less the first part of
  its compound. "Can be heard as" marks the inference.
- Beat. The count of syllables, row by row, is the ground. Of the fifty-five
  rows thirty-seven have four syllables, twelve have three (rows 5, 9, 10, 12,
  13, 16, 18, 19, 51, 52, 53, 54), four have five (14, 20, 38, 49) and two
  have six (47, 48). From row 21 to row 46 every line has four but row 38.
  The change comes after the last question mark. See section 3 for the
  stresses.
- English. The Corders' singing version beside the German: the same syllable
  count in every line (4, 4, 4, 4, 6, 6, 5, 4, 3, 3, 3, 3) and the same rhyme
  scheme, with other things said. The word for unconscious has no English; in
  its place stands a kiss. Auden on song heard as syllables and on Wagner in
  English: The Dyer's Hand, p. 473.

## 3. Hearings I was unsure of

The stresses are those of the lines said aloud. I did not set them against the
notes of the score.

- Row 43.0: marked with a stress on the first syllable (the verb of the
  question) and on the third. The other hearing gives the first two syllables
  lightly and stresses only the third, as row 46.0 is marked.
- Row 44.0: stress on the first syllable and a lighter one on the third, as
  the dictionaries give this separable verb. Said as a question it can take
  full weight on the third.
- Row 46.0: the meter is given as the same four-syllable line as its
  neighbours, but the voice is marked as passing over the first beat (the
  pronoun). Heard in the pattern of rows 43.0 to 45.0 the pronoun takes the
  beat; that hearing would put weight on her last word for herself.
- Rows 47.0 and 48.0: two light syllables, then stresses on the third and
  sixth. I hear no other way.
- Row 49.0: two light syllables, a stress on the first part of the compound
  and a lighter one on the second. The other hearing gives both parts full
  weight, which is how the meter line is written.
- Row 53.0: stress on the first syllable and a lighter one on the last. For
  the rhyme with row 54.0 the last syllable can be given full weight.
- Rows 51.0, 52.0, 54.0 and 50.0: no doubt.

The caption leans on the syllable counts and on where each line begins, not on
any of the doubtful stresses. The counts were made by ear and checked against
a count of vowel groups in each row. The count that `words.mjs` prints by rule
comes out lower for rows that end in an unstressed e and was not used.

## 7. Found and left out of the list of what else Auden said

By the examiner's rule (an entry stays if it bears on a passage shown, or on
the work as he taught it, or was said in 1941). A paraphrase is given where
the lead or the examiner may want the item back.

- Forewords and Afterwords, p. 501 ("As It Seemed to Us", The New Yorker,
  3 April 1965; `fa.txt` lines 23709 to 23712, between the foot numbers 500
  and 501): Auden recalled that his mother taught him, at eight, both words
  and music of this opera's love-potion scene, which mother and son would
  then sing together. It is the most personal thing he says of the opera and
  the lead may want it on the page.
- Alan Ansen, The Table Talk of W. H. Auden (1990), p. 17, the entry dated
  8 January 1947 (Internet Archive item `tabletalkofwhaud0000anse`, read in
  snippets: `tt_fts.txt`, `tt_fwd.txt`, `tt_back.txt`, `tt_back2.txt`; the
  head of p. 17 stands a little before the remark and that of p. 18 a little
  after). In talk Auden said that the two lovers devour each other and try to
  take the world's place, that the love of man and woman fits neither of the
  two extremes, theirs and Don Giovanni's, and that Kierkegaard should have
  heard this opera; he joked that the two parts ought to be sung by two
  women. He printed the matter himself the next year ("The Greeks and Us",
  p. 25: the cases in life nearest to both myths are not heterosexual) and in
  1962, so it is not in the list.
- Forewords and Afterwords, p. 101 ("Shakespeare's Sonnets"; `fa.txt` lines
  4863 to 4865): the story is a myth and not something that could happen in
  history.
- Forewords and Afterwords, p. 250 ("The Greatest of the Monsters"; `fa.txt`
  line 12000): stage directors, having found that the second-act love scene
  works with only the faces lit, now give whole operas in the dark.
- Forewords and Afterwords, p. 284 ("Lewis Carroll"; `fa.txt` line 13493):
  1865 is also the year this opera, which he calls a masterpiece, was first
  performed.
- Forewords and Afterwords, p. 409 ("Lame Shadows", 1970; `fa.txt` lines
  19447 to 19452): on Thomas Mann's story of the same name; he calls Melot
  the malevolent troublemaker and Mark aged, melancholy and probably
  impotent.
- The Dyer's Hand, p. 25 ("Writing"; `dh.txt` line 1562): one cannot have an
  adequate notion of this opera without having heard a great Isolde. Also
  p. 144 (Bertie Wooster fancies himself a Tristan for a week), p. 265
  (Othello and Desdemona are not this kind of pair) and p. 392 (Byron's Juan
  cannot quite play Tristan). On p. 178 the opera is named inside a quotation
