# Tristan und Isolde: the examiner's record

Examined on 2026-10-03 (see examiner.txt). Verdict: **mended**. The three
passages and all eleven tabs stand; nothing had to be removed whole. What was
wrong was of the kinds EXAMINER.md lists: general statements that one word
broke, Auden made to say a little more than he said, a ground that was false,
paraphrases that changed the grammar, and five sentences that stood on none of
the four grounds. Last, I read the close of the duet in the page images of the
vocal score, which the poet had not seen: the captions of the second passage

names"). I also read, in page images, Wagner's poem as he printed it in 1873
and the two pages of the 1906 Textbuch on which the poet's grounds lean; the
Textbuch joins the two speeches of names with a brace, which its scan text
had not shown. Every change is listed in section 10. The checker ends with no

## 2. The ear

Only the last passage is scanned. I marked its twelve lines myself, by word
number, before opening the poet's marks, by the rules of German stress: a
separable verb is stressed on its first part (44.0 w0, with a lighter stress
on its third syllable); the prefixes of 46.0 w1, 51.0 w0 and 52.0 w0 are
unstressed; 53.0 w0 is stressed on its first syllable with a lighter stress on
its last; the compound of 49.0 w2 has its main stress on the first part and a
lighter one on the second. Pronunciations: German Wiktionary under
untertauchen, verhauchen, ertrinken, versinken, unbewusst and Atem; DWDS under
untertauchen and Atem.

Where I differed from the poet: nowhere in a syllable the caption leans on.
Two syllables can fairly be heard either way: 43.0 w0 (the verb of the
question) with more or less weight, and 46.0 w0 (the pronoun), which the poet
shows as a beat the voice passes over. The old first point said rows 47.0 and
48.0 were "the first of these twelve to begin off the beat"; that leaned on
the doubtful pronoun of row 46.0. It now says only that each of the two long
lines begins with two light syllables, which no reading disputes. Caveat 4
tells the reader of both doubtful syllables.

At the end I set my hearing against the music, in the page images of the
vocal score (pp. 274 and 275, leaves 285 and 286). The two lines of six
syllables are set alike: the first two words are two short notes that lead
to a strong beat, the stressed syllable of the third word is a long note on
that beat, and the rhyme word falls on the first beat of a bar. That is the
shape marked (two light, a stress, two light, a stress). Of the two doubtful
syllables, the pronoun of row 46.0 is a short note before a bar line, as the
voice line here has it; the first two words of row 43.0 are short notes
before a bar line as well, so the music gives the first word less weight
than the mark shown. A sung setting does not settle how a line is said, and
the caveat already covers the syllable, so the mark stays; `open` tells the
lead.

The counts. I counted the syllables of all fifty-five rows of the song again,
by a count of vowel groups printed row by row (no text printed), and by ear
where the rule fails (row 14.0, where a u after q is no vowel): thirty-seven
rows of four syllables, twelve of three, four of five (rows 14.0, 20.0, 38.0,
49.0) and two of six (rows 47.0 and 48.0). Rows 21.0 to 42.0 are twenty-two
rows, twenty-one of four syllables and one of five (row 38.0). So "the only
ones in the song" and "twenty-one of the twenty-two lines before them" are
true. The song has thirteen question marks, the last at row 46.0. Runs of
three-syllable rows before the end: never more than two together.

The stated measure ("short lines of one or two beats", then the counts) is a
description, not the name of a meter, and it is the real shape of the lines.

## 3. The terms, and what the page joins

- Chiasmus (Crossing). Silva Rhetoricae under chiasmus: grammatical structures
  repeated in inverted order; under antimetabole: the same words repeated in
  inverted order. Rows 0.0 and 1.0 are pronoun then name, name then pronoun,
  with different words, and so rows 3.0 and 4.0: chiasmus is the right name
  and the plain-words gloss is right. The join `[[0,3],[1,2],[6,9],[7,8]]`
  ties pronoun to pronoun and name to name in each singer's first two lines;
  the photograph shows two crosses and no stroke the caption does not mean.
  In the vocal score and in the 1906 Textbuch Tristan's two lines run name
  then pronoun, pronoun then name: a crossing too, the mirror of hers. The
  figure and its gloss hold for either wording; the marks on rows 3.0 and 4.0
  are true of the German shown (section 6).
- Alliteration (first passage, Rhyme). English Wikipedia, "Alliteration" and
  "Alliterative verse": the repeated first consonant of nearby words; the Ring
  librettos written in it. The three dotted letters (14.0 w1, 15.0 w0 and w1)
  each begin a stressed first syllable with m (German Wiktionary under Minne,
  meiner, Macht).
- "Phrases set side by side and words of like meaning" (Grammar) renders
  Auden's two terms of rhetoric; Silva Rhetoricae under appositio and
  synonymia bears the rendering out.
- "A verb in that form shows no person and no tense": English Wikipedia,
  "Nonfinite verb"; the German infinitive used as a noun, neuter and with a
  capital: "German verbs" and "German nouns".

## 4. The words

I fetched every dictionary page behind a gloss, a note or a ground (kept as
`dwds_*.txt`, `dwb_*.txt`, `wikt_*.txt`, `url_*.txt`, `wp_*.txt` in
`tristan-exam/`), and read each against what the reading says. The glosses are
right. Found and mended:

- Gehorsam (25.0 w3). Pfeifer in DWDS says the adjective arose as a rendering
  of the Latin word for the Christian's obedience to God and the clerical
  hierarchy; the noun follows in the same entry (Old High German from about
  1000). The caption said "a word made for a Christian's obedience to God".
  Caption, reason, note and ground now say that its adjective arose to render
  the Latin. Read later in page images: the poem as Wagner printed it in
  1873 (Gesammelte Schriften und Dichtungen, vol. 7, p. 50) and the vocal
  score (p. 113) both have the word with a small g, the adjective itself,
  where the German shown (the 1971 text) has the noun. The gloss is right for
  the text shown; the note now adds this fact in one sentence, and `open`
  tells the lead.
- entbrennen (9.0 w1). DWDS gives only the figurative senses (to be seized by
  a passion; to break out). The plain sense, to be set alight, is Grimm's
  sense 2 (for Latin accendi), where Luther's Bible is cited for a heart
  kindled with feeling (Genesis 43:30; 1 Kings 3:26). The gloss "catching
  fire" stands; the grounds of the Grammar and Words tabs now say which
  dictionary gives which sense.
- sollen (43.0 w0). The caption gave only the dictionary's first sense. DWDS
  has sense 1 (the will of someone who is not the subject; also of fate) and
  sense 2 b (the speaker is undecided how to act). Both are now in the caption.
- The compound at 11.0 w0. Grimm has no entry for it (searched again: no
  result). The note went on to say that the compound "appears to be Wagner's
  own", which no source says; that guess is cut.
- bewusst with a dative (Act 1, source file line 1967): Grimm under bewust,
  sense 1, known to someone. The caption of the last passage's Rhyme tab had
  turned the grammar of that line round (see section 6).

Confirmed as written: Werk sense 2 (deed); vermessen sense 3 (elevated); zur
Hand nehmen; Minne (DWDS with Pfeifer: avoided from the sixteenth century,
back in literary use in the eighteenth; Grimm, vol. VI, col. 2238, says the
same and adds that scholars knew the word again in the seventeenth); Frau
Minne (English Wikipedia; Grimm under frau cites a medieval address to Love as
lady and queen); entwenden (elevated; Grimm cites Genesis 31:9); weihen and
todgeweiht; Pfand (DWDS 1 a; Grimm: also of persons, with surety and hostage);
fassen; wenden; enden sense 3 (obsolete, to bring to an end); küren with its
kin in Pfeifer; ward (elevated, passing out of use); eigen (DWDS 1 c,
historically unfree; Grimm 3: of bondmen, and also of lovers); namenlos senses
1 and 2; nennen; trennen; erkennen (Grimm: Luther's verb for Genesis 4:1);
endlos; ewig; ein; Brust; Liebeslust; Lust (and the Online Etymology Dictionary
under lust for the narrowing of the English word); schlürfen; untertauchen;
verhauchen; Schwall; Schall; All (Pfeifer: said of God in Middle High German,
of a beloved person in the seventeenth century, then of the universe);
ertrinken; versinken; unbewusst. That the senses were current when the poem
was written (printed 1859): Grimm under verhauchen (to breathe out one's life
or spirit, cited from Wieland, Hölty and Platen) and under unbewuszt, and the
Grimm entries already named.

The sounds: Hand, Pfand and the ending of entwandt with final t (DWDS, German
Wiktionary under Pfand and gewandt); bewusst, Brust, Lust (Wiktionary, DWDS;
Grimm notes the spelling that rhymes purely with the other two); Schwall,
Schall, All (Wiktionary: the same vowel and final consonant, one consonant
fewer at each step).

## 6. The reason and its ground, tab by tab

Each caption and each reason was taken a sentence at a time.

**Whose work: Verbs.** Sentences 1 and 2 stand on the text (lines 2315 to
2337: Brangäne calls Isolde's shame her own work; the answer begins with the
two words of the caption). Sentences 3 to 6 stand on the marks. Fault: "the
short brackets mark the verbs, ten in all" was broken by the infinitive at
25.0 w4, a verb that is not marked. Now "the verb of each clause, ten". Fault
in the reason: its first words said that Isolde's sentences hand the deed
over, where rows 14.0 and 15.0 say it was taken out of her power. Cut; the
reason now begins with the count. The subject of rows 16.0 and 17.0 could by
form be either word; the next clause has no new subject and the English makes
Lady Love the taker, as the ground says. The forms (two present subjunctives
at 22.0 w3 and 23.0 w2): DWDS and English Wikipedia, "Subjunctive mood". Auden
(Prose II, pp. 139 to 140) says what the caption says, of Tristan's tie to
Isolde. The reason holds: her own verbs are past, what may come is another's.
One thing more, from the page images read at the end: the two verbs of rows
22.0 and 23.0 have these forms in -e in the vocal score too (p. 112, with
"may" in the English under them), but the poem as Wagner printed it in 1873
gives them the ending of the two verbs before them (vol. 7, p. 50). So "what
may come" is true of the words as sung, which the German shown follows, and
not of the poem as first printed. A source ground records it; nothing in the
caption had to change.

**Whose work: Rhyme.** Fault: "the rhyme comes back with another owner". The
end words of rows 12.0 and 13.0 do not rhyme with each other; each comes back
six lines later. The clause is cut and the reason says "end words". Fault: the
caption said Auden set the speech's opening "over his pages on these lovers";
the section is on Don Giovanni and his servant as well, and on these lovers
with their servants. Now "on these lovers and their servants". His sentence on
the exchange of potions (p. 122) is given as he has it: bound from outside,
not by their own decisions, and not answerable.

**Whose work: Words.** Fault: Auden's second belief was given as "taught to
give the self to God absolutely". He says the dedication is an act of free
choice and an absolute commitment, and he says it of both myths. The caption
now has both words and "could only arise where people are taught", and the
ground says it is said of both myths and not of this speech. The page is 25
(the printed note said 23 and 25). The sentence that the English under the
glosses was made to be sung was cut to make room; the translators are named
in the last passage and in the sources. "Earlier in the speech Love is a
queen, with life and death her subjects": rows 4.0 to 9.0 of the German part
say so (read again). Gehorsam: see section 4.

**Two names: Crossing.** The marks, the figure, the small word Isolde praises
earlier (lines 3361 to 3403, which the hosted English leaves out: searched,
not there) and Pearce, pp. 137 to 138, stand as written. Two Auden grounds
that no sentence of the caption used (Prose II, p. 138; The Dyer's Hand,
p. 121) were taken out of the grounds list; both remain in the Auden entries.

Fault: the caption said flatly who sings which lines. The page does not print
the singers' names (the part was cut without the three headings at source
file lines 3619, 3627 and 3635); the 1906 Textbuch prints Tristan's three
lines first; and the score has them otherwise again. I read the close of the
duet in the page images of the vocal score (Internet Archive item
tristanisolda00wagn, printed pp. 173 to 178, leaves 184 to 189 of the scan;
kept in `tristan-exam/scorepages`). Two voice staves stand over the piano; on
p. 178 they carry the singers' names, hers above. On p. 174 the upper voice
begins the lines of names in the second bar; the lower answers a bar later,
and so with each line: his second begins in the bar where hers ends, his
third as her last syllable sounds. His words are the Textbuch's: name then
pronoun, pronoun then name. The two voices sing one word of the six lines at
the same moment, the word for I (second system, third bar). The caption now
begins: this German text gives Isolde the first three lines and Tristan
the next three, and in the vocal score he answers each as it ends. It says
"this German text" and not "the German libretto", since the libretto of 1906
has the lines the other way. A source ground gives the score. To make room
the sentence from Pearce was shortened without change of sense. "He does the
same" holds in either wording: each calls the other by the singer's own name
and then drops it.

Last of all I read the Textbuch's own page in the page image (Internet
Archive item tristanundisolde00wagn_0, printed p. 62, leaf 65 of the scan;
kept in `tristan-exam/tb1906`). The scan's text had hidden one thing: a
single brace joins Tristan's three lines and Isolde's three under them. I
take that as the way a libretto marks lines sung at the same time (my
reading of the sign; I opened no handbook on it), and it is near what the
score has. So the Textbuch does not say that he sings his lines before hers.
The ground now gives the brace and the page, and the printed note says that
the libretto of 1906 "prints his lines first, bracketed with hers" where it
said "gives Tristan his lines first".

**Two names: Grammar.** Fault: "so the lines have no doer and no time in
them; they name a state" with the two words of row 10.0 as its example. Those
two words are about time, so "no time in them" was false as said, and "they
name a state" stood on no ground. Now: a verb in that form shows no person
and no tense (the handbook fact), and the lines set phrase beside phrase,
with two words of like meaning in row 10.0 (the marks; DWDS under endlos and
ewig). That is also what Auden's sentence of 1969 is about, so the caption now
arrives at him. The count: sixteen words in rows 6.0 to 13.0, sixteen marks,
no finite verb (the participle at 12.0 w1 is used as an adjective and is
marked so). Also mended: "the eight lines both sing" is now "the last eight
lines", in the caption and in the reason. Who sings them is no part of this
tab's reason, and in the score the two do not sing them together (see the
next tab).

**Two names: Rhyme.** Fault, the most serious found: a ground said that in
both librettos, the 1971 text and the 1906 Textbuch, each name ends two lines
of one singer and no line of the other's, so that the word for I is the one
line end they share. I read lines 5513 to 5535 of the Textbuch scan's text
(scratchpad `tristan-poet/tb1906_djvu.txt`, and at the end the page image of
its p. 62): there Tristan's three lines come first and end on the pronoun of
address, on her name and on his name; hers follow as printed here, the two
speeches joined by a brace. In that wording the word for I ends one line only
(hers), and the word the two singers share at a line's end is her name. So
the caption's claim holds for the 1971 text alone. The vocal score, read
afterwards (see Crossing), has the Textbuch's words for Tristan's lines. The
caption now says "In this German text" and the reason "As printed"; the
ground says what the Textbuch and the score have; caveat 3 tells the reader;
`open` asks the lead whether to keep this German text or cut the part again
from one that follows the score. Also: "what such lovers want" became "these
two want" (Auden's sentence, p. 24, is about Tristan and Isolde).

Second fault, found in the score. The caption said that both then sing four
lines on a single rhyme, and the reason spoke of "the lines both sing". The
German text shown does head rows 6.0 to 13.0 for both (line 3635), and the
English beside it heads the whole close so (act-2, block 121). In the vocal
score (pp. 175 to 178) the upper voice alone sings rows 6.0 to 9.0, while the
lower holds the two words of row 10.0 under her; he never sings those four
lines. The two then sing rows 10.0 and 11.0 apart, he ahead of her. She sings
row 12.0 while he rests, and he row 13.0 with her silent until his last note;
then he sings row 12.0 and she row 13.0. Only in the last four bars before
Scene 3 do the two sing a line at the same time: row 13.0, their syllables
together from its second syllable on, the written notes of the two staves the
same for nearly three bars, and both hold its last syllable into the first
bar of Scene 3. This is what Pearce reports Auden telling the class before he
played the record: the voices kept from unison until the duet's end. The
score bears the report out, and the caption can now show it on the passage:
the libretto gives the eight lines to both, the score gives four of them to
Isolde alone and joins the voices only in the last line. Caption and reason
are rewritten to say so, with a source ground for the score. The sentence of
1948 (these two want two souls made one) moved from the caption to the
reason; its ground still serves.

Third, a thing the page shows that the reading did not say: the English
printed beside this passage has four lines of names, given to both singers,
where the German has six (act-2, block 122, rows 28 to 31, under the heading
of block 121), so twelve English lines stand beside fourteen German. This is
the form of the poem as Wagner printed it. I read the close of the second act
in the page images of his collected writings (Gesammelte Schriften und
Dichtungen, vol. 7, Leipzig: E. W. Fritzsch, 1873, pp. 70 and 71; Internet
Archive item 11673506bsb, leaves 73 and 74; the title page is leaf 0): one
heading, Beide, stands over the whole close, and the names have four lines,
the words of rows 0.0 and 1.0 and then a line that drops his name and a line
that drops hers; the eight lines of rows 6.0 to 13.0 follow as shown. So
three forms of these lines exist: the poem's four lines for both, which the
English libretto follows; the six lines of the vocal score and the 1906
Textbuch; and the six lines of the 1971 text shown, whose wording of
Tristan's two lines agrees with neither. The vocal score's own English under
the notes has six lines, as its German has. Caveat 3 now tells the reader
that the poem as printed in 1873 and the English libretto have four lines of
names for both; a source ground and a line in the sources give the 1873
volume; `open` gives the lead the rest.

**Two names: Words.** Tristan's sentence earlier in the scene (lines 3405 to
3419) is in the past tense in the German and a wish in the hosted English
(act-2, block 76, which has no word for nameless); the caption says "speaks
of". Auden (p. 122) says that yielding would bring the two to know something
about each other; "a little" was the poet's shading and is now "something of
each other". The guess about the compound at 11.0 is cut (section 4).

**The last twelve lines: Sentence.** Faults: only one sense of sollen; "The
one who asks is not named again", which repeats the sentence before it; and
"Tristan is his case". Auden does not say in so many words that Tristan is the
instance of the second flight of Eros: the sentence on Tristan follows it as
the sentence on Don Juan follows the first. The caption now says only that
his next sentence is about Tristan, and says Eros, his word, where it said
love. The ground on the stage directions went out with the sentence it
served, and the link to them with it. The marks: one finite verb with its
subject (43.0 w0 and w1), the pronoun at 46.0 w0, five infinitives, four
phrases that begin with the same preposition (three after row 46.0), no
pronoun and no finite verb in rows 47.0 to 54.0. The 1906 Textbuch punctuates
the twelve lines as the 1971 text does (read at about lines 9040 to 9062 of
its scan text, and then in the page image of its printed p. 96, leaf 99 of
the scan: the same two question marks, the same commas and the last
exclamation mark, with comma and dash in the other order after rows 50.0,
52.0 and 53.0). The vocal score and the poem of 1873 have a comma for the
first of the two question marks (row 44.0); the second, at row 46.0, on
which this caption and the Beat caption lean, is in all four.

**The last twelve lines: Rhyme.** Faults: "one word growing shorter" (they
are three words; what the page shows is a consonant fewer at each step);
"there aware of you alone" turned the Act 1 line round: its pronoun is a
dative, known to me (Grimm under bewust, sense 1); and "Her end can so be

first two are reworded, the third cut. The same rhyme at the end of the first
lines both sing in Act 1 (source file lines 1967 and 1969) and at the end of
the duet (3647 to 3651), and the last line without the word for love (5793):
read again. Auden's Nirvana: p. 122. After the score was read, one more

lines given to both lovers in Act 1", in the caption and in the link of the
printed note. The libretto heads those lines for both (line 1939, the first
such heading in the poem; act-1, block 334 in the English); how the two sing
them I did not look up in the score, and the caption no longer implies it.

**The last twelve lines: Beat.** Fault: "The change can be heard as the place
where asking stops and sinking begins" stood on no ground (the verbs for
sinking come four lines later). Cut. In its place a fact of the marks: the
two long lines are the ones that end on the words for surge and sound, with a
dictionary ground for the two senses. Auden (p. 473) on what decides the
vocal line is as he has it. Read at the end, in page images: the vocal score
(pp. 274 to 276) has the twelve lines as shown, but the poem as Wagner
printed it in 1873 (vol. 7, pp. 111 to 112) has four lines where rows 47.0
and 48.0 stand: before each of the two nouns comes a line that says whose
surge and whose sound it is. The two lines of six syllables are therefore
the form Wagner gave the words when he set them. Every count in the caption
is true of the text shown, and the printed note says the syllables are
counted "in the German printed here"; the first line of the sources now says
that the text is close to the words as set to music. That Wagner cut his own
lines here bears on Auden's sentence about syllables and the vocal line; I
left it for the lead in `open` and did not write it into the caption. The
score also shows where the measure changes: one bar in 2/4 is set in at the
third word of row 47.0, and 4/4 is marked again at that line's rhyme word
(p. 275, first two systems). The caption's "After the last question mark the
measure changes" is said of the words, and is true of them; that the music
changes its bar at the same place is in `open` for the lead, since the page
shows no music.

**The last twelve lines: English.** Faults: "A kiss needs a second person"
is a general remark on no ground; "draws the scents in": the English has no
word for scents, and what its singer wins to her breathing is the breezes of
p:265.36; and Auden's reason was left out, with his indicative turned into a
conditional. Now: the last eight German lines name no kiss and no person (the
marks); breezes; and Auden's two steps kept apart, in the caption's own words:

two syllables, a word and a pronoun against a preposition and the same

the Breitkopf vocal score (Internet Archive item tristanisolda00wagn), read
first in the scan's text and then in the page image (leaf 4: the vocal score

vocal score" is now seen and not inferred: in the page images of its last
