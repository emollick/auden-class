# Augustine, Confessions: the poet's notes

Piece `augustine`. Text shown: E. B. Pusey's translation of 1838, as hosted
(`augustine/book-01` to `book-13`). Three plates, twelve lenses.

`node build/tools/check-reading.mjs augustine` ends with "no problems" and
"no warnings" (last run after the last edit). No warning is left to answer.
The text is prose in translation, so there is no beat or rhyme lens and no
scansion to defend.

## 1. The point of view

Auden's view, as `view` gives it, stands on two places.

1. The lecture on Othello in *Lectures on Shakespeare* (ed. Kirsch, 2000).
   Research notes C give the sentence (section 5, and the appendix on the
   lectures): Iago is an example of the idea of the gratuitous act, the idea
   is foreign to Greek thought, and it first comes up in the Confessions in
   the episode of the pear tree. He then quotes the passage. His words
   cover that idea and that episode. They do not cover the rest of the
   Confessions, and no caption makes them.
   The page is not in the research notes. I took 197 from the editor's notes
   at the back of the book, seen in full-text snippets of the archive.org
   copy (`lecturesonshakes00aude`): the note on the words about the pear-tree
   episode is keyed to 197 and cites Pusey's translation in the Dent edition
   of 1932, pp. 25-26. So the English the editor prints there is Pusey's,
   the translation hosted here. The book was not read in a full copy; this
   is in `open`. The editor's note on the French term, just before that
   one, sends the reader to *Forewords and Afterwords* 36-37, "Squares and
   Oblongs" 167-68 and *The Dyer's Hand* 430-31. Two of those three are the
   pages this reading uses, found here independently in the scans.
2. "Augustus to Augustine" (1944), in *Forewords and Afterwords*, read in
   the scan `fa.txt`, lines 1725 to 1804. In this scan the page number
   stands at the foot of its page (36 is at line 1777), so the sentence on
   love as a relation that goes both ways begins at the foot of p. 35 and
   ends on p. 36; nothing evil in matter, man as created and fallen, and
   flesh and spirit are p. 36; the sentence on ethics begins on the last
   line of p. 36 and runs onto p. 37, where the sentence in `view.quote`
   also stands (23 words, checked letter by letter against the scan, with
   the scanner's slips set right). The essay spells the French term with a
   final e. The lecture spells it without, and the captions follow the
   lecture.

Auden says nothing in these sources about the first paragraph of Book I or
about X.27. Where a caption on plate 1 or plate 3 brings him in, it says
"Auden's summary" or "Auden, setting out the doctrine Augustine held", and
the ground gives the page. Those sentences of his set out the Christian
doctrines that Augustine put against the classical ones (the essay is a
review of Cochrane's book). They are not about these paragraphs, and the
captions do not say they are.

The plates follow his view in order: what man is and wants on the first
page; the act done for nothing and loved for itself; the same verb, *amavi*,
said to God.

The piece carries two quotations from work in copyright, the limit: the
sentence in `view.quote`, and seven words from Pearce in the first `auden`
entry. Everything else of Auden's is paraphrase.

## 2. Plate by plate

### Plate 1, `opening`: Book I, block 0, words 0 to 84

Why this passage. In 85 words it holds the address to God, the borrowed
psalm, and the sentence on the restless heart. Three of the leads in
pieces.json meet here (the book as apostrophe, the opening paragraph, the
Psalms woven in). The plate stops at word 84 because the paragraph then
turns to a run of questions, which is another subject. The first *me*
comes at word 86, so "no *I* yet" is true of the plate and only just.

- **Address** (figure, apostrophe). Thirteen words for Thou, counted in the
  proof: Thou at 2, 45, 61, 70; Thy at 12, 15, 28, 59, 67; Thee at 19, 53,
  84; Thyself at 74. The term is as Silva Rhetoricae defines it (speech
  turned from one audience to another). It fits because Augustine himself
  says his hearers are his own kind and his speech is before God (II.3.5,
  block 4 of book-02), and says why he tells God what God knows (XI.1.1,
  block 0 of book-11). A reader who holds that apostrophe must be a turn
  inside a speech may prefer "address"; the tab already says Address. The
  psalm verse he names in XI.1.1 has the same words as the verse Book I
  begins with (the Latin margin there gives Ps. 95, 4, which is 96:4 in
  the King James numbering; the words are those of 145:3 too).
- **Build** (sentence). Pusey's Latin edition prints the scripture words in
  italics and names the verses in its margin: Ps. 144, 3 and 146, 5 (145:3
  and 147:5 in the King James numbering), James 4:6, 1 Peter 5:5. In the
  psalms the words are said of the Lord in the third person; I read both
  verses in the King James Bible. The wish is said twice in exactly the
  same Latin words (*laudare te vult homo*); Pusey keeps the four English
  words and changes their order. The comparison with Auden says "two parts
  like these", not "the same", because his terms (fallen creature; spirit)
  are doctrine and the sentence's are plainer.
- **Word**. *Inquietum*, *requiescat*, *ad te*, *excitas*: the Latin is
  I.1.1 in Pusey's Latin edition; the senses are Lewis and Short's
  (inquietus: restless, unquiet; requiesco: to rest, repose; quies: rest,
  quiet; ad: first, the direction toward an object; excito: to wake or
  rouse up). "Not at rest" and "comes to rest" are my plain renderings, and
  the glosses under the words carry the dictionary's own words beside them.
  Pusey takes *ad te* as "for Thyself"; "toward" is the dictionary's first
  sense, and both readings are in `open`. In the Latin, *excitas* has no
  object; the *us* of the plate is Pusey's. *Excito* at XI.1.1 is what
  Pusey turns as "stir up".
- **Whole** (shape). All numbers are counted (section 5 below). The plan is
  Augustine's own, from Retractations II.6, which Pusey's Latin edition
  prints at its head and his preface to the translation quotes. The return
  of the rest: *requiescat in te* (I.1.1), *requiescamus in te* (XIII.36.51,
  block 49 of book-13, the third paragraph from the end, where Pusey has
  "rest in Thee" and the verb is future), *tua quies tu ipse es* (XIII.38.53,
  block 51, the last paragraph). An earlier draft said the rest is reached
  "only" on the last pages. That was a general statement I could not
  prove, so the caption now says only that the words return there.

### Plate 2, `pears`: Book II, block 8, words 189 to 260

Why this passage. Auden's view is about this episode, and the lecture as
printed quotes it in Pusey's words: archive.org snippets of the book show
words 197 to 233 of this paragraph inside the quotation (from *it sought
there* to *not that for*), so most of the plate is what the editor has
Auden read out. How far the quotation runs past that I could not see, and
its extent is the editor's reconstruction, so no caption says that Auden
read these lines. The story of the
theft is 78 words of plain telling (words 91 to 168) and has little to mark.
The 72 words chosen are where Augustine says what his heart sought, and
they hold the word *gratuitously*, the three clauses that end in *itself*,
and the three *I loved*.

- **Itself** (figure, antithesis). Each pair sets something beyond the deed,
  denied, against the deed. Silva Rhetoricae: contrasting words or ideas
  set side by side, often in parallel structure. The noun that comes back
  in a new form is polyptoton by the same handbook; one figure to a lens,
  so the caption describes the returning noun without naming it. Whose
  craft: the three pairs and the returning nouns are Augustine's (*malitiae*
  and *malitia*; *deficiebam* and *defectum*; *dedecore* and *dedecus*). The
  Latin joins the first pair with *nisi* (except) and the other two with
  *sed* (but); Pusey's *but* serves for both. *Ipsum* stands once in the
  Latin, and Pusey has *itself* three times. Pusey's *temptation* is
  *causa*, and the Latin of the next chapter uses *causa* four times more
  (II.5.11), under the edition's heading *Neminem peccare sine causa*. That
  heading is the edition's, not Augustine's, and the note says so.
- **Loved** (figure, anaphora). *Amavi* comes four times in the Latin; the
  fourth closes its clause and Pusey drops it. The link to plate 3 is the
  same word in the same tense at X.27.38.
- **Word**. *Gratis*, *causa*, *foeda*, *defectus*, *firmamentum*, *dedecus*,
  all from II.4.9 in the Latin, senses from Lewis and Short. *Gratis* is the
  contracted ablative plural of *gratia* (Lewis and Short under gratia; the
  Online Etymology Dictionary under gratis), and *gratuitus* is formed from
  *gratia* (Lewis and Short). The pairing of *gratia* with *gratuitum* is at
  II.7.15 (block 14), where Augustine also says that all is forgiven him.
  Under gratuitus Lewis and Short cite the sentence of Sallust (Catiline
  16.3) that Augustine quotes at II.5.11. English *gratuitously* in the
  sense "without cause" dates from the 1690s (Online Etymology Dictionary),
  so it was current for Pusey. "Was nothing" is II.8.16 (block 15).
- **Book** (shape). Counts in section 5. Pearce's page is a student's
  notes; it names Augustine and not the Confessions (in `open`). Its
  sentence is about the conflict of self and ego as Augustine gives it, and
  it names no book. The caption sets it beside the count and does not say
  that Auden meant Book II. The page is 134, not 133: see section 5. The
  likeness between Augustine's guess at II.6.14 (block 13: a prisoner
  mimicking freedom by doing unpunished what was forbidden) and Auden's
  reason for criminal gratuitous acts (*The Dyer's Hand*, p. 430) is my
  comparison of two things each of which can be read; the caption says "a
  like reason" and claims no more. See section 4 for a place where Auden
  makes the link himself, which the rules kept out of the reading.

### Plate 3, `late`: Book X, block 37, the whole paragraph

Why this passage. It is the lead "Too late loved I Thee", and it answers
plate 2 with the same verb. The paragraph has 112 words, two more than the
guide's 110. I kept it whole: it is one movement from *too late* to
*peace*, and the only sentence that could go (words 54 to 69) is the one
that says the things are nothing apart from God, which the Word caption
leans on. The checker warns above 130.

- **Word**. *Sero*, *amavi*, *intus*, *foris*, *formosa*, *deformis*: X.27.38
  in the Latin, senses from Lewis and Short (serus, with the adverb sero:
  late, too late; intus: on the inside; foris: out of doors, abroad,
  opposed to intus; formosus: finely formed, beautiful; deformis: departing
  from the right shape, misshapen). Pusey's *abroad* in the sense "out of
  the house" is sense 2 in Webster (1828).
- **Crossing** (figure, chiasmus). Thou, me, I, Thee. Chiasmus and not
  antimetabole, by Silva Rhetoricae's distinction, because the words change
  form. The Latin crosses too (*mecum eras, et tecum non eram*), with the
  persons the other way about. "Not a matter of place" is X.26.37 (block
  36).
- **Senses** (figure, enumeration). Five sentences, one sense each. The
  earlier list is X.6.8 (block 7), thirty paragraphs before, with sight
  first and with the sentence on God's word striking his heart. The caption
  sets the two facts side by side and does not say that the one causes the
  other.
- **Verbs** (sentence). The six verbs of the first two sentences all end in
  *-st* (in the Latin, *-sti*). The present tense of *hunger* and *thirst*
  is *esurio* and *sitio*. See `open` on *panted*: Pusey's first edition
  and the Latin (*anhelo*) have the present there too, and the caption does
  not lean on that word. The prayer is X.29.40 (block 39), and again at
  blocks 44 and 59. Block 38 between them also prays, so the caption says
  "two paragraphs later", not "his next prayer".
