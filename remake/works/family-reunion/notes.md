# The Family Reunion: the poet's notes

Piece `family-reunion`, written on 2026-10-03. Handed in: `reading.json`,
this file, and `count.mjs`, a short script that makes every count in the
reading again from a scan of the play and prints numbers only.

No line of the play and no sentence of Eliot's or Auden's prose is copied
in this file. Places are given by page, and what stands there is said in
my words. The two lines the piece quotes are in `reading.json` only (the
Line tab: p. 126, 7 words; p. 78, 8 words). Page numbers of the play are
those of the New York edition (Harcourt, Brace, 1939), the edition on
Auden's list.

A poet had this piece before me and was stopped. I treated that draft as
someone else's work. Section 9 says what was kept and what was cut.

The checker, run last: no problems, no warnings, the printed notes fit to
print (also with `--print`). There is no warning to answer.

## 1. Why one page of four drawings, and why these four

The play is in copyright in the United States through 2034
(`remake/research/D_texts.md`), so no passage can be set on the page. The
piece has one plate marked `noText` and four shape lenses. They are drawn
from facts: counts, names, pages, the order of the scenes, who sees what.

Auden left two judgments of the play (section 2), and the piece is built
on them. The verse is the gain: Eliot went far toward a verse for the
modern stage, though Auden could not hear the three stresses Eliot
counted. The convention is the fault: the theme is spiritual election, and
a convention taken over from English high comedy is bound to make the
election look like a matter of brains. The other two tabs take the two
things Eliot himself said he built into the play, the chorus and the Greek
story, and set beside each what Auden said.

- **Line.** Eliot's rule for his line, beside two lines of the play with
  the stresses of speech marked. It answers Auden's first judgment, and it
  shows his dissent on a line he tested himself.
- **Chorus.** The six scenes and the five places where two aunts and two
  uncles speak as one. Eliot's reason for the device, and his later
  verdict on it as Auden reports it.
- **Greek.** The story of Orestes laid beside the play: four things kept,
  four changed. Where Aeschylus has a trial the play has a calling, which
  is where Auden's Orestes (acquitted, unrepentant) and Auden's Harry
  (choosing a way of life) part.
- **Sight.** Who sees the Eumenides and who does not, Harry's word
  *election*, and what the play says of John. This is the ground of
  Auden's chief complaint.

### Why these two lines

The plate cannot show a passage, so the choice of passage comes down to
the two lines quoted in the Line tab.

- **p. 126, Amy's voice from the next room.** It is the last thing she
  says. The play prepares it: on p. 15 she says that this is the thing she
  does not want, and the tenth line of her first speech (p. 11) ends on
  the same three words. It is seven words of one syllable each, so there
  is no doubt where the syllables are. Three of them carry the stress (the
  noun, the verb, the last noun), two before the pause and one after. It
  keeps Eliot's rule with no strain, and it is short, so that beside the
  other it shows what a line of no fixed length looks like.
- **p. 78, Harry to the doctor.** This is Auden's own test. He opened the
  play at random, landed on this page, and said he could read none of the
  lines with fewer than four stresses and that his third sounded like
  five. The line quoted is the one he appears to mean (section 4 gives the
  reasoning and the doubt).

### The quotation budget

Two quotations are allowed in the piece and both are spent on these two
lines. Auden and Eliot are paraphrased everywhere; no `quote` field is
used under `view` or `auden`. Besides the two lines the reading names
single words and four short phrases of the play (*election*; *hunters*,
*pursuers*, *spectres*, *bright angels*, *ghosts*; *Eumenides*; *sin and
expiation*; *crime and punishment*; *ill at ease*). They are listed in `open` for the lead in case
they are to count as quotation.

## 3. The tabs one by one: the reason, and the ground of each sentence

`grounds` in the reading lists these with their sources. Here each
sentence of each caption is named with its ground.

### Line

Reason (`why`): Eliot made a line of three stresses and no fixed length so
that verse could sound like the speech of his day. Auden thought the verse
a real advance and heard four stresses where Eliot counted three.

1. No fixed length; the first speech runs from 8 to 16 syllables. *Source*:
   Eliot, *Poetry and Drama*, p. 27. *Plate*: the count (section 6).
2. Three stresses and one pause, a caesura, dividing them two and one.
   *Source*: the same page. The plain-word glosses stand on Baum's
   glossary: caesura, the classical term for a pause; stress, the emphasis
   that sets one sound off from others; accent, the emphasis normal speech
   puts on one syllable of a word.
3. He wanted the rhythm of the speech of his day, for easy talk and
   intense speech alike. *Source*: pp. 27 and 31 (and p. 25, where he
   says he wanted to do without prose).
4. Amy's last line keeps the rule. *Text*: p. 126, her last speech.
   *Plate*: three stresses, two before the pause and one after.
5. The second line is from the passage Auden opened at random. *Auden*:
   *Prose* III, p. 258. *Text*: the lines he quotes are on p. 78.
6. Four accented words, and a fifth stress may fall on *can*. *Plate*: the
   marks. The four are the four words of two syllables, each with its
   dictionary accent on the first.
7. Auden could read no line there with fewer than four, and heard five in
   what appears to be this one. *Auden*: p. 258. "Appears" marks the
   inference (section 4).

One thing I took care not to say. On p. 24 of the lecture Eliot says he
wanted nothing in it to sound like Shakespeare and that strict blank verse had
drifted far from modern speech. He says it of *Murder in the Cathedral*,
not of this play, so the tab does not set this line against blank verse.

### Chorus

Reason: Eliot wanted the chorus to be more a part of the play, so he gave
it to four of its persons. Together they say they are afraid and lost, and
alone each speaks of private concerns or of the others' faults.

1. Two aunts and two uncles, with parts of their own; five places.
   *Text*: the list of Persons and the heading of the first chorus, p. 21,
   which names the four. *Plate*: the five places.
2. At all but the fourth, each of them then says a line alone. *Plate
   and text*: pp. 22, 42, 67 and 129; at pp. 93–94 no one speaks apart and
   the four go out.
3. Together: ill at ease (p. 21), afraid (p. 66), lost (p. 128). *Text*.
   The third chorus speaks as "I"; the others say "we".
4. Alone: the vicar's tea (Violet, p. 22), one another's faults (p. 42,
   where the four accusations make a ring: Ivy of Charles, Gerald of Ivy,
   Violet of Gerald, Charles of Violet), a ticket to London (Ivy,
   p. 129). *Text*.
5. After *Murder in the Cathedral* Eliot resolved to bring the chorus
   further inside the play. *Source*: *Poetry and Drama*, p. 25.
6. By Auden's report he later found the device more artificial than an
   outright chorus. *Auden*: p. 258. Eliot's own words for it are on p. 28
   of the lecture: not very satisfactory, too much to ask of actors, a
   trick of use in no other play.
7. The two scenes without a chorus are those where Harry's pursuers, the
   Eumenides, are shown. *Text*: stage directions on pp. 60 and 106; the four leave
   before each of those scenes (pp. 44 and 94) and none speaks in either.
   The caption gives this as a fact and draws nothing from it.

### Greek

Reason: Eliot kept the Greek frame and put a calling where the trial was.
The play calls its story one of sin and expiation, not of crime and
punishment (Agatha, p. 101). He later judged the two stories ill fitted.
The `why` sets the play's own words beside the change and does not claim
to know Eliot's motive for it.

1. Under the play lies the story of Orestes. *Source*: Morshead's
   Aeschylus; Eliot names Aeschylus as his source on p. 30 of the lecture;
   the play's own list of persons ends with the Eumenides, and its fourth
   chorus names Argos (p. 94).
2. Kept: a cursed house, a son come home, pursuers that he sees and those
   about him do not. *Source*: the end of *The Libation-Bearers* (Orestes
   tells the women that they cannot see them and he can; the closing
   chorus counts three storms on the house of Atreus), and Cassandra in
   the hosted *Agamemnon* (`agamemnon/p5`, block 75). *Text*: pp. 23–24,
   and Agatha on the curse, pp. 106–07.
3. The killings become a murder planned and stopped (p. 100) and a push
   that Harry thinks he may have dreamed (pp. 29 and 101). *Source* for
   the Greek killings; *text* for the play.
4. Aeschylus ends with a court that frees Orestes. *Source*: *The Furies*,
   Athena's casting vote and the equal count.
5. Agatha: a story of sin and expiation, of a sin perhaps not his own; he
   turns and follows. *Text*: pp. 101, 106, 110–11. *Expiation* is glossed
   in the caption as amends, from the Online Etymology Dictionary (the
   making of satisfaction or reparation for an offense; atonement).
6. Auden: Orestes is acquitted without repenting; Harry chooses his way of
   life. *Auden*: *The Enchafèd Flood*, pp. 109–10; *Secondary Worlds*,
   p. 11. The first is not said of Eliot's play, and the entry under
   `auden` says so.
7. Eliot later thought the two stories ill fitted. *Source*: *Poetry and
   Drama*, p. 30.

The name. Liddell and Scott give *Eumenides* as the gracious goddesses, a
euphemism for the Erinyes or Furies, and as the title of Aeschylus's play.
In the play the name stands in the list of persons and in three stage
directions (p. 60 once, p. 106 twice). No one speaks it, and the word
Furies is nowhere in the book. (The common noun *fury* is spoken once, by
Amy on p. 114, of something else.) Eliot himself calls them the Furies in
the lecture, p. 30.

### Sight

Reason: only some of the house see the Eumenides, and Harry follows them.
Auden names the theme spiritual election, which the comedy convention is
bound to make seem a matter of brains, against Eliot's meaning.

1. Harry sees his pursuers at the window. *Text*: he halts in the doorway,
   looking hard at the window (direction, p. 23) and says he sees them (p. 24);
   they are shown in the window on pp. 60 and 106.
2. So do Agatha, Mary and the chauffeur. *Text*: Agatha says she has seen
   them and Mary's answer shows that she has too (p. 117); Agatha tells
   Downing that she and Mary have seen them, and Downing says he saw them
   before his master and has grown used to them (p. 125). Mary calls
   herself a cousin on p. 52; in the list of persons she is the daughter
   of a cousin of Amy's.
3. Amy and the four of the chorus do not; the brothers never arrive.
   *Text*: Harry tells the assembled family that they do not see them and
   Gerald sees no one (p. 24); Amy says no one is there but the family
   (p. 110); John's crash (p. 81), Arthur's telephone call and the
   newspaper report (pp. 89–92).
4. Harry speaks of his election, his being chosen, and does not
   understand it. *Text*: p. 111. The gloss stands on the Online Etymology
   Dictionary, entry *election* (the theological sense, God's choice of
   someone, from the late fourteenth century), and on Agatha's word to him
   on p. 102, that he is chosen.
5. John, who gets the house, is called steady (the doctor, p. 77), slow
   (Ivy, p. 87) and hardly conscious (Harry, p. 84). *Text*; that John is
   to be master is on pp. 111 and 118.
6. to 8. Auden on the convention. *Auden*: *Prose* III, p. 355.

Nothing is said in the play of whether the doctor, the sergeant or the
parlourmaid see them, and the facts list the three under `notSaid`.

At their first showing (p. 60) Mary tells Harry that no one is there and
draws the curtains (p. 61). Her later words to Agatha (p. 117) are why she
is counted among those who see.

## 4. The two scansions, and the other ways of hearing them

Eliot scans no line of the play in the lecture; he gives the rule. Auden
gives counts and no positions. The marks are mine. Words are numbered
from 0.

**p. 126 (seven words, seven syllables).** Marked: stress on words 1, 3
and 6; pause after word 3. The other way: the pause after word 1, which
puts one stress before it and two after. The rule is kept either way.
I hear no fourth stress in it.

**p. 78 (eight words, twelve syllables).** The pause is not in doubt: a
full stop stands after word 4. Marked: stress on the first syllable of
words 1, 4, 5 and 7, the four words of two syllables. Other ways of
hearing it:

- A fifth stress on word 6 (*can*). The doctor has just hoped that nothing
  has happened, and Harry's answer turns on that word. This is the reading
  the caption names, and it gives Auden's five.
- A fifth on word 3 (*my*) in place of word 6, set against the doctor's
  *your* in the line before. Not marked.
- A speaker who leans on word 6 may let word 7 go light, since that verb
  has just been said twice. That gives four by another route (1, 4, 5, 6).
- Three stresses, as Eliot's rule asks, can be had only by passing lightly
  over one of the four accented words. That is how his count could be
  defended, and it is what Auden says he cannot do.

So the caption claims only what holds on every hearing: four accented
words, and a fifth stress that may fall on *can*.

**Which line Auden calls his third.** He quotes the doctor's two lines and
Harry's answer down to the sentence about the parlourmaid. On p. 78 the
doctor's second line is short and Harry's first words are set far to the
right: the two are the halves of one verse line. Counted so, the lines he
quotes are four, and the third is the line quoted in the piece. Counted
as five printed lines, the third would be Harry's four opening words,
which cannot hold five stresses, and the doctor's short line could not
hold four. So he must be counting verse lines. This is an inference: the
snippets do not show how the review sets the lines out. It is said as
such in the caption ("appears to be"), in `facts.auden.note`, in a caveat
and in `open`.

## 6. The counts, and how to make them again

`count.mjs` in this folder does all of it and prints numbers only:

    curl -sL -o <scratch>/fr.xml https://archive.org/download/familyreunionpl00elio/familyreunionpl00elio_djvu.xml
    node remake/works/family-reunion/count.mjs <scratch>/fr.xml

It cuts the scan into its printed lines (3346 entries for pp. 11 to 131),
classes each by its distance from the margin (a verse line, the
turned-over end of a line, a part line set further in, a speaker's name,
a folio), and then counts. The entry numbers of the five chorus passages
were fixed by reading the scan page by page; they hold only for this file,
and the script says so if the number of entries changes. It also tells
whether each count is the one in the reading.

- **The chorus.** Words spoken together (the seven speeches headed Chorus
  and the one line headed All) and apart (the single speeches of the four
  that stand between or after them): 86 and 60; 211 and 53; 113 and 56;
  220 and 0; 286 and 71. In all 916 and 240. Lines that turn over on the
  page are joined, a word split at a line end is closed up, pencil marks
  in the scanned copy are dropped, and a hyphenated name counts as one
  word.
- **The first speech.** Twelve lines; syllables 12, 12, 13, 14, 14, 16,
  13, 11, 11, 8, 11, 13, by the site's syllable tool
  (`site/js/kit/syllables.js`) and then by my own count of each line. The
  two agree. *Fire* in the last line is taken as one syllable; as two the
  line would have 14, and the range of 8 to 16 would stand.
- **The two quoted lines.** 7 words and 7 syllables; 8 words and 12
  syllables.
- **The scenes.** First pages 11, 45, 62, 71, 95, 112; last page 131.
- **The name.** Eumenides four times in the book: the list of persons and
  three stage directions (pp. 60, 106, 106). Furies: never.

## 7. How Auden's reviews were read

*Prose* III and *Secondary Worlds* are restricted scans at the Internet
Archive. Their text can be searched but not opened, and a search returns a
snippet of some twenty words round the phrase. I chained snippets: each
query was a few words from the end of the last snippet. No query held more
than six words of a sentence. The service is

    https://archive.org/services/search/beta/page_production/

with `service_backend=fts`, a quoted `user_query`, and
`filter_map={"identifier":{"<id>":"inc"}}`. It sometimes answers with no
body and can hang, so run it under a time limit. What this method cannot
give: the full text in order, the layout of quoted verse, and anything
between two snippets that did not join. The caveats and `open` say so.
