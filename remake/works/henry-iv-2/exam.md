# Henry IV, Part 2: the examiner's record

Begun 2026-10-03, 05:41 UTC (see `examiner.txt`). Words of the play are
pointed at by part, row and word number; single words are named where a
number alone would not be clear. Nothing of Auden's or Pearce's is quoted.

This file is added to as the examination goes on. The verdict is at the end.

## 1. The proof

`node build/tools/check-reading.mjs henry-iv-2` as the poet left it: no
problems, no warnings, printed notes fit to print. Every line of the proof
was read against `words.mjs`. Each mark sits on the word it is meant for.
No word on the three plates is joined to the next by a dash (the hyphened
compounds at a3s1 5.23 w3 and a5s5 57.3 w1, 57.8 w6 are single words and
are marked whole).

## 2. The ear

Every scanned row was marked by me, by word number, before the poet's
marks were looked at: a3s1 rows 5.0 to 5.4 and 5.22 to 5.27, and a5s5 rows
51.0, 53.0, 55.0 and 57.0 to 57.8.

The stated meter ("five beats to the line, rising") is the real meter of
both speeches. The King's speech on sleep has twenty-eight lines. I
counted the syllables of each with `words.mjs` and by ear: twenty-six have
ten (or eleven with a light ending, rows 5.0 and 5.7), and two have
twelve, rows 5.1 and 5.26. The eight rows the rule counts at eleven were
each looked at: two light endings; two words the rule over-counts (5.2 w0,
5.3 w7); four words run together in speech (5.12 w4, 5.16 w5, 5.18 w3,
5.20 w5). None of them reaches twelve however it is said. Each of the two
long lines has a sentence end after its sixth syllable (5.1 w4, 5.26 w4),
in the hosted text and in both early printings.

My marks and the poet's agree on every beat that a caption leans on. Where
I heard a line another way, both ways are defensible and nothing is built
on the syllable:

- 5.1 w1 to w3: I gave w2 a full stress; the poet gives it a light one.
- 5.2 w3, w4: I gave w4 a light stress beside w3; the poet stresses w3 only.
- 5.22 w0, w1: either word can carry the stress.
- 5.26 w8: can be a full stress; the poet marks it light.
- 51.0 w6, w7: w6 can be stressed as strongly as w7.
- 53.0: the stresses on w0 and w4 can each move to the pronoun after.
- 57.0 w9 is counted as one syllable and 57.4 w1 as one; each can be two.
  The reading says so in a point on the line and in `caveats`.
- 55.0, on which the third passage's Beat caption does lean: ten
  monosyllables, stress on w1, w3, w5, w7 and w9. The possessives (w0, w2,
  w8) could be pressed by an actor; they would not take the beat from the
  nouns. The three stops inside the line are in the Quarto (three commas)
  and the Folio (comma, semicolon, comma) as well as in the hosted text.

## 3. The terms

- Chiasmus (first passage, tab Cross). *Silva Rhetoricae* was opened:
  chiasmus is the repetition of ideas, or of grammatical structures, in
  inverted order, and it says antimetabole (words repeated in reverse
  order) is sometimes known as chiasmus. Both crossings do cross: 90.0 w4
  and w9 against 92.0 w10 and w14 (the two adjectives change places and
  nouns); 94.0 w0, w3 to w5 against 96.0 w0 to w2, w5 (subject and object
  change places, the verb stays: 94.0 w2, 96.0 w4). The term stands, and
  the ground already names antimetabole.
- Antithesis (third passage, tab Balance). *Silva Rhetoricae*:
  contrasting words or ideas set side by side, often in parallel
  structure. Row 57.5 w1 to w3 against w6 to w8 is that. Stands.
- The plain-words glosses of both are right.

## 4. The words

Every entry of Alexander Schmidt's *Shakespeare-Lexicon* behind a gloss
was fetched from the Perseus Digital Library and read (the pages are in
my scratch folder). Each says what the reading says, and the entries cite
or quote the lines as the reading claims: Great 1 and 3, Infamy, Slender
1 and 2, Waste (substantive) 1 with its note of the pun, Waist 1, Mislead,
Misleader; Fright (verb), Steep (verb), Partial, Repose, Sea-boy, Rude 2,
Appliance, Mean (substantive) 4 and 5, Boot 3, Deny 5c, Low 3, Uneasy 2;
Impartial 1, Vain 2, Heart 2, Jester 3, Surfeit-swelled, Profane
(adjective) 2, Hence 6, Grace 3, 9 and 11, Gormandize, Fool-born,
Competence. The lexicon is of Shakespeare's own usage, so each sense was
current when the play was written.

One thing mended here (see section 6, change 7): the gloss "here,
medicines" on a3s1 5.25 w4 is the lexicon's, which sets this line among
the uses of the word "of medicaments"; but that group stands under its
general sense 4 (whatever is used to effect a purpose), and a reader
should be told so. A note was added to the gloss.

The early printings were read in the Internet Shakespeare Editions
old-spelling transcriptions (Quarto 1600, Folio 1623), fetched whole. What
the reading says of them is so. Three things it did not say are now said
(changes 8 and 9): the Quarto differs from the Folio at a3s1 5.22 w6 as
well; and the full stop after a5s5 57.0 w5 is an editor's (the Folio has
a colon there and the Quarto a comma, and the Quarto has no stop at all
after w3).

## 6. The reason and its ground

Every caption and every `why` was taken a sentence at a time. For each
sentence I looked at the proof, read the lines and the scene around them,
opened the source, or found Auden's page. What stood is listed first, then
what did not.

### What stood

**First passage (a1s2 86.0 to 96.0).** The scene was read from the Chief
Justice's entrance (block 18) to Falstaff's later answer about his age
(block 120).

- Cross. Three charges (86.0, 90.0, 94.0); the second and third replies
  cross. Second reply a wish, third turns the charge on the Prince: the
  lines say so. The six-word phrase from Act 2 is at a2s1 block 71, said
  by the Chief Justice to Falstaff. Auden's remark on word-play as a
  chosen way of being liked: Pearce p. 147. All stand.
- Word. The three charges are about how he lives; each reply brings in
  his size (88.0 w6, 92.0 w13, 96.0 w12 to w13). Lexicon senses as given;
  the Quarto spells the two words at 90.0 w7 and 92.0 w13 alike. Auden
  pp. 183 to 184. All stand.
- Sentence. Subjects and verbs counted on the proof: the first person
  takes one verb twice (92.0 w1, w6) and another once (96.0 w7), and no
  other. Auden p. 186 (one cause of his speech; one theme in five words,
  which are a phrase long out of copyright) and p. 207, where Auden
  himself quotes row 96.0 as what must be added. This is the strongest
  ground in the piece: Auden points at these very words. Stands.
- Play. `count.mjs` was run, and I counted again with a script of my own
  that splits on spaces only: 1,640 lines of verse; 13,188 words of prose;
  Falstaff 5,373 words of prose and eleven lines set as verse; nine of
  nineteen scenes without prose and Falstaff in none of them; the old King
  296 lines and no prose; the Prince no prose after a2s4 block 283. Peto's
  news is of posts from the north (a2s4 block 295). Abbott section 515a
  and Baum chapter 4 say what is claimed. Auden p. 221. Stands.

**Second passage (a3s1 block 5).** The whole speech and the scene to
block 30 were read.

- Beat. Counted above (section 2). The two long lines are the two where
  the one spoken to changes; no other line of the twenty-eight is
  lengthened and there is no other change of hearer. Baum chapter 4 (the
  six-stress line tends to split into three and three); Abbott sections
  493 and 500. The caption states the coincidence and no motive. Stands.
  Abbott's section 501, which the poet did not cite, is now named in the
  ground: it says the form in one speaker's mouth is most frequent in
  comic and lighter serious verse.
- Sentence. First-person words: rows 5.0, 5.2, 5.3, 5.4 only (my script
  and the poet's agree). The first question is not returned to. The second
  verb of the last question stands two lines after the conjunction that
  begins its second half (5.24 w0, 5.26 w0). Strunk's rule 18 was opened
  (Project Gutenberg no. 37134) and says what is claimed. Stands, with
  change 4.
- Word. Lexicon: Partial cites 5.22; Deny 5c quotes 5.26, and the first
  example of that sense is of suits granted and denied. The word at 5.22
  w3 comes once in the play and its opposite twice, both in a5s2 (42.1,
  61.14), both of the Chief Justice: searched again by me. Stands, with
  change 4b.

**Third passage (a5s5 51.0 to 57.8).** The scene was read from block 9 to
block 61, and the King's whole speech.

- Names. Three possessive names in 55.0; no name for Falstaff anywhere in
  the King's twenty-six lines (read through); three labels in the lines
  shown; five orders to Falstaff (57.0 w6, 57.5 w0, 57.6 w0 and w2, 57.8
  w0 to w1). Pearce p. 147 and Auden p. 191. Falstaff says nothing more to
  the King. All stand.
- Balance. Antithesis in 57.5. The wish of the same build is at a1s2
  92.0; the allowance at 57.19 to 57.20, fifteen lines on, with the same
  noun as 90.0 w1 and 92.0 w8. Lexicon: Grace 9 quotes the line; Mean 5
  cites both places; Competence quotes 57.19. Auden p. 190. The caption
  sets the two places side by side and claims no design. Stands.

### What did not stand, and what was done

1. **Second passage, Rhyme.** The caption gave Auden's remark that father
   and son each speak of how uneasy a king is, and went straight on to
   the new King's words in Act 5 of this play. A reader would take those
   for the son's speech Auden meant. They are not: on p. 107 he names a
   speech on sleep in *Henry V*. The caption now says so, and gives the
   Act 5 line as a place in this play. The ground for the Act 5 line says
   that it is the reading's addition. The Baum ground was made exact: he
   says the thing of the four-beat couplet.
2. **Third passage, Word.** The caption closed by setting the King's
   word at 57.4 w7 beside Auden's phrase for what the Prince is to
   Falstaff while the music lasts (p. 510). Each half was true. The
   pairing was the poet's: Auden says nothing of 57.2 to 57.4, and a
   closing echo of that kind is what rule three forbids. Cut. In its
   place the caption gives what Auden does join to this speech on pp. 510
   to 511: music helps Falstaff keep his illusion, time stands still for
   him and he will not grow older; and at their next meeting come the
   King's first words to him, which hold the first of the four phrases
   the caption began with. Auden calls them the first words Falstaff is
   to hear; the King speaks first to the Chief Justice (51.0), so the
   caption calls them his first words to Falstaff. `why`, `grounds` and
   `stands` follow.
3. **Third passage, Beat.** After Auden's remark that Falstaff still
   talks as his comic world talks (p. 191), the caption said that here he
   uses the King's measure, and the `why` joined the two with "yet". That
   makes Auden's sentence bear on Falstaff's verse. It does not: his
   example on that page is a prose speech from Part 1, and he says nothing
   of this line. The caption now says his example is prose and this call
   is verse, and stops. Also: the caption said the King "answers in six
   syllables"; the full stop after 57.0 w5 is an editor's (Folio colon,
   Quarto comma), so the caption now says the answer stops after six
   syllables, and the point on 57.0 w6 says whose the full stop is.
4. **Second passage, Sentence.** Pearce's note has Auden tell the class
   to notice the King's rhetoric in the whole play. The caption left the
   scope out, which let the remark read as one about this speech. It is
   now in. The ground says that the notes do not name the speech.

   4b. **Second passage, Word.** The caption had Auden say that impartial
   justice is a king's duty, in general. He says it of the son, at the
   rejection (p. 190). The caption and the `why` now say so. A sentence
   that only repeated the first one was taken out to make room.
5. **The page of a remark.** See section 5. Pages 102, 107 and 110 became
   107 and 110 in the ninth entry under `auden`, in `sources`, and the
   caveat about the lectures no longer says a page was not seen.
6. **Words kept from the source.** Three entries under `auden` kept
   strings of their source's own words in a paraphrase: the second
   (three adjectives of Pearce's in a row, and a three-word phrase), the
   fourth (two short phrases of Auden's) and the ninth (two of the
   editor's text). Each was put in other words. The model's `open` list
   names the same fault.
7. **A gloss.** See section 4. A note was added to the gloss on a3s1
   5.25 w4.
8. **The early printings.** The caveat now gives the Quarto's reading at
   a3s1 5.22 w6 with the other two.
9. **An editor's stop.** The point on a5s5 57.0 w6 now says that the
   full stop is an editor's.

## 7. The prose

Every printed string (captions, reasons, notes, glosses, labels, sources,
caveats) was read, and searched by script for dashes inside sentences,
rhetorical questions, "not ... but", "we", the stock words, and the words
the checker flags under rule three. None found. The only British spelling
is inside the lexicon's own words, given as such in one note. American
English otherwise. No caption ends on a sentence that sums up what the
passage "means".

## 8. The rules

- Text typed into the reading: single words, and phrases of at most six
  words in captions (the longest are at a2s1 block 71, a5s2 49.1 w0 to w5
  and a5s5 57.0 w0 to w5, six words each). One caveat names a three-word
  phrase in two readings.
- In copyright: two quotations, 22 words (`view.quote`, exact, p. 198) and
  13 words (third entry under `auden`, exact, p. 190). Nothing else of
  Auden's, Pearce's or Kirsch's runs to more than a common word or two.
- Plates: 71 words of prose; eleven lines; twelve lines.
- Captions 100 to 110 words; every `why` 40 words or fewer; `view.says`
  70 words.

## 9. The printed notes

`view.source` and each `auden[].source` hold the book, the year and the
page. `sources` has one entry for each book or page stood on; Strunk's

## 12. Verdict

Mended. No plate failed and no lens was removed. Nine things were
changed, set out in section 6; the chief ones are a wrong page for one of
Auden's remarks, and three captions that set Auden's words beside
something he did not say them of.

The checker was run after every change. Its last three lines, from
`node build/tools/check-reading.mjs henry-iv-2 --print` on the reading as
it now stands (exit code 0):

    no problems
    no warnings
    the printed notes are fit to print

The poet's `notes.md` was left as written. Two of its statements are now
out of date: its section 1, point 3, on the King's measure, and what it
says of the page of the remark from the New York lectures.
