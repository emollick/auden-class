## 2. Why these three passages

### The first passage: a1s2, rows 86.0 to 96.0 (71 words of prose)

Three charges by the Chief Justice and Falstaff's three replies. Chosen
because the thing to be seen is on the surface of the words:

- the second and third replies give the judge's words back in the opposite
  order (90.0 w4 and w9 against 92.0 w10 and w14; 94.0 w0 and w3-5 against
  96.0 w0-2 and w5), with the verb kept (94.0 w2, 96.0 w4);
- the replies turn three words about how Falstaff lives into words of size
  (86.0 w9, 90.0 w4, 90.0 w7 against 88.0 w6 and w10, 92.0 w13-14, 96.0
  w12-13), and the lexicon marks the pun at 90.0 w7 and 92.0 w13;
- the grammatical subject changes: the judge's sentences are about what the
  one addressed does; in the replies the doer is someone else (88.0 w0-6,
  96.0 w0-2) and the first person only wishes (92.0 w0-1, w5-6) and is
  (96.0 w6-7);
- Auden points at this very place. Near the end of his essay (p. 207) he
  quotes the whole of the reply in row 96.0 as what Falstaff has to add to
  his one theme, and the essay's title appears to come from the end of that
  row (96.0 w14-17). In the 1941 class he said Falstaff uses word-play on
  purpose.

It is also prose, and the plain home for the count of verse and prose that the
leads ask for.

Considered and not taken: the next exchange of the same kind in the scene
(blocks 122 and 124), where one sentence is given back with its two nouns
exchanged. It is the neatest crossing of the three, but it stands 26 blocks
further on and would have made the passage too long. It was in an earlier
draft of the first caption and was cut for length.

### The second passage: a3s1, rows 5.0 to 5.4 and 5.22 to 5.27

The first five and last six lines of the King's speech of 28 lines. The two
ends are shown and the middle is left out because:

- the speech has exactly two lines of twelve syllables, its second (5.1) and
  its last but one (5.26), and each breaks after the sixth syllable where a
  sentence ends and the King turns to a new hearer (to Sleep; from Sleep to
  the low). Both had to be on one page for that to be seen;
- the speech ends on its closing rhyme (5.26, 5.27), with the same verb in
  both lines (5.26 w8, 5.27 w1);
- every first-person word of the speech is in the first five lines (5.0 w4,
  5.2 w5, 5.3 w6, 5.4 w2); the last lines have the articles instead (5.26
  w3-4, 5.27 w2-3 and w6-7);
- 5.22 w3 and 5.26 w0 are the words that meet Auden's account of what a king
  owes (impartial justice) and of what this King did.

Auden's remarks used here: the 1941 remark that this King's rhetoric is
hollow; the essay on the injustice by which he took the crown; the New York
lecture, which cites this speech and its last line.

What the omission costs is said in `caveats` and `open`: nothing on the page
marks the gap except the heading, and one pair of the lines left out rhymes
(5.8, 5.9), so the caption does not say that the speech is unrhymed until its
end. The measure is named "five beats to the line, rising" and no longer
"unrhymed".

One more cost of the cut. Row 5.3 ends on the same word as row 5.26, the
first line of the closing rhyme. In the speech it stands twenty-three lines
before it, too far to be heard as a rhyme, and the Rhyme tab marks it as no
rhyme (the letter x, which the kit draws as a dot with no arc). On the page
the cut brings the two within six rows of each other. This is said in `open`.
Row 5.2 likewise ends on the same word as row 5.6, which is not shown.

### The third passage: a5s5, rows 51.0 to 57.8 (twelve lines)

The King's order to the Chief Justice, the Justice's line, Falstaff's line,
and the first nine lines of the King's speech. Chosen because:

- it is the one place where the prose speaker of the first passage is seen in
  the King's measure, in a line of ten monosyllables broken by three stops
  (55.0), answered by a sentence of six syllables (57.0 w0-5);
- the two speakers name each other in opposite ways in a few lines: three
  names with a possessive (55.0 w0-1, w2-3, w8-9) against three labels (51.0
  w6-8, 57.0 w4-5, 57.1 w5-8) and five orders (57.0 w6, 57.5 w0, 57.6 w0 and
  w2, 57.8 w0-1);
- row 57.5 has the build of the wish in row 92.0 of the first passage, so the
  third passage answers the first, and the last order shown (57.8) forbids the
  sort of reply the first passage shows;
- Auden wrote most about this moment: the rejection as the new King's first
  public act, the allowance that cannot mend it, Falstaff drawn out of his
  world of play into time and death.

The passage stops at 57.8 to stay within fourteen lines. The remaining
seventeen lines of the speech are used only for what can be counted in them
(no name for Falstaff; the allowance at 57.19 and 57.20; the third use of the
word marked at 94.0 w2 of the first passage, at 57.17 w8).

## 3. The scansions, and the other way of hearing each

Marks: `/` a full stress, `\` a light one, `x` none. I said each line aloud
before marking it. Where two hearings are fair, no caption leans on the
doubtful syllable: the second passage's Beat caption rests on the count of
syllables and on where the sentences end, the third's on row 55.0 and on the
stress at 57.0 w6.

Second passage (a3s1):

- 5.0. Eleven syllables, the last light. I give w0 no stress. It can take
  one, which opens the line on a stress. The beat on w3 is passed over either
  way.
- 5.1. Twelve syllables. I hear w2 lightly stressed and w1 not, so the first
  beat of the measure is passed over. The regular way stresses w1 and leaves
  w2 light. w3 is one syllable here; said as two, the line has thirteen.
- 5.2. w0 is two syllables (the tool's rule gave three; the division is
  given). I stress w3 and not w4, an inversion after the comma. The other
  way keeps the measure: w4 stressed, w3 light.
- 5.3. w7 is two syllables (the rule gave three). No doubt about the stresses.
- 5.4. Regular; the beat on w4 is passed over and the last syllable is light.
- 5.22. w0 light, w1 full; w5 stressed against the measure and w6 not. The
  other ways: w0 full (the line then opens on a stress), or w6 stressed for
  the contrast with the hearer of 5.26.
- 5.23. w2 and the first syllable of w3 both stressed, w1 not, and the beat
  on w4 passed over. w2 could be lighter. w6 is one syllable.
- 5.24. The beats on w1 and w4 are passed over, and w5 takes a light stress
  off the beat. The regular way gives w4 the light stress and w5 none.
- 5.25. Regular.
- 5.26. Twelve syllables as printed. By the measure the beats fall on
  syllables 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12; the voice gives none to w2 (the fourth
  syllable), so the first half can be heard with two stresses (the third
  pinned remark says so). Another hearing runs w0 and w1 together, which
  gives eleven syllables and five beats with one extra light syllable before
  w4. I mark w8 light; it can be full, which puts three stresses together at
  the end. Because of all this the caption says "twelve syllables, six beats
  by the same count" and "six syllables on either side", and does not rest on
  a sixth spoken stress.
- 5.27. Regular.

Third passage (a5s5):

- 51.0. w6 light, w7 and w8 full. The other way gives w6 the full stress (he
  is pointing) and w7 a lighter one.
- 53.0. I stress w0 and w4 and not w1 and w5: two inversions, one after each
  stop. The regular way stresses the pronoun (w1, w5) both times. The fourth
  beat falls on w7, a contracted word, and is marked as a light stress; the
  other hearing puts that stress on w6 and none on w7. No caption speaks of
  this line's stresses.
- 55.0. Regular: ten monosyllables, stress on every second; pauses after w1,
  w3 and w7.
- 57.0. w9 is counted as one syllable, which makes ten. It can be said as
  two, a light eleventh (the second pinned remark says so). w4 light and w5
  full; both can be full. w6 is stressed after the full stop where the
  measure expects a light syllable; I do not think the other way (stress on
  w7) is sayable.
- 57.1. Eleven syllables, the last light. w2 light.
- 57.2. w2 and w3 both stressed, w1 not. The regular way stresses w1 and
  leaves w2 lighter.
- 57.3. Regular; w5 light.
- 57.4. w1 is counted as one syllable. Said as two, the first syllable of w2
  has to be slurred to keep ten. w4 light; an emphatic reading makes it full.
- 57.5. w0 light, then regular.
- 57.6. w0 stressed where the measure expects a light syllable; it can be
  lighter. The third syllable of w1 carries a light stress on a beat.
- 57.7. w1, w2 and the first syllable of w3 all stressed. w2 can be light.
- 57.8. w1 and w3 light, the beats on w2 and w4 passed over. Other ways: w1
  full and w3 unstressed; a strictly regular reading (stress on w2 and w4) is
  not natural speech.
