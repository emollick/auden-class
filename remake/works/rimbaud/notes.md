## Auden's view, and where it was read

No record was found of what Auden said about Rimbaud in the 1941 class
(see "Looked for and not found"). The view is from *The Enchafèd Flood*,
the Page-Barbour lectures of March 1949, chapter 3, the section headed
"The Artist as Don Quixote". Read in the scan `research/auden/ef.txt`
(first Vintage Books edition, September 1967). In this scan the page
number stands at the foot of its page, so a passage belongs to the
number that follows it.

- p. 147 (lines 6040–6070): the longing for innocence as the mark of
  the man whose career is exploring the unknown; Auden puts this as a
  question that expects yes, and the reading reports it as his
  statement. Then: in earlier ages the artist recorded the acts of
  others; in the Romantic period the maker becomes the hero whose deeds
  he has to record; from about 1770 to 1914 the heroic figures are
  individual geniuses, and the artist's knowledge came chiefly from
  himself.
- p. 148 (lines 6072–6104): the artist as subject of his experiment and
  its recorder (this is the sentence in `view.quote`); the poète maudit
  marked out by a catastrophe that drives him to the limits of
  exhaustion; then the sentence that what Rimbaud said of himself in
  this book, before he said good-bye to art, is true more or less of
  them all. The French that follows on pp. 148–49 (lines 6106–6122) is
  exactly rows 16.0 to 18.0 of `rimbaud-fr/s5`, which is why those rows
  are the first plate.
- p. 149 (lines 6127–6137): some of them came to see that the artist is
  not the religious hero but only the explorer of possibility (the
  clause in `auden[0].quote`). "Thus Rimbaud" follows, with an English
  version of row 4.0 of `rimbaud-fr/s9` joined to row 6.0 without its
  first word, running over to p. 150 (lines 6147–6157). Block 5 is left
  out by Auden; the third plate leaves it out too.
- p. 150 (lines 6163–6178): the new age in which the artist neither can
  have such heroic importance nor wants it; remember their courage.
- The two figures: p. 94 (lines 3902–3905), the religious hero as one
  committed to something with absolute passion; pp. 96–97 (3977–4028),
  the Ishmael of Genesis; p. 100 (4135–4137), Don Quixote as the
  greatest picture of the religious hero; pp. 101–102 (4192–4221),
  Ishmael the wanderer without a fixed commitment, self-conscious where
  Don Quixote forgets himself. That Melville's narrator bears the name:
  `moby-dick/c001`, block 0.
- Other uses of Rimbaud in the same book (`auden[2]`): p. 18 (lines
  809–820), the Rimbaud of “Le Bateau ivre” as a Don Quixote of freedom
  who still regrets Europe; p. 25 (1157–1166), the poem “Democracy”;
  p. 108 (4470–4491), two quotations for the unhappy childhood of the
  Romantic hero. Found by searching the scan for his name; all seven
  hits were read.

## Plate 1, "hallucination": `rimbaud-fr/s5` rows 16.0, 17.0, 18.0

**Why this passage.** It is the passage Auden himself quotes, in
French, as true of all the Romantic artists. It is 94 words. Things can
be seen in it: two past tenses doing different work, a list with no
joining word, and in each paragraph a word that judges set next to a
word that keeps what he believed at the time. That is Auden's "subject
of the experiment and recorder" in the grain of the sentences.

**Tense.** Past definite at 16.0 w1, 17.0 w1, 18.0 w1; imperfect at
16.0 w6 and w44, 18.0 w10 and w18. What the two tenses tell: Noël and
Chapsal, *Nouvelle grammaire française*, revised by A. Vaillant
(Philadelphia: Moss, 1863), no. 118 (pp. 31–32) and no. 481 (p. 147),
read in the Internet Archive text `nouvellegrammair00noe.txt` (no. 118
at line 1523; nos. 477–484 at lines 9645–9700). The title page says the
book was adopted for schools, hence "a school grammar of the time". The
Dial has one English past for both tenses and marks the imperfect once
(p:16.0 w9–11). First line of the chapter: `rimbaud-fr/s5` block 0.
The form at 18.0 w1 is the same in the present and the past definite;
the verbs around it are past and the Dial reads it as past.

**List (asyndeton).** Items at 16.0 w8–9, w15–22, w23–29, w30–35, then
two nouns with only their articles at w36–37 and w38–39; the one verb
at w5–7; what the first item stood in place of at w10–14. A search of
blocks 16 to 18 finds no "et" in the French and no "and" in the
English. Littré, *asyndète* (a synonym of *disjonction*; the joining
conjunctions are cut away) and *disjonction*, sense 2 (they are dropped
"to gain speed"). Also Littré *salon* 1, *calèche* 1, *vaudeville* 3,
*épouvante*. Auden's "terrors": p. 148. The caption names the things
seen in phrases of at most six words; it does not translate the
sentence.

**Judgment.** Littré, read at littre.org on 2026-10-03: *hallucination*
(a term of medicine), *franchement* 2 and 3, *sophisme*, *magique* 1,
*sacré* 2, *désordre* 1, *oisif* 1. "One of each kind in each
paragraph" was counted in the proof: 16.0 w3 and w7; 17.0 w3 and w4;
18.0 w6 and w4. The later sentence about the sophisms of madness:
`rimbaud-fr/s5` block 46.

**Innocence.** Littré *félicité* 1, *bête* 1 (the remark that the beast
lacks reason is an example sentence inside the entry), *chenille* 1,
*représenter* 10, *limbes* (the definition, a citation from Voltaire,
and the etymology all put limbo at the edge of hell), *taupe* 1.
Baptism and the pagans: `rimbaud-fr/s3` block 3. Auden: p. 147. The
token at 18.0 w22 runs two words together across a dash, because the
hosted text has no spaces there; the gloss sits on the whole token.

## Plate 2, "stanza": `rimbaud-fr/s5` rows 37.0 and 38.0–38.3

**Why this passage.** The chapter quotes seven of his own poems. This
is the shortest place where the frame can be seen with what it frames:
one prose sentence that judges (37.0) and the stanza it introduces. The
stanza can be counted and its rhyme can be heard, and it comes back as
the last stanza of the same poem (block 43). The Dial leaves the poems
in French, so the pair shows only the English of the prose sentence
(`rimbaud/s5` row 36.0, words 23 to 35; the Dial joins what the hosted
French sets as blocks 36 and 37).

**Count.** The rules of counting: L. E. Kastner, *A History of French
Versification* (Oxford, 1903), Internet Archive text `kastner.txt`:
chapter 1, pp. 1–2 (a fixed number of syllables; the last sounded
syllable stressed; the other accents free, and no two readers agree on
them); chapter 2, pp. 4–5 (a mute e at the line end is never counted; a
mute e before a vowel is elided). So 38.0 counts five with the mute e
of word 0 elided, 38.1 one and four, 38.2 five with the final mute e
uncounted, 38.3 three.

Other ways of hearing it:

- The accent marked on 38.1 w0 is my ear: a one-word question, with a
  pause after it. A reader may run the line as one phrase with only the
  accent on its last syllable. The count does not change. This is in
  `caveats`.
- In 38.2 a reader may lean on word 2. I marked only the fixed accent.
- The whole poem in this book (blocks 38 to 43), counted the same way:
  stanza 1: 5, 5, 5, 3; stanza 2: 5, 5, 5, 5; stanza 3: 5, 5, 5, 5;
  stanza 4: 5, 5, then 4 to 6 by how two vowels together are divided,
  5; stanza 5: 5, 5, 3, 4; stanza 6: 5, 5, 5, 3. In the printing of
  1886 (*Les Illuminations*, La Vogue, p. 86, French Wikisource, page
  marked as validated; saved as `ws_et1886.html`) every line counts
  five, the third line of this stanza ends on a different word, and the
  fourth has five syllables. So the short line belongs to the version
  in this book. Whether Rimbaud shortened it on purpose or wrote the
  poem down from memory I could not find out; the remark pinned to row
  38.3 says only what the two printings have, and a caveat says the
  rest.

The short unrhymed closing line of Old French epics: Kastner, chapter
12, p. 309, note 1 (text line 21229 onward); that the chansons de geste
are the national epic, chapter 3, p. 40. The five-syllable line and
where whole stanzas of it occur: chapter 8, pp. 151–52 (line 10716
onward). The caption says that this way of ending is on record there
and that Rimbaud says old poetic stuff had a good share in his alchemy
(`rimbaud-fr/s5` block 15; Littré *vieillerie* 1 and 2). It does not
say he took the device from the epics. No source says that.

Auden on verse measured by syllables: “Marianne Moore,” *The Dyer's
Hand*, p. 297 (`research/auden/dh.txt`, lines 14088–14098; the head of
p. 297 is at line 14078, that of p. 298 at line 14128). What his words
cover: verse in which only the number of syllables counts is very hard
for an English ear, and the French twelve-syllable line is his case.
The caption says no more than that.

**Rhyme.** Kastner, chapter 3, p. 39 (line 3441 onward): the definition
of rhyme, masculine and feminine; most feminine endings are no longer
heard, yet the rules forbid coupling the two kinds even when they sound
alike; a few modern poets have broken the rule. So by rule a, b, a, c;
by ear three lines on one vowel and one line alone. The list of things
he loved: `rimbaud-fr/s5` block 2; the two English phrases in the
caption are the Dial's.

**Frame.** Littré *expression* 2 and 4, *bouffon* (noun 1; adjective 1
and 2), *égaré* 1 and 5, *égarement* 2, *possible* (for the idiom at
37.0 w9–10), *retrouver* 1 and 2, *éternité* 1, *mêlé* 1. The tense
of 37.0 w3 is imperfect (Noël and Chapsal, no. 118). The glosses under
the stanza are each six words or fewer. The caption reports what the
stanza says and does not give it line by line.

**Chapter (shape).** Counted in `rimbaud-fr/s5` with a small script
that uses the site's own word-splitting (`count.mjs` in the scratch
folder): 23 prose blocks, 663 words; 34 verse blocks, 113 lines, 518
words; 2 headings, 7 words; 1,188 in all. The runs of prose and verse,
block by block, are in the lens's `facts` under working keys. The first
edition (French Wikisource, saved as `ws_alchimie1873.html`) prints
blocks 36 and 37 as one paragraph and sets a rule between the fourth
and fifth poems; the hosted text shows neither. The 23 paragraphs
follow the hosted text; the seven poems follow the first edition and
the note in the index of the hosted English. "Mostly in past tenses":
I read the 23 prose blocks through; the last block is in the present.
The translator's note: *The Dial*, July 1920, p. 2, read in
`research/texts/rimbaud_season_in_hell_dial_1920_ia_ocr.txt`, lines 10
to 70.

## Plate 3, "adieu": `rimbaud-fr/s9` rows 3.0 (words 31–80), 4.0, 6.0

**Why this passage.** Rows 4.0 and 6.0 are what Auden quotes, in
English, for the artist who finds he is not the religious hero. The end
of row 3.0 comes just before and holds the claims he gives up: three
sentences that begin alike. 91 words in all. The heading says which
paragraphs are shown; block 5 (two short questions) is left out, as in
Auden's quotation, and a caveat says so.

**Figure (anaphora).** Sentence heads at 3.0 w31, w42, w57; the verbs
at w32, w43–44, w58–59; the repeated words inside at w33–34, w36–37,
w39–40 and at w45–46, w48–49, w51–52, w54–55. Littré *anaphore*. That
the verbs claim less each time is read off the three verbs themselves.

**Time.** Past indefinite at 3.0 w31–32, w42–43, w57–58 and 4.0 w3–5;
present at 3.0 w65–66 and 4.0 w13–15; future at 6.0 w1–2; imperative,
first person plural, at 6.0 w10. Noël and Chapsal no. 118 and nos. 480
and 481 (pp. 146–47): the past indefinite may be used of a time with
some part still to run, the past definite only of a time completely
over. Littré *aller* for the imperative forms.

**Sentence.** The main clause begins at 4.0 w13, the fourteenth word.
Its verb is passive in form. Littré *rendu* 4 is "brought back to,
restored to", said of persons, which is the sense here; sense 2 of the
same entry is "arrived where one meant to go", which does not fit a man
given back to the ground against his claims, and every English version
I saw (the Dial's and the one Auden quotes) takes it as done to him.
The peasants of “Bad Blood”: `rimbaud-fr/s2` block 3. Littré *paysan*
1, *sol* (second entry) 1 and 2, *rugueux*, *étreindre* 1 and 2.

**English (compare).** Littré *mage* 1 to 3 (the magician sense is
marked as out of use), *dispenser* 4, *devoir* (the noun, sense 1, and
the etymology: the infinitive used as a noun), *morale* 1, *embrasser*
1, *emporter* 1 (with the familiar oath), *aller* (the conjugation and
sense 22). The Dial's other renderings: `rimbaud/s8` block 2 and
`rimbaud/s2` block 22. The last sentence of the caption, that the
English Auden quotes keeps the magus and the duty, was read in the scan
at pp. 149–50; no more of that version is given, because it is in
copyright.
