# rimbaud

Source: Internet Archive, item `sim_dial_1920-07_69` ("The Dial 1920-07: Vol 69", a microfilm of the magazine), the plain machine reading `sim_dial_1920-07_69_djvu.txt`, kept whole in `.cache/texts/ia_sim_dial_1920-07_69_djvu.txt` (7,061 lines; the piece is lines 1 to 1424). Not Project Gutenberg: the file is raw.

What it is: "A Season in Hell, by Arthur Rimbaud", the first piece in The Dial (New York) for July 1920, pages 1 to 26. Seen on the page images: page 1 has the masthead, "JULY 1920", the title and "BY ARTHUR RIMBAUD", then a note in square brackets headed "Translator's Note", which runs on to page 2; the translation follows and ends on page 26 with the date of the work; a poem by another hand begins under it on the same page (which is why page 26 carries that poem's running head). The whole translation is in this one issue, all nine sections. The number of the volume is not printed on these pages; the index of the volume (item `sim_dial_1920_69_index`) is headed "Volume LXIX" and enters the piece by its title under Rimbaud's name, page 1. The wrapper and the contents page of the issue are not in the scan.

Translator: none is printed. The note is headed "Translator's Note" and is not signed; no name stands on the piece, anywhere else in the issue, in the index of the volume, or in the August issue (which prints the Illuminations, again with no name). So `translator` is null. The translation is attributed to James Sibley Watson, Jr., one of the owners of The Dial (so poets.org's page on him); the `note` says "attributed". `year` 1920.

Dropped: the masthead, date, title and author lines; the translator's note (lines 10 to 70, about 500 words); 24 running heads with their page numbers; 16 lines that are not print of these pages (14 are the ends of lines of the facing page, caught at the edge of the film beside page 23; 2 are marks at the edge of the film beside pages 8 and 24). The section headings go into the labels.

Parts: nine, with the ids of `rimbaud-fr`: `s1` the untitled opening, `s2` Bad Blood, `s3` Night in Hell, `s4` Délires I: Foolish Virgin, `s5` Délires II: Alchemy of the Word, `s6` The Impossible, `s7` The Lightning, `s8` Morning, `s9` Adieu. The Dial keeps "Délires" in French. The third heading line of Délires I (The Infernal Bridegroom) is a heading block at the top of `s4`, as in the French. 9 parts, 7,600 words, 23 pages; 173 `p`, 34 `v`, 3 `h`. The script checks that the words of the blocks are the words of the lines kept, less the headings and less one for each pair joined at a line end.

Verse: The Dial prints the seven poems of Délires II in French and does not translate them. They are `v` blocks, a block for each stanza: 34 blocks, 113 lines, the same stanzas as `rimbaud-fr`. Two poems have titles in small capitals, kept as heading blocks. The deeper indenting of some lines is not kept. The Dial's French is not the text of 1873: 23 lines differ from `rimbaud-fr` (stops, accents and capitals, and three places with other words). I checked each on the page and kept what The Dial prints.

Against the French, block by block: `s1`, `s2`, `s3`, `s7`, `s8`, `s9` have the same paragraphs in the same order. `s4` has two more, because The Dial twice sets a short quoted sentence as a paragraph of its own. `s5` has two fewer and `s6` one fewer, because The Dial runs two paragraphs of the French together (twice in `s5`, once at the head of `s6`). A few short phrases of the French have no English (three seen in Bad Blood: the crowd and the comparison with Joan of Arc in the paragraph of the firing squad, and a two-word question near the end); the page shows these are the translator's doing, not the scan's.

Corrections, all in tables in the script, by line of the file: 111 misreadings mended (`FIXES`: 69 in the English, of which 20 only take out a space before a stop and 12 restore dots the machine dropped; 36 in the French verse, most of them accents; 6 in headings set in small capitals); 39 places where the file breaks a paragraph or a stanza in two (`GLUE`, eight of them at the turn of a page) and 5 where it runs two together (`BREAK`); 47 words broken at a line end (45 joined, 2 compounds keep their hyphen); 14 line ends at a dash joined without a space, as The Dial sets its dashes.

How it was proofed: every line of the repaired text was compared with the page image, twice (the 26 images at `archive.org/download/sim_dial_1920-07_69/page/n0.jpg` to `n25.jpg`; leaf n(k) is page k+1). Every word of the English not found in the 28 English texts already in the site was looked at: 105 forms, all right as they stand. The start of every paragraph was checked against the indentation recorded in the scan's word boxes (`_djvu.xml`), and the spacing of lines and the foot of every page were checked for lines the machine might have missed: none.

Typography: the dots of omission are spaced as The Dial prints them, and four where a full stop comes first. The thin space The Dial puts before `; : ! ?` and between a double and a single quotation mark is not kept. The large initial of the first word, which the machine did not read, is restored. The date at the end has a short dash on the page. Italics are lost (two French words left in the English, some Latin, a few emphasised phrases). The white space that divides some sections into smaller ones is lost, as in `rimbaud-fr`.

Kept as printed, though they look like slips: in Bad Blood a sentence that goes straight from a dash into a capital with no stop (line 235); "difformities", "unconcerns", "vertigoes", "spitefullest"; "offenses" and "plow" beside "honour" and "colour"; "for ever" once and "forever" once; in the French verse "Hesperides", "gout", "Qu'a" and one "A" without their accents beside another "À" with it.

Check-text: no problems, no warnings. Doubtful: no reading is left in doubt. What rests on something outside the scan is the translator's name.
