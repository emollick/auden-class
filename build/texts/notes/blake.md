# blake: Blake, The Marriage of Heaven and Hell (c. 1790-93)

- Source: Project Gutenberg no. 45315 (https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/45315), cached as `.cache/texts/pg45315_blake.txt` (a fresh download on 2 Oct 2026 was byte for byte the same). It follows the edition of John W. Luce and Company, Boston, 1906: a reading text, with spelling, capitals and pointing modernised from Blake's plates. The file has no editor's introduction, no plate numbers and no descriptions of the designs; its one `[Illustration]` mark is on the title page and went with it.
- meta: `translator` null. `year` 1790. The work is dated c. 1790-93 and the field takes one number; 1790 is the year the text itself points to, the year Blake wrote on plate 3 of one copy, and the date Joseph Viscomi's introduction to the work (the Blake Archive editors' edition) accepts, while noting that c. 1790-3 is the range most often cited. Put 1793 if the end of the range is wanted. (The reader does not show the year of a work that has no translator.)
- One part, `marriage`. Blake's headings are `h` blocks, level 3, in this order: The Argument; The Voice of the Devil; A Memorable Fancy; Proverbs of Hell; A Memorable Fancy four times more; A Song of Liberty; Chorus. The script throws if the order is anything else.
- Verse: The Argument is five `v` blocks (2, 6, 8, 4 and 2 lines). The Devil's sentence that ends the first Memorable Fancy is two long lines; the edition turns each over onto a second, indented line begun without a capital, and the script joins the turn-overs, giving two lines and not four. The Proverbs of Hell are one `v` block, a proverb to a line; the source gives them no grouping (each is a paragraph of its own).
- 69 proverbs where Blake's plates have 70: this edition prints the proverb of prayers and praises and the proverb of joys and sorrows as one sentence. Kept as printed, and the script expects 69. All the others are there, one to a line.
- A Song of Liberty: its twenty numbered verses are `p` blocks with their numbers, as the source sets them (as prose). So are the numbered points in The Voice of the Devil.
- Counts: 1 part, 4,589 words, 93 lines of verse (22 + 2 + 69), 13 pages; 10 headings, 104 paragraphs, 7 verse blocks.
- Dropped: the title pages; italics marks (three paragraphs begin with "Note."); and four rows of asterisks, which stand where a new plate begins without a heading (after the Proverbs, and in the second, third and fourth Memorable Fancy). The contract has no block for a break, so what follows each row now simply runs on under the heading before it; the passage after the Proverbs is the plainest case.
- Kept: one bracketed word supplied by the editor in the second Memorable Fancy.
- check-text: no problems; three warnings, verse lines over 110 characters. They are the three longest proverbs, each one line by design.
- Check in the script: the words of the part are the words of the source lines less the twenty asterisks. A separate comparison word by word found them the same and in the same order.
