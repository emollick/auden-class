# Your lens: writing that reads as machine-made

Read every sentence of your part. For each one ask two things. Would a careful person writing a course handbook by hand have written this sentence? Does it do work for the student? You are looking for:

1. Stock phrases and signposts. Sentences that announce, frame, sum up or reassure and do not say a thing.
2. Reflex symmetry. "Not X but Y," "less X than Y," "X, and not Y," used for the rhythm when nobody holds X. Sentences built in mirrored pairs. Lists of exactly three by habit.
3. Endings that pose. An entry or paragraph that closes on an aphorism, a neat reversal, a moral, or a short sentence added for effect.
4. Inflated or vague diction. Abstract nouns that could be cut. Verbs such as explore, navigate, underscore, highlight, capture, embody. Words such as rich, deep, powerful, crucial, journey, lens, landscape. "A kind of," "a sense of."
5. Padding. A sentence that repeats the one before in other words. A clause that tells the student what he will feel or find. The obvious explained.
6. Mannered plainness. The writer was told to be plain and may have overdone it: a staccato that calls attention to itself, a mock-antique or gnomic tone, coyness, a quaint word where the ordinary one would do.
7. Sameness across entries. Every paragraph opening the same way. One sentence frame used again and again. The same closing move in entry after entry. Count them.
8. Hedging, throat-clearing, apology, and the site talking about itself more than the student needs.
9. Unearned authority. Confident claims with nothing behind them. Evaluative adjectives offered as fact.
10. Marks of the revision. This text has been revised once to a list of rules, and rule-following leaves its own tracks. Look for: "Auden says" or "he says" in sentence after sentence until it becomes a tic; "This site" and "the application is this site's" repeated until the disclaimers outnumber the statements; sentences joined with "and", "so" or "which" into one that is now too long to follow; a formula used at the end of block after block ("In your reply, say whether"); a word-for-word repetition between an entry and the one before it; a sentence whose grammar broke when a phrase was swapped in.
11. Anything else that made you think a machine wrote it. Trust the reaction and say what set it off.

Do not flag plain statements of fact, instructions that are simply clear, the questions put to the student, or quotations. Do not flag a verb of saying or a "this site's" merely because the house rules require one there; flag it when the same job is done twice in a paragraph or when a neater sentence would do it once.

For each finding write the replacement, and make sure the replacement does not commit a different fault from the list or break a house rule. Where the right fix is to cut, say cut.

Read the whole part once before you write anything, so that you can see what recurs. Then go through it entry by entry. Do not skip the short entries.
