# G, weeks 13 and 14. Lens: writing that reads as machine-made

Findings are in the order of the text. Fixes give only the sentences that change; where a fix ends "(the rest stands)" the remainder of the entry is to be left as it is. Titles and quotations are not copied here; they are shown as [quotation kept] or [title kept].

### week13.questions.1.auden.paras.1
- where: He adds two remarks on what was possible
- problem: A signpost that counts the remarks and says nothing. Of the three clipped sentences that follow, two have a subject, "It," that has to be traced back to a clause. The last sentence joins a contrast with "and," so the turn in Auden's thought is lost. Earlier in the paragraph "only possible" makes the reader stop to work out that it means possible and no more.
- fix: Second sentence: Until the Rachel, Ahab's end is still no more than a possibility. From the fourth sentence to the end: He adds that what was possible for Ahab was never unlimited, since he could never have become Starbuck, or Pip, or Ishmael, or anyone else, and that it did not run out as time passed. The past cannot be altered, Auden says, but it can always be redeemed now.
- weight: should

### week13.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: turns round the hardest saying in the Gospels
- problem: "Hardest" is a ranking given as fact. If it is Auden's, say so; if it is the site's, it has nothing behind it. "Turns round" is British idiom in an American text and does not say what Ahab does with the saying.
- fix: In refusing the Rachel he carries out, for his own purpose, the Gospel saying that a disciple must hate father and mother, wife and children. (This takes "turns round" to mean that he obeys the saying for the wrong reason. If Auden means something else, say that in plain words.)
- weight: should

### week13.questions.1.auden.paras.3

- problem: The piece was named by its title two paragraphs earlier and is now called by a date the student has not been given. A second work is reported in between, so the student cannot be sure which one is meant.
- fix: Open with the title again, as the first paragraph has it: [title kept] ends with Ishmael.
- weight: should

### week13.questions.2.text
- where: Hear the movement twice.
- problem: "Hear" as a command is not how an American handbook tells a student to play a record. It reads as plainness put on. week13.intro.2 uses the same verb.
- fix: Listen to the movement twice. (In week13.intro.2: The second question asks you to listen to it.)
- weight: could

### week13.questions.2.auden.paras.1
- where: took down what Auden said while he worked
- problem: Two faults. In the first sentence "he" could be Pearce or Auden, and the sentence trails off in a chain of prepositional phrases. The second sentence has no verb of saying, so the claim reads as the site's own, and the quoted words are fastened to it in a way that does not parse (a relation "of" one thing "to" another, then "between," then "or between").
- fix: While Auden fitted the record onto the portable phonograph he had brought to class, he went on talking, and Donald Pearce wrote it down. The relation of an individual to his fate, Auden said, the relation [quotation kept] or that between K. and the Castle, was reflected best in this movement, so far as music can express such a thing.
- weight: should

### week13.questions.2.auden.paras.2
- where: He started the record.
- problem: Two short sentences on one subject, set one after the other for the beat. They report a single action.
- fix: He gave no answer to his own question and started the record.
- weight: could

### week13.questions.2.auden.paras.3
- where: Nothing had been explained.
- problem: The paragraph opens by announcing that Pearce says something, then delivers it in clipped sentences, one of them ending on a four-word tag, and closes on a maxim. It is the clearest case in this part of plainness turned into a pose, and the staging is the site's, not Pearce's.
- fix: In place of the whole paragraph: Auden had set a novel beside a piece of music and explained neither. Pearce, who became a teacher, came to think that understanding seldom arrives any other way.
- weight: should

### week13.questions.3.auden.paras.1
- where: What they have in common is a kind
- problem: "A kind of" is a hedge of the sort the house rules ban, and the cleft opening delays the one word that matters. The next sentence avoids the plain noun for self-knowledge with an appositive that makes the reader stop.
- fix: The fault they share is sloth. Each has set out, as Ishmael does, on the voyage toward self-knowledge, and then has tried to get off the boat. (Keep the qualifier only if it is Auden's, and then say what kind he means.)
- weight: should

### week13.questions.3.auden.paras.2
- where: But fear is a right way to begin
- problem: The paragraph never says "Auden says," so six sentences of judgment on Starbuck stand as the site's own. This one is a maxim in the site's voice, with an odd article ("a right way") and "and" doing the work of "but."
- fix: Fear is the right place to begin, Auden says, but it is not enough to go on with.
- weight: should

### week13.questions.3.auden.paras.3

- problem: The sentence sets Stubb's pride in his kindness against what he does to Pip, and "and" hides the contrast.
- fix: He is proud of his kindness, yet it is he who leaves Pip alone in the sea, never having really looked at him.
- weight: should

### week13.questions.3.auden.paras.4

### week13.questions.3.auden.paras.5
- where: Ahab's madness turns on the whale, and Pip's
- problem: "Turns on" can mean attacks or depends on, and in week13.questions.4.auden.paras.3 the same phrase plainly means attacks. In the last sentence "Auden says" is lodged inside the condition, and the appositive gives only one half of the exchange.
- fix: Ahab's madness is aimed at the whale, and Pip's at himself. Auden says that both would have been saved if each had had the other's quality, Pip the strength of Ahab and Ahab the humility of Pip. (Use "is aimed at" only if that is the sense intended; if "depends on" is meant, say that.)
- weight: should

### week13.questions.4.auden.paras.1
- where: In the second he turns the sentence round
- problem: The reversal is plain on the page and need not be pointed out, and "turns round" is British idiom. Before it, six short sentences of one shape set out a contrast that two would carry.
- fix: In place of everything after the second sentence: The Dynamo's world is the natural world, made of masses and of recurring events and describable only in numbers. The Virgin's world is the historical world, made of faces and of events that happen only once and describable only in speech. In the first, he says, freedom is the consciousness of necessity, and in the second necessity is the consciousness of freedom.
- weight: could

### week13.questions.4.auden.paras.2
- where: and that religion treats
- problem: "That religion" has nothing to point back to, because only the first false copy has been called a religion. The first half is given as Auden's and the second as fact, and "treating" and "treats" sit side by side in two senses.
- fix: In place of the second, third and fourth sentences: The first takes masses for faces, and its religion, Auden says, prays to the Dynamo. The second takes faces for masses, and its religion makes a statistic of the Virgin.
- weight: could

### week13.questions.4.auden.paras.3

## Patterns

Most frequent first. The counts are for this part only. "After-paper paragraphs" means the 25 entries labelled auden.paras.

1. Short opening sentence. 13 of the 25 after-paper paragraphs open on a sentence of nine words or fewer. Seen at week13.questions.4.auden.paras.2 and week14.questions.1.auden.paras.4. One at a time these are fine. As the opening of every second paragraph they become a drumbeat. Let some paragraphs begin on what is now their second sentence.

2. A sentence of five words or fewer set among long ones for the beat. 12 in the same 25 paragraphs. Seen at week13.questions.3.auden.paras.4 and week13.questions.3.auden.paras.5, which have two each. This is the staccato that calls attention to itself.

3. "Turn" as the working verb. 10 uses in seven phrases (turn back, turn into, turn round, turn on, turn to, turn out, turn joy to sorrow), and "turn on" in two senses. Seen at week13.questions.3.auden.paras.5 and week13.questions.4.auden.paras.3.

4. "And" where the sense is "but." 9. Seen at week13.questions.3.auden.paras.3 and week13.making.1.table.key.rows.1.kind. Inside a sentence "but" joins a contrast once in the whole part (week14.questions.1.auden.paras.4), and "though," "although" and "however" never occur, nor "yet" as a conjunction. It looks like the ban on "not X but Y" carried too far. Four of the nine are dealt with in the findings (week13.questions.1.auden.paras.1, week13.questions.3.auden.paras.2, week13.questions.3.auden.paras.3, week14.questions.2.auden.paras.1). The other five, with the change:
   - week13.questions.1.auden.paras.2: "and does it for" becomes "but for"
   - week13.questions.3.auden.paras.3, the saying of Kafka's: "and that holding back" becomes "but that holding back"
   - week13.making.1.table.key.rows.1.kind: "and cannot" becomes "but cannot"
   - week13.making.1.table.key.rows.7.news: "and does not" becomes "but does not"
   - week14.making.1.translate.after.1: "and gave up" becomes "but gave up"

5. A sentence that only announces the next. 8. Seen at week13.questions.1.auden.paras.1 and week14.questions.2.auden.paras.2. The others are in week13.questions.2.auden.paras.3, week13.questions.3.auden.paras.4, week13.questions.3.auden.paras.5, week13.questions.4.auden.paras.3, week14.questions.1.auden.paras.2 and week14.questions.2.auden.paras.3. Each has a fix in the findings.

6. "Then" as the hinge of the telling. 5, three of them opening a paragraph. Seen at week13.questions.4.auden.paras.3 and week14.questions.2.auden.paras.3. The third opener is week14.questions.1.auden.paras.2; the two inside a paragraph are in week13.questions.2.text and week13.questions.2.auden.paras.2 and can stay.

7. Auden's judgment printed with no verb of saying, so that it stands as the site's. 4. Seen at week13.questions.3.auden.paras.2 and week14.questions.1.auden.paras.2. Also week13.questions.2.auden.paras.1 and the second half of week13.questions.4.auden.paras.2.

## What is clean

No em-dash, no exclamation mark, no rhetorical question in the site's own voice, no hype word, and dates in American form throughout. The reading lists, the test items, the word-list for the translation, the rules of both exercises and the source lines read as a handbook should, apart from a capital letter, a pair of quotation marks and a page range. The trouble is concentrated in the after-paper paragraphs: 26 of the 36 findings are there, and that is where the short declarative sentence has hardened into a manner.
