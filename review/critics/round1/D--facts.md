### week4.questions.2.auden.paras.1
- where: is a short note among some notes on Don Quixote
- problem: Too absolute. The book speaks of Hamlet in other places as well: the copy at research/auden/dh.txt has ten mentions, among them a remark on the soliloquy as a stage convention, Hamlet named as one of Shakespeare's five mythical characters, and the Gravediggers' song. ### week4.making.1.brief.2
- where: his examination on *Hamlet* asked for the meanings
- problem: No paper "on *Hamlet*" is on record. Princeton's and Project MUSE's descriptions of Kirsch's edition (https://muse.jhu.edu/book/67129) say the Saturday classes for credit students went, in the spring term, largely to comment on words and phrases in *Hamlet* and *The Tempest*; the one paper printed there is the fall final. The earlier research note (B_pedagogy.md, 1.N3) has the spring final asking students to identify passages and also to gloss words in *Hamlet*. I could not open Kirsch's pages to confirm it (archive.org, Google Books, MUSE and JSTOR all refused).
- fix: In the spring of 1947, at the New School in New York, the final examination of his course on Shakespeare asked, among other things, what single words in *Hamlet* mean. (In week4.making.1.source.1 write "The spring examination at the New School" for "The examination on *Hamlet*".) Flag: the editor should see Kirsch's sentence before this is printed.
- weight: should

### week4.making.1.rules.2
- where: Use a dictionary that gives the history of a word
- problem: Schmidt's *Lexicon* does not give the history of a word. It lists the senses each word has in Shakespeare, with the places, and no etymology (see any entry under the Perseus link the rule already gives; the link is right and the book is free).
- fix: Use a dictionary that shows what a word meant in Shakespeare's time. The *Oxford English Dictionary* does, and gives the word's history too, if a library lets you use it. Alexander Schmidt's *Shakespeare Lexicon* [link kept] is free and lists the senses each word has in the plays, without their history.
- weight: could

### week5.reading.4.paras.3
- where: The English printed here was made from an Italian version
- problem: Only half true. The book is a libretto for the Italian stage version (its title page names two houses, the Royal Italian Opera in London and the Academy of Music in New York: https://archive.org/details/magicflutediezau00moza), and the English of the recitatives does follow the Italian column. The English of the songs does not. It renders Schikaneder's German line for line: the opening cry for help with its cunning serpent (magic-flute, act-1, block 3) and the Queen's second air, hell's vengeance boiling in her heart (act-2, block 45), where the Italian column of the scan has serpents of hell, Megaera and Alecto. The duet for Pamina and Papageno is the old English singing version from the German too.
- fix: The English printed here comes from a libretto made for the Italian version of the opera, which was given in London and New York in the nineteenth century. That version cuts the spoken scenes short and has them sung, and the English follows it there. The songs are put into English from the German words. You will hear more talk than you read. (The same claim stands in the note on this book elsewhere on the site and wants the same change.)
- weight: must

### week5.reading.4.paras.4
- where: goes through the trials and the other refuses them
- problem: Imprecise as an account of the opera. In Schikaneder's dialogue Papageno says he wants no wisdom and will not fight for it, then agrees to be tried once a wife is promised him, and fails the trial of silence (plot summary of the second act at https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Zauberfl%C3%B6te: unwilling at first, talked round by the priest with the promise of Papagena, then led into speaking; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Magic_Flute has him fail the trials). Mendelson, *Early Auden, Later Auden*, uses the word "refuses" too, but as Auden's argument. So "refuses" is Auden's reading, which the page reports two paragraphs later, and not the plot. The same word is in week5.making.1.table.key.rows.5.who.
- fix: As you listen, set Tamino beside Papageno. Tamino goes through the trials. Papageno says he wants no wisdom, is talked into the first trial and fails it. Notice what each is given at the end. (In the key, let the entry end "and has no wish to be tried" where it now ends with the refusal.)
- weight: could

### week6.questions.2.auden.paras.1

Auden named Kierkegaard again in the poem "A Thanksgiving", written in 1973, as one of three writers who led him back to belief (https://www.christiancentury.org/reviews/2007-09/selected-poems; Mendelson, *Early Auden, Later Auden*, p. 789, on the poem). - fix: Auden wrote about Kierkegaard many times. In 1968 he reviewed volume one of the *Journals and Papers*, and by that time he had doubts.

### week6.questions.4.auden.paras.4
- where: the one theological writer he could read in those years
- problem: Wrong period. Coming after the paragraphs on 1939 and 1940, "those years" means then. Mendelson, the source cited, says the recollection is of Auden's time at school and university, that is the 1920s (*Early Auden, Later Auden*, p. 399; his note gives the essay as the one in *Modern Canterbury Pilgrims*, ed. James A. Pike, 1956). Mendelson also says the essay was written in 1955, where the page and week6.questions.4.auden.sources.3 say 1956, which is the year the book came out.
- fix: He had not come to Pascal fresh. In an essay written in 1955 and published the next year he recalled that at school and at Oxford Pascal was the one writer on theology he found readable, and the one who disturbed his complacency. Looking back, he also found in him [quotation kept], a romantic pleasure in being unhappy. (In the source line: "for the letters and for the essay, published in 1956".)
- weight: must

## Patterns

1. A statement says more about a book, a list or a libretto than is in it (6): week5.reading.4.paras.3, week6.questions.5.auden.paras.1; also week4.questions.2.auden.paras.1, week4.making.1.rules.2, week6.making.1.brief.2, week6.test.items.13.where.
2. A statement rests on a book nobody in this round could open, or on a count nobody made (8): week4.making.1.brief.2, week6.intro.3; also week5.making.1.brief.1, week5.questions.3.auden.paras.1, week6.questions.2.auden.paras.1, week6.questions.4.auden.paras.2, week6.questions.4.auden.sources.1, week6.questions.5.auden.paras.2. Five of the eight hang on books whose scans are closed to search: Kirsch's edition of the Shakespeare lectures, Ansen's *Table Talk*, *The Prolific and the Devourer*, *New Year Letter* and the 1947 Baudelaire. One hour with those five books would settle all five, and the year of the Ecco Press edition with them.
3. A date or a phrase of time puts a remark in the wrong years, or gives the year of a reprint for the year of first printing (2): week6.questions.4.auden.paras.4, week5.questions.1.auden.paras.3.
4. Auden's reading of a plot, or a loose pronoun, stands where the plain fact of the play or opera should (2): week5.reading.4.paras.4, week5.test.items.2.q.

Not a fault, but worth knowing: every one of the 42 test questions was run against the site's own copy of the book, and each accepted answer and each place given is right, with the one qualification under week6.test.items.13.where. The key to the sixteen words has no error of sense or of etymology.
