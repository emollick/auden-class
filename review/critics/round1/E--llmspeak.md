# E, weeks 7 to 9: writing that reads as machine-made

Findings are in the order of the text. Where an entry has more than one fault, its label is repeated.

## Week 7

### week7.intro.1
- where: A play in verse, and the first three books
- problem: The entry opens on a sentence with no verb. All three weeks in this part open that way (week8.intro.1, week9.intro.1), so it reads as a tic. This one also leaves the play unnamed.
- fix: This week you read a verse play, *Peer Gynt*, and Books I to III of *The Brothers Karamazov*, the longest novel on the list. The novel takes four weeks.
- weight: should

### week7.intro.2
- where: is his mother telling him that he lies
- problem: "his" has only a book title to refer to. The second sentence is a clipped echo of the first (lies, lying) and says less than it could: "about lying" does not tell the student what he will do.
- fix: *Peer Gynt* opens with Peer's mother telling him that he lies. For this week's exercise you write a story in which every sentence is a lie.
- weight: should

### week7.reading.3.auden.paras.1
- where: The world has the unwelcome part of correcting it
- problem: "Has the unwelcome part of" is a stiff, mock-old idiom, and "and" joins two things the sentence means to oppose.
- fix: The world plays the unwelcome role of correcting that picture, while two lovers confirm it for each other.
- weight: should

### week7.reading.3.auden.paras.1
- where: Wagner, he told the class, understood the psychology
- problem: The last "he" in the paragraph was the romantic lover. Here it is Auden, who has not been named since the first sentence.
- fix: Auden told the class that Wagner understood the psychology of romantic love perfectly.
- weight: should

### week7.reading.3.auden.paras.2
- where: we see a Wagnerian tenor and soprano
- problem: Coy. The argument turns on what such singers look like, and the sentence does not say it. A student who has never seen one loses the point.
- fix: On the stage the lovers are a Wagnerian tenor and soprano, who are seldom young or good-looking, so nobody can suppose that their passion has anything to do with looks. (Check the added clause against the pages cited before using it.)
- weight: could

### week7.reading.3.auden.paras.3
- where: Their passion is not for each other. It is
- problem: A denial and then its correction, set as two short sentences. This is the turn the house rules forbid, and the cadence comes back in week7.questions.2.auden.paras.4 and week9.questions.2.auden.paras.1.
- fix: What each of them wants is an end to being a separate person at all, and the other is the means to it.
- weight: should

### week7.reading.3.auden.paras.4
- where: It is Brangäne and Kurwenal who bring everything about
- problem: A cleft sentence built for emphasis, with a predicate that says nothing. The student is not told what the two servants do.
- fix: flag only. Replace "bring everything about" with what the essay says they do (the pages cited, 120–123). Brangäne's handing over of the potion is the plain instance. Write it as a statement: "Brangäne and Kurwenal, he says, ..."
- weight: should

### week7.reading.3.auden.paras.4
- where: They are sane dreamers, and so the lovers
- problem: The entry closes on an aphorism. The step from "sane" to "doomed" is left out, so the sentence sounds wise and explains nothing.
- fix: Because the two dreamers are sane, he says, they know that no such love could last in the world they live in, and so in the dream the lovers have to die. (Check the middle clause against the pages cited.)
- weight: should

### week7.words.setting
- where: telling his mother what happened to him
- problem: The week is about Peer's lying, and the site here reports his tall story as fact in its own voice.
- fix: Make the first sentence "Peer Gynt is telling his mother a story about what happened to him on the mountain." Begin the second with "In the story," and leave the rest as it is.
- weight: could

### week7.test.items.11.where
- where: The Confession of a Passionate Heart
- problem: The chapter title carries an em-dash, the only one in the whole site. It is the translator's; the house rule has no exception for titles.
- fix: flag only. Either let the translator's punctuation stand as quoted matter and note the exception, or print the title with a comma before its last two words.
- weight: should

### week7.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: On the stage, acting stands for making.
- problem: The paragraph closes on a seven-word maxim that restates its fourth sentence. It is there for the sound of an ending.
- fix: Cut the last sentence. Put its point in the sentence that begins "So he shows": after "as a part" add ", so that acting stands on the stage for making". The paragraph then ends on the slave-trader and the prophet, which is the concrete case.
- weight: should

### week7.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: The dreamer and the madman are his neighbors
- problem: Gnomic opening. "His" has no noun yet (the poet arrives a sentence later), and "neighbors" is a figure the paragraph does not explain.
- fix: The poet resembles the dreamer and the madman, except that those two are in earnest. He pretends for fun and knows that he is pretending.
- weight: should

### week7.questions.1.auden.paras.4
- where: This gives the mottoes an odd turn.
- problem: A signpost: it announces that something odd is coming and does not say what. It also leans on "the mottoes," which this paragraph never states.
- fix: The motto the play gives to men is to be oneself. If a man is called to be an actor, he can keep it only by pretending to be what he is not.
- weight: should

### week7.questions.1.auden.paras.4
- where: It may be that the poet must pay
- problem: The paragraph ends on a hedged sentence that belongs to nobody (Auden, Ibsen or the site), and on an image, the ladle, that it has not explained.
- fix: Join it to the sentence before: "Auden says Ibsen leaves it in doubt whether she is right, and that the poet may have to pay for his calling by being melted down in the Button-moulder's ladle."
- weight: should

### week7.questions.1.auden.paras.5
- where: He adds a complaint. The play is fascinating
- problem: A four-word announcing sentence, and then two evaluative adjectives stated as fact in the site's voice. The attribution hangs on the announcement alone.
- fix: He complains that the play, though fascinating, is badly built.
- weight: should

### week7.questions.1.auden.paras.5
- where: where the scenes could be any number
- problem: The phrase does not parse at first reading. It means that the number of scenes is arbitrary.
- fix: where there could as well be more scenes or fewer
- weight: should

### week7.questions.2.gloss.1
- where: Later in the term he sent the class home
- problem: In the sentence before, "he" is Pearce. Here it is Auden, with nothing to mark the change.
- fix: Later in the term Auden sent the class home with the order [quotation kept]
- weight: should

### week7.questions.2.auden.paras.3
- where: They trust too much in their own powers.
- problem: Six clipped sentences in two matched sets of three, after the tag "and Auden lists the reasons." The staccato calls attention to itself. Two sentences can carry the same matter.
- fix: Keep the first sentence. Then: "He passes a test that his brothers fail. In Auden's account the brothers pass the dwarf by because he does not look useful, trust too much in their own powers, and want their father's reward without loving him. The hero, he says, is humble enough to ask for help, cares whether his father gets well, and is well disposed toward everyone he meets."
- weight: should

### week7.questions.2.auden.paras.4
- where: Nobody succeeds by will alone. He needs luck
- problem: Three faults. The paragraph opens on a signpost ("draws a rule from it"). The rule comes as a denial and then its correction in two sentences. "He" refers to "Nobody." After the signpost no sentence is attributed, so the moral at the end reads as the site preaching.
- fix: Auden's rule is that a person's real strength lies less often in the powers he is proud of than in those he thinks unimportant or takes for weaknesses. Will alone is never enough, he says: everyone also needs luck, or grace, which anyone humble enough to ask may have.
- weight: should

### week7.making.1.brief.2
- where: Peer Gynt would have found it easy.
- problem: A quip set down as the opening sentence. It winks at the student and leaves the link to the play unstated.
- fix: Cut the sentence and begin: "Writing about *Peer Gynt*, Auden says that the poet asserts his freedom by lying, that is, by making worlds he knows to be imaginary."
- weight: could

## Week 8

### week8.intro.1
- where: Four books of the novel.
- problem: A verbless opener again (see week7.intro.1), and this one only repeats the line printed above it, which already names Books IV to VII.
- fix: Cut the sentence. Begin with "Book V has the two chapters".
- weight: should

### week8.reading.2.auden.paras.2
- where: He gives the reason. An idol is something I
- problem: After a four-word signpost the site speaks as "I" for the rest of the paragraph, with no quotation marks. The reader cannot tell whose "I" it is, and the site is not meant to speak as Auden. The same slide occurs in week9.reading.2.auden.paras.1 and week9.questions.2.auden.paras.2.
- fix: His reason is that an idol is something a person makes responsible for his existence so that he need not answer for himself. The relation can run only one way: an idol that turns round and asks its worshipper to be responsible for it has stopped being an idol.
- weight: must

### week8.reading.2.auden.paras.4
- where: The next page gives the cost.
- problem: Another short announcing sentence before the sentence that says the thing. Harmless alone; it is one of eleven of its kind flagged in this part.
- fix: On the next page he gives the cost: a free place is also a lonely one, and whoever goes there will long for home and company.
- weight: could

### week8.questions.1.gloss.1
- where: and what Ivan calls it
- problem: The hint says that Ivan calls "it" a poem, and one sentence later tells the student to notice what Ivan calls "it." The first "it" is Ivan's story and the second is what Alyosha does. A student will read the instruction as already answered.
- fix: Book V, Chapter 5. Ivan calls his story a poem. When it is over, notice what Alyosha does, and what name Ivan gives the act.
- weight: must

### week8.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: it is not the state of being equally free
- problem: The entry stops on a denial. The student is told what freedom is not, in Auden's reply, and never what it is. It reads as a paraphrase cut off halfway, and the last thing shown under the question is a negative.
- fix: flag only. Add one sentence from pp. 50–51 giving the positive half of the reply, and fold the denial into the sentence before it: "The reply grants that freedom is what is offered, though not a state in which a man is equally free to do anything. It is, he says, ..."
- weight: must

### week8.questions.2.auden.paras.1
- where: Two things he said elsewhere bear on it.
- problem: The sentence repeats the heading printed directly above it almost word for word.
- fix: cut. The heading already gives "The first" and "The second" something to count from.
- weight: should

### week8.questions.2.auden.paras.2
- where: He asks to understand first.
- problem: A five-word closing sentence that makes the site's own link between the definition and Ivan by setting them side by side. The student has to guess that the link is the site's and what it is.
- fix: Join the last two sentences and say the link outright: after "with his own eyes" continue ", which is to ask to understand before he will believe".
- weight: could

### week8.questions.2.auden.paras.3
- where: is tempted in particular ways
- problem: An announcing sentence with an empty adjective. "Particular" tells the student nothing; the number does.
- fix: Such a man, he says, has two temptations.
- weight: could

### week8.making.1.brief.2
- where: for the hardest speech in the novel
- problem: A superlative offered as fact, with nothing behind it. It is the one piece of salesmanship in the week.
- fix: Do the second of these for the Inquisitor's speech. The chapter is twenty-six pages long. State his case in no more than two hundred words.
- weight: should

## Week 9

### week9.intro.1
- where: The night of the murder, the drive to Mokroe
- problem: The third verbless opener in three weeks (see week7.intro.1). Here it is a bare list of four events.
- fix: Give the list a subject and a verb. Begin "Books VIII and IX cover" and let the four items follow as they stand.
- weight: should

### week9.reading.2.auden.paras.1
- where: The moment you were asked to mark is Auden's
- problem: The opening sentence calls the moment an example before the student knows what it is an example of. Seven sentences later "Auden's instance is" makes the same point again.
- fix: Cut the first sentence and open with the second. Change the later sentence to: "His instance is the moment you were asked to mark: [quotation kept] that go with Germont to Violetta's deathbed."
- weight: should

### week9.reading.2.auden.paras.1
- where: I take an enemy for a friend
- problem: The site says "I" in Auden's person, without quotation marks, in the middle of a third-person report (see week8.reading.2.auden.paras.2).
- fix: Put it in the third person. Begin the sentence "Someone takes" in place of its first two words and leave the rest.
- weight: should

### week9.reading.2.auden.paras.2
- where: The same notes say what opera imitates.
- problem: The paragraph above ends on the notes the orchestra plays for Germont. "The same notes" is read at first as those notes, and the misreading even makes sense. The sentence is also a question set up for the next one to answer, with "imitates" said twice.
- fix: The same essay says that opera imitates human willfulness.
- weight: must

### week9.reading.2.auden.paras.2
- where: We do not only have feelings, we insist
- problem: A comma splice carrying a "not only X, Y" turn, in a first person plural that is neither quoted nor attributed.
- fix: People, he says, insist on having their feelings, whatever those feelings cost them.
- weight: should

### week9.reading.2.auden.paras.2
- where: as a novel does, people who might be good
- problem: The phrase after the comma is tacked on with no word to join it, and the reader has to go back to see that it explains "character."
- fix: So opera cannot show characters as a novel does, as people who could turn out good or bad and who are acted on as much as they act.
- weight: could

### week9.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: about a murder does the opposite
- problem: Two of the four sentences in this paragraph are announcements: this one, and the lead-in to the quotation. The lead-in is needed. This one holds the point back until the third sentence.
- fix: A work of art about a murder, he says, makes the reader identify with the murderer, against the reader's wish. His example is *Crime and Punishment*. Then the lead-in and the quotation as they stand.
- weight: could

### week9.questions.2.auden.paras.1
- where: The same essay has an account of this kind
- problem: "The same" points to an essay named only under the other question. A student who wrote on this question alone may have seen no essay to refer back to.
- fix: Auden's essay on detective stories has an account of this kind of guilt, which he gives as a confession of his own. (Give the essay's title after "stories" as it is given in the source line.)
- weight: should

### week9.questions.2.auden.paras.1
- where: It is having them that makes him feel guilty.
- problem: Three short sentences in a row (he can, he cannot, a cleft to finish) where one sentence holds the thought. The plainness has become a pose here.
- fix: To some degree he can refuse to act on the desires that tempt him, though he cannot keep from having them, and the having is what makes him feel guilty.
- weight: should

### week9.questions.2.auden.paras.2
- where: tells me to choose the good ones and reject
- problem: "Me" and, two sentences on, "I" are Auden's first person carried into the site's own sentences without quotation marks (see week8.reading.2.auden.paras.2).
- fix: Put both in the third person: "and tells a man to choose the good ones", and "However good he becomes, Auden says,".
- weight: should

### week9.questions.2.auden.paras.3
- where: He does not ask for it.
- problem: The paragraph closes on a six-word sentence that turns the phrase before it round, for the effect of an ending. These last three sentences are also the site's reading of Mitya, set where the student expects Auden's, with nothing to say so.
- fix: Make it an instruction, as the other entries do when the site speaks for itself: "Apply this to Mitya, who says the blow was not his. If so, the daydream is open to him, with the guilt carried off by whoever struck it. Notice that he refuses it."
- weight: should

### week9.questions.2.auden.paras.4
- where: Auden set a verse of Paul at the head
- problem: The entry ends on a line of scripture dropped in without a word on why it is there. It is a fact about the essay used as a curtain line.
- fix: flag only. Either move the sentence up to the first paragraph, where the essay is introduced, or say what it bears on, for example: "The verse of Paul that Auden set over the essay says the same of the law: [quotation kept]"
- weight: could

### week9.making.1.answer.after.1
- where: through as one sentence of argument
- problem: An odd coinage. The four stanzas are not one sentence, and "sentence of argument" is not a thing a student can look for.
- fix: Change the first sentence to "Read the four stanzas through as a single argument." Leave the other two as they are.
- weight: should

## Patterns

Most frequent first. Counts are of the places flagged above. Nearly all of them are in the paragraphs that report Auden; the instructions, test questions and source lines are clean.

1. Announcing sentences: 11. A short sentence, some of only four words, says that a reason, a rule, a complaint or a cost is coming, and the next sentence gives it. Seen in week7.questions.1.auden.paras.5 and week8.reading.2.auden.paras.2. Also flagged in week7.questions.1.auden.paras.4, week7.questions.2.auden.paras.3 and .4, week8.reading.2.auden.paras.4, week8.questions.2.auden.paras.1 and .3, week9.reading.2.auden.paras.1 and .2, week9.questions.1.auden.paras.3. Four milder ones were left alone because they carry some matter (week8.reading.2.auden.paras.1, week8.questions.1.auden.paras.3, week9.questions.1.auden.paras.3 before the quotation, week9.questions.2.auden.paras.2). The fix is the same each time: fold the announcement into the sentence it announces.

2. A pronoun or a "the same" with nothing clear to point to: 8. Seen in week7.reading.3.auden.paras.1 and week8.questions.1.gloss.1. Also week7.intro.2, week7.questions.1.auden.paras.3, week7.questions.2.gloss.1, week7.questions.2.auden.paras.4, week9.reading.2.auden.paras.2, week9.questions.2.auden.paras.1. Short sentences make this worse, because each new sentence starts again with "he" or "it."

3. Odd, vague or coy wording where a plain word was wanted: 7. Seen in week7.reading.3.auden.paras.4 and week9.making.1.answer.after.1. Also week7.reading.3.auden.paras.1 and .2, week7.questions.1.auden.paras.5, week8.questions.2.auden.paras.3, week9.reading.2.auden.paras.2.

4. Endings that pose: 7. A paragraph or entry closes on a maxim, a turned phrase, a bare quotation or a denial. Seen in week7.questions.1.auden.paras.2 and week9.questions.2.auden.paras.3. Also week7.reading.3.auden.paras.4, week7.questions.1.auden.paras.4, week8.questions.1.auden.paras.3, week8.questions.2.auden.paras.2, week9.questions.2.auden.paras.4.

5. Whose voice: 6. An evaluation, a moral or the site's own reading is stated flat, with no sign of whether Auden or the site holds it. Seen in week7.questions.1.auden.paras.5 and week8.making.1.brief.2. Also week7.words.setting, week7.questions.1.auden.paras.4, week7.questions.2.auden.paras.4, week9.questions.2.auden.paras.3.

6. Clipped runs where the plainness has become a manner: 6. Seen in week7.questions.2.auden.paras.3 and week9.questions.2.auden.paras.1. Also week7.intro.2, week7.reading.3.auden.paras.3, week7.questions.2.auden.paras.4, week9.reading.2.auden.paras.2. By my count the running prose of this part has 218 sentences with a mean of 13.7 words. One sentence in four has eight words or fewer, and one in twelve has twenty-five or more. Eleven paragraphs of four or more sentences average under thirteen words a sentence.

7. A denial, then its correction in a second short sentence: 5. Seen in week7.reading.3.auden.paras.3 and week7.questions.2.auden.paras.4. Also week9.reading.2.auden.paras.2 (as "not only"), week9.questions.2.auden.paras.1 (as "can" and "cannot"), and week8.questions.1.auden.paras.3, where the correction never comes. In each case the contrast is Auden's and should stay. What reads as machine-made is the two-beat delivery.

8. Auden's first person taken over without quotation marks: 4. Seen in week8.reading.2.auden.paras.2 and week9.reading.2.auden.paras.1. Also week9.questions.2.auden.paras.2, and as "we" in week9.reading.2.auden.paras.2. The "we" in week7.reading.3.auden.paras.2 has "he says" on both sides of it and was left alone.

9. Week introductions that open without a verb: 3 of 3. Seen in week7.intro.1 and week9.intro.1; the third is week8.intro.1.

## Seen and left alone

- The standing instruction for each opera (read the libretto, hear it through, the running time, then "While you listen") is the same in all three weeks. It is a rubric, and a student is helped by its being the same.
- House-rule marks. One em-dash, flagged above. One exclamation mark, inside a quotation in week7.questions.2.gloss.1. One question outside the set questions, in week7.questions.1.auden.paras.1, where it is reported as the question the play asks. I found no breach in dates, in dashes within page ranges, or in American spelling.
- Titles of books are not in italics in the week titles and the lines under them. That holds for all fifteen weeks, so I took it as the way headings are set. The editor should confirm that it is meant.
- The lines that say where a test passage comes from take two forms: part, book and chapter with their names on the items that ask who speaks, and bare book and chapter numbers on the rest. Both are clear. It is a choice for the editor, outside this lens.
