# D, weeks 4 to 6: writing that reads as machine-made

Fixes are given as replacement sentences or as edit instructions. Where a fix keeps a quotation or a title that the text prints in quotation marks, it is shown as [quotation kept] or [title kept].

## Week 4

### week4.intro.1
- where: Three plays. The list has only
- problem: A verbless count stands as the first sentence, and the same opening move begins weeks 5 and 6. The heading above it already names the three plays. The clause about the lecture being written down names no writer.
- fix: Cut the two-word opening. Then: "The list names only the second part of *Henry IV*. In Donald Pearce's notes the lecture also covers the first part." (Editor to confirm that Pearce is the writer meant.)
- weight: should

### week4.intro.2
- where: is one that Auden set the class himself
- problem: The hint printed under question 1 says the same thing in its first sentence, so the student reads it twice. The frame "is one that" adds words and no fact.
- fix: cut
- weight: could

### week4.questions.1.auden.paras.1
- where: wrote down what he could afterwards
- problem: House rule is American English, which prefers "afterward". The same form is in week5.questions.1.auden.paras.1.
- fix: Change "afterwards" to "afterward" in both entries.
- weight: could

### week4.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: and Auden made something of that
- problem: The clause announces a point and does not give it. Before it and with it are four sentences in a row that open with "He", and the last two sentences of the paragraph drop the attribution so that the site seems to assert them itself.
- fix: Replace the paragraph with: "Auden said that Iago is cold: he cannot love other people, and has made a strength of it. He is the one brilliant person in the play, with what Pascal calls the geometrical mind, solving the problems of life by intellect alone. He alone in the play shows a knowledge of the Bible. From this Auden argued that Iago is a saint turned inside out, whose real passion was for sainthood, and that in saint and villain alike ethics and aesthetics have nearly become one thing."
- weight: should

### week4.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: What the two share is distance from the ethical
- problem: A "what X is Y" frame carrying an abstract noun phrase. A student cannot tell from it what Iago and Falstaff actually do.
- fix: "The two are alike, Auden said, in leaving right and wrong out of their dealings with people."
- weight: should

### week4.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: Iago's detachment is the scientist's and Falstaff's is
- problem: A mirrored pair of possessives, with no attribution, standing as its own sentence after a sentence that it only finishes.
- fix: Join it to the sentence before: "Each keeps life at arm's length without seeming to, Iago in the manner of a scientist and Falstaff in the manner of a playwright."
- weight: could

### week4.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: The Falstaff of these lectures is no hero
- problem: A short sentence placed for effect, in litotes, saying less than the sentence after it.
- fix: Cut the sentence, and begin the next one "In these lectures Falstaff is an opportunist" in place of "He is an opportunist".
- weight: could

### week4.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: who is cold as Iago is cold
- problem: An echo construction with an antique ring. The same turn comes back in week5.questions.1.auden.paras.4, where the word repeated is "innocent".
- fix: "who is as cold as Iago"
- weight: should

### week4.questions.1.auden.paras.4
- where: He did not keep this view of Falstaff
- problem: A paragraph that opens "He did not" and then gives the later view. The same frame opens week5.questions.1.auden.paras.3 and week6.questions.4.auden.paras.4. Three uses make it a formula.
- fix: Keep this one, which is the plainest. Change the other two as shown at those entries.
- weight: should

### week4.questions.1.auden.paras.4
- where: Iago he came to describe as a practical joker
- problem: The object is moved to the front for rhythm. A handbook would use the plain order.
- fix: "He came to describe Iago as a practical joker", with the rest of the sentence unchanged.
- weight: could

### week4.questions.2.auden.paras.1
- where: is a short note among some notes on
- problem: A "what X is Y" frame again, and "a note among some notes" is clumsy. The sentence is a signpost that could carry its one fact in half the words.
- fix: "In *The Dyer's Hand* Auden gives Hamlet one short note, placed among his notes on Don Quixote."
- weight: should

### week4.questions.2.auden.paras.2
- where: whose mother married his uncle, who murdered his father
- problem: Two relative clauses are stacked, and the reader has to stop and work out who killed whom.
- fix: "he is the man whose uncle murdered his father and then married his mother."
- weight: should

### week4.questions.2.auden.paras.4
- where: Hamlet would change places with him
- problem: The closing sentence repeats what the second paragraph has said, that Hamlet would like to be what the Greek hero is, and turns it into a neat last line.
- fix: Cut the second sentence. End the entry on the comparison with week 2.
- weight: should

### week4.questions.3.auden.paras.1
- where: sets the two kinds of tragedy side by side
- problem: The sentence announces a comparison before the two kinds have been named. "Sets beside" or "side by side" is used five times in these three weeks to introduce a comparison, and this is the first. Later in the paragraph a five-word sentence does nothing but point to the play.
- fix: "In an essay on the Elizabethan stage Auden says that in a Greek tragedy everything that could have gone otherwise is over before the play begins, while in an Elizabethan tragedy the hero can turn back up to the last moment. His example is *Othello*:" and run on into the last sentence, which stays as it is, with a small letter on its first word.
- weight: should

### week4.questions.3.auden.paras.2
- where: he is harder on the Moor
- problem: "The Moor" is elegant variation for a name used in the same sentence. The paragraph then ends on a five-word sentence set apart for effect.
- fix: First sentence: "In his essay on *Othello* he is harder on Othello himself." Last two sentences as one: "So we pity him without respecting him, and our respect, Auden says, goes to Iago."
- weight: should

### week4.making.1.title
- where: Sixteen words in Hamlet
- problem: House rule: titles of plays and operas in italics. This heading has none, and the same is true of week5.making.1.title and week5.making.1.table.key.title. The week titles and subheadings are also plain.
- fix: flag only. If headings can carry italics, set the titles of the plays and of the opera in italics. If they cannot, leave them, and the editor should note the exception.
- weight: could

### week4.making.1.brief.2
- where: Each has changed since Shakespeare used it
- problem: Two clipped sentences where one would do, and it is the sense that has changed, which the sentence does not say.
- fix: "Below are sixteen words from the play whose meaning is no longer what it was in Shakespeare's time."
- weight: could

### week4.making.1.table.key.rows.13.from
- where: What does not change goes on
- problem: A gnomic half-sentence in a dictionary note. It sounds like a proverb and does not show the steps by which the sense moved.
- fix: "Old English *stille*, at rest. The sense passed from motionless to unchanging to continual, and so the adverb came to mean always."
- weight: should

### week4.making.1.table.key.rows.16.from
- where: fine, showy as well as bold
- problem: Five items divided only by commas. The reader cannot see where the foreign words stop and their meanings start.
- fix: "Italian *bravo* and French *brave*, which meant fine or showy as well as bold."
- weight: could

## Week 5

### week5.intro.1
- where: first acted within six years of each other
- problem: The whole entry is a fragment with no verb, and it is the second week to open on a count of the books. The fact about six years gives the student nothing to do or to look for.
- fix: cut. The subheading already lists the reading, and week5.intro.2 can open the week.
- weight: should

### week5.intro.2
- where: the mathematical mind and the intuitive mind
- problem: Week 4 reports the same lecture and calls this the geometrical mind. A student will not know that the two names are one thing, and the cross-reference this sentence makes then fails.
- fix: flag only. Use one name in both weeks. The Pascal printed in the site's reader has "mathematical", so keep this entry and change the week 4 sentence. If Pearce's notes have "geometrical", week 4 can read "what Pascal calls the mathematical, or geometrical, mind".
- weight: should

### week5.reading.4.auden.paras.1

### week5.reading.4.auden.paras.2

### week5.questions.1.auden.paras.1

### week5.questions.1.auden.paras.3

### week5.questions.1.auden.paras.3

- fix: "Prospero pardons them like a man who has his enemies at his mercy."

### week5.questions.1.auden.paras.3

### week5.questions.1.auden.paras.4

### week5.questions.2.auden.paras.4

### week5.questions.3.auden.paras.1
- where: twice in his collected prose, and both times

### week5.questions.3.auden.paras.2
- where: He allows each a use.
- problem: The sentence promises a use for Pascal's term and a use for Kierkegaard's. Only the first follows, and the paragraph ends. The reader is left waiting for the second.
- fix: flag only. Either add one sentence giving the use Auden allows Kierkegaard's term, taken from the same page of the essay, or change this sentence to "He allows the wager one use." and begin the next with "It reminds people".
- weight: must

### week5.questions.3.auden.paras.3
- where: He is thankful for that. Had he known
- problem: Five short sentences in a row after the quotation, so that the paragraph taps. "Had little sense of" is a roundabout way to say he did not know.
- fix: "Looking back at the three or four choices that decided his own life, he says that at the time he did not know how serious they were." Keep the sentence about the brook as it stands. Then: "He is thankful that he did not know: had he seen the risk, he says, he would not have dared."
- weight: should

### week5.making.1.brief.2

### week5.making.1.table.key.intro.1

### week6.intro.1

### week6.intro.2

### week6.questions.2.auden.paras.3

### week6.questions.2.auden.paras.4

### week6.questions.3.auden.paras.1

### week6.questions.3.auden.paras.2

### week6.questions.3.auden.paras.3

### week6.questions.3.auden.paras.3

## Patterns

Most frequent first. Counts are for weeks 4 to 6 only.

1. Impersonal "one" in the paraphrases of Auden: 8. It is his idiom and reads as imitation of his voice. See week5.questions.1.auden.paras.3 and week6.questions.3.auden.paras.1.
2. Attribution that lapses after the first sentence of a paragraph shown after the paper, so that Auden's judgments read as the site's own: 7 paragraphs. See week4.questions.1.auden.paras.2 and week6.questions.3.auden.paras.2.
3. Short sentence set at the end of a paragraph or entry for effect: 5. See week4.questions.3.auden.paras.2 and week6.questions.2.auden.paras.1.
4. "Sets X beside Y" or "side by side" to introduce a comparison: 5, and "set against" or "set above" 3 more. See week4.questions.3.auden.paras.1 and week6.questions.5.auden.paras.4.
5. A command used as a condition ("Take", "Show", "Let", followed by "and"): 5. See week5.questions.2.auden.paras.1 and week6.questions.3.auden.paras.3.
6. "X and not Y" or "X, not Y": 5. Not every one is a fault: the one in week5.making.1.rules.2 is a real instruction. See week5.questions.2.auden.paras.4 and week6.questions.2.auden.paras.3.
7. British forms under an American house rule: 4 ("afterwards" twice, "dykes", "get round"). See week5.questions.1.auden.paras.1 and week6.questions.3.auden.paras.3. "Plough" in week6.making.1.rules.4 is not counted: the Blake printed in the reader spells it so, and the rule should match the text.
8. Runs of four or more short sentences of one shape: 4. See week4.questions.1.auden.paras.2 and week6.making.1.brief.2.
9. Sentence that begins with "So" to draw the conclusion: 4. Each is defensible alone, and together they are a habit. See week4.questions.2.auden.paras.2 and week6.questions.3.auden.paras.2.
10. A week that opens on a count of the books, in a fragment: 3 of 3. See week4.intro.1 and week6.intro.1.
11. Paragraph that opens "He did not" to turn to Auden's later view: 3. See week5.questions.1.auden.paras.3 and week6.questions.4.auden.paras.4.
12. Comparative pivot ("he is harder", "he is blunter", "he goes further"): 3. See week4.questions.3.auden.paras.2 and week6.questions.3.auden.paras.3.
13. Figure of turning over ("turned inside out", "turned upside down", "turn it over"): 3. See week4.questions.1.auden.paras.2 and week6.questions.5.auden.paras.2.
14. Claim of a complete count of Auden's writing ("twice in his collected prose", "the last time"): 2. See week5.questions.3.auden.paras.1 and week6.questions.2.auden.paras.1.
15. Echo construction (the adjective repeated after "as" or "in the way"): 2. See week4.questions.1.auden.paras.3 and week5.questions.1.auden.paras.4.
16. "What X is Y" frame: 2. See week4.questions.1.auden.paras.3 and week4.questions.2.auden.paras.1.
17. Vague "once" where the source is known: 2. See week6.intro.3 and week6.making.1.brief.1.
18. "Compare" with one object: 2. See week5.questions.1.auden.paras.1 and week6.questions.3.auden.paras.2.

House rules otherwise kept in this part: no em-dashes, no exclamation marks, no rhetorical questions outside the set questions, dates in American form, en-dashes only in page ranges.

What is sound and should be left alone: the instructions in the exercises, the test questions, the notes on the operas, the settings for the passages, the source lines, and the plain statements of what is the site's own choice.
