# H, week 15: findings of the student critic

### week15.sub
- where: Eliot: The Family Reunion
- problem: The week also sets Wagner's Götterdämmerung: a libretto of 58 pages and about four and a half hours of listening. The title, this line and the three opening paragraphs never name it, so a student who plans the week from the top of the page leaves out its longest single task.
- fix: If no week names its opera in this line, leave the line and add a fourth opening paragraph: "The opera is Wagner's *Götterdämmerung*, the last and the longest of the nine. Read its libretto, then allow four and a half hours to hear it."
- weight: should

### week15.reading.3.paras.3
- where: each tell part of what has gone before
- problem: The paragraph promises that the opera fills in the story, then names three tellers the student has never met and does not say who they are. It gives no word of the story itself, and the first stage direction of the libretto already points back to an earlier opera. A student new to the *Ring* is lost in the Prelude.
- fix: "You are reading the end of a story, so here is its beginning. The dwarf Alberich stole gold from the Rhine and made of it a ring that gives power over the world. Wotan, the chief of the gods, took the ring from him by a trick, and Alberich cursed it. Siegfried, a hero who knows no fear, now holds the ring, and has won Brünnhilde, Wotan's daughter. The opera tells this again as it goes: the three Norns, who spin the rope of fate, in the Prelude; Waltraute, Brünnhilde's sister, in Act 1; and Siegfried himself in Act 3." The editor should check the summary against the libretto.
- weight: should

### week15.reading.3.auden.paras.1
- where: and the giants a strong and stupid working class
- problem: The student has heard only the last of the four operas. No giant appears in it, one dwarf has a single scene, and the chief gods never come on to sing. The reading cannot be matched to anything just heard, and the pair who are to deliver the country are not named.
- fix: After the sentence on the historian, write: "In the three earlier operas the gods rule from a fortress that the giants built for them, and the dwarfs dig gold under the earth. On this reading the gods stand for a reactionary nobility, the dwarfs for a middle class greedy for money, and the giants for a working class, strong and stupid. Siegfried, the hero without fear, and Brünnhilde, the emancipated woman, are to free the country from all three."
- weight: should

### week15.reading.3.auden.paras.3
- where: What is odd about these people is that they
- problem: The words point back to the sentence before, which is about people in any tragedy, and the student first takes them that way. They mean Wagner's characters. The example of Oedipus then comes after the point it is there to prepare.
- fix: Keep the first sentence. Then: "In a tragedy people make mistakes and pay for them afterwards, and as a rule they do not know what they are doing. Oedipus does not, though the audience does. Wagner's people are odd because they know beforehand that what they are about to do is a mistake."
- weight: should

### week15.reading.3.auden.paras.4
- where: say the same and give it a name
- problem: The pronoun has no clear owner, and the name turns out to be a heresy that is defined only by a quotation in a philosopher's terms. Nothing puts the definition into plain words, so the student cannot tell what the two kinds of necessity are or what they have to do with the opera just heard. This is Auden's main charge against Wagner and it cannot be followed.
- fix: flag only for the gloss, which has to come from the note itself. The shape wanted: "The notes to *New Year Letter*, published that spring, give this habit of mind a name, the Romantic heresy, and define it: [quotation kept]. In plain words, [one sentence from the editor saying what each kind of necessity is, with one character of this opera as the instance]."
- weight: must

### week15.reading.3.auden.paras.4
- where: Another note explains the word.
- problem: Two terms have just been introduced, and the student has to read on to learn which one is meant. The definitions that follow are never tied back to Wagner, so the verdict on the operas stays unexplained, and the listening task the student was given (who knows what is coming, and what they do about it) is left without its answer. The source list gives the line number of one note only.
- fix: Put the definitions before the verdict and draw the link: "Another note separates two things. A tribulation is a trouble that a person can only endure. A temptation is a conflict that he has to settle by an act of will. Wagner's characters, on this account, meet every conflict as if it were the first kind: they know what is coming and they bear it. Hence the note's verdict: as studies in tribulation the operas are masterpieces, and as plots they are absurd." The editor must confirm that the fourth sentence is Auden's meaning, and add the second note's line number to the sources.
- weight: should

### week15.reading.3.auden.paras.5
- where: and what Wagner wrote was grand opera
- problem: The paragraph leans on three things the student has not met: the term for the older, Parisian kind of opera, *Die Meistersinger*, and Verdi's *Un Ballo in Maschera*. None is on the list and none is explained, so the closing comparison tells the student nothing. The year 1969 cannot be matched to the source list, which gives the essay no date.
- fix: Rewrite the second half: "From *Die Meistersinger* (1868) on, Auden says, Wagner dropped the theory and wrote grand opera, the older kind with set numbers, big choruses and stage spectacle. This opera has drugged drinks, a massed chorus and three conspirators who swear an oath together. Auden agrees with Bernard Shaw that it is close kin to Verdi's *Un Ballo in Maschera* (1859), an Italian opera of plotters and an oath." Add the date of the essay to its source line. The editor should check the two dates.
- weight: should

## Patterns

Each finding is counted once, under its main fault.

1. An instruction, rule or hint that cannot be followed as written, or that leaves out what the student needs before starting (13). See week15.questions.4.gloss.1 and week15.making.1.rules.3.
2. A name, a term or a work brought in with nothing to say who or what it is, often from books the student was not set (12). See week15.reading.3.paras.3 and week15.questions.3.auden.paras.3.
3. A pronoun or a short phrase with two possible owners or meanings (8). See week15.reading.3.paras.2 and week15.questions.4.auden.paras.1.
4. A report of what Auden wrote that is not tied to the question or task the student has just finished (5). See week15.questions.1.auden.paras.1 and week15.reading.3.auden.paras.4.
5. A sentence that clashes with something the student was told in another part of the site (4). See week15.intro.1 and week15.making.1.brief.1.
6. One thing printed in two forms (2). See week15.test.items.9.where and week15.questions.1.text.

Not found in this part: em-dashes, exclamation marks, rhetorical questions, or any wording that plays at 1941 or imitates Auden. The test questions of fact were checked against the text in the reader and can all be answered from the chapters named.
