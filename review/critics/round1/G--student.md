## Patterns

Counts are of faults. One finding can show more than one, so the counts add up to more than the number of findings.

1. A word that can point two ways, or a second name for one thing (16). Pronouns with two owners, "the book," "these," "the visitor," "a bad line," "when you do," a bare surname. See week14.questions.2.auden.paras.4 and week13.making.1.table.key.intro.1.

2. Something I need before I can begin is held back or left out (13). What the record is and where to get it, page counts, which reading is optional, what happens when the chart is full, what the reply is for when Auden gave no answer, how the word-list is laid out. See week13.intro.2 and week13.making.1.rules.3.

3. Auden's argument packed so tight that a step or a term is missing (11). The paragraphs shown after a paper give his conclusion and his private words (saint, masses and faces, false copy, daemon) with no plain sentence to carry me across. These are the paragraphs I am asked to reply to. See week13.questions.1.auden.paras.2 and week13.questions.4.auden.paras.1.

4. Questions and hints that lead, read two ways, or cannot be answered as put (10). Three hints point at an answer without saying so; two questions do not say what would count as an answer. See week13.questions.1.text and week14.questions.1.gloss.1.

5. A work or a person brought in with no name, no date or no description (9). "An essay," "a later essay," "the article of 1945," the poem of 1941, the pianist, the translator. Several lean on paragraphs under another question, which I see only if I wrote that paper; the site lets me answer one question a week. See week13.questions.1.auden.paras.3 and week14.questions.2.auden.paras.5.

6. Put-on turns of phrase (8). "Hear" for "listen to," twice; the object set first in the sentence, twice; "comes of"; "there to be read"; a weekday in quotation marks; "on a September 11." They are the only places where the handbook voice slips into an essayist's. See week13.questions.3.auden.paras.4 and week13.questions.2.text.

7. The word-list's own conventions (5). The closing item of an entry is never explained, one plural is missing, and the note for an order collides with a verb that means to order. See week14.making.1.translate.wordsIntro.1 and week14.making.1.translate.glossary.4.1.2.

8. A turn of thought joined with "and" where "but" or "yet" is meant (4: two listed, and two more that I let pass, in the paragraph on Stubb and in the note under Lemont's version). It looks like a side effect of the rule against "not X but Y": the contrast is still there and the reader has to supply it. See week13.questions.1.auden.paras.1 and week13.questions.3.auden.paras.2.

9. Small breaches of form (4). Straight quotation marks once, capitals that vary for the whale and for K.'s title, one British spelling, one shortened page range. See week13.words.cite and week14.making.1.source.1.

10. Auden's view given as flat fact, with no "he said" (3). See week14.questions.1.auden.paras.2 and week13.questions.2.auden.paras.1.

Not found in this part: em-dashes, exclamation marks, rhetorical questions, hype, hedging, dates in any form but the American. Both tests are clear apart from the items listed, and the instructions for the translation can be followed as written.
