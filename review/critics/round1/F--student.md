# F, weeks 10 to 12: the student's reading

Read as a student who has enrolled, has not read the books and knows little about Auden. To see how each entry reaches that student I also read, without changing them, the site's own display code and the entries of earlier weeks that this part points back to. Where a finding rests on that, it says so.

### week12.questions.2.auden.paras.4
- where: Most American novels, the note goes on, are parables
- problem: The first paragraph has just told me that the whale is a symbol and that a symbol is unlike a parable. Here Auden calls novels such as this one parables, and nothing says that he uses the word more loosely in 1941 than in 1949. I could not square the two. The poem is also named as if I knew what it was.
- fix: First sentence: "That spring he published *New Year Letter*, his long poem with notes." Add at the end: "He uses the word parable more loosely here than in 1949, when he keeps it for the lessons drawn in the whaling chapters and calls the whale a symbol." The editor should confirm that this is a fair account of the two uses.
- weight: should

## Patterns

Most frequent first. The count is the number of findings above, unless it says otherwise.

1. Rules, hints and after-notes that mislead, or that cannot be carried out as written (14). A hint that points to the wrong end of a chapter, a passage "in the version you learned" that a prose learner never learned, a count that may mean right answers or attempts, an order to sort what cannot be sorted, an errand set after the paper is closed. See week11.questions.2.gloss.1 and week11.making.1.brief.2.

2. A name, a book or a term used without saying who or what it is (12). The ship and her captain before the novel is opened, Ishmael on a page to be done before reading, Heine, Mephisto, Auden's first name, his long poem, and the book of his lectures that carries most of these weeks. See week11.intro.1 and week11.questions.1.auden.paras.1.

3. Pronouns and pointing words with two owners or none (9): "his", "it", "this book", "the same point", "something like this", "the following May". See week10.intro.1 and week12.questions.2.auden.paras.3.

4. Auden is reported, and the step that ties the report to the question I had just answered is left out (8). The paragraphs after the question on the devil are the plainest case: three reports and no word on sadness or honesty. See week10.questions.1.auden.paras.2 and week11.questions.2.auden.paras.4.

5. British or dated usage in a text meant to be American (7 places, four of them inside findings above): "round" for "around" in week11.questions.1.auden.paras.1, week11.questions.3.auden.paras.4 and week12.questions.2.auden.paras.2; "sat the examination" in week10.questions.2.auden.paras.4; "get by heart" in week10.questions.2.text and week10.questions.2.auden.paras.3; "so that we should have" in week11.questions.2.auden.paras.1.

6. Slips against the house rules on type and form (7). Titles in roman in the three sub-headings and three week titles, and in one overture name (week10.sub, week10.reading.3.paras.3). Straight quotation marks round two chapter titles (week11.words.cite, week12.words.cite). Straight apostrophes in 33 entries of the site's own prose, beside curly ones in the titles taken from the books (week10.test.items.5.speakerShown). One hedge (week10.questions.1.auden.paras.1). One page range shortened where the rest are written out (week10.making.1.source.1).

7. Pointers to paragraphs I may never have been shown (4). Each week I answer one question of two or three, and what Auden wrote is shown only after that one paper. A paragraph that says "you met" or "the other question" cannot count on the others. See week11.questions.3.auden.paras.1 and week12.questions.2.auden.paras.1.

8. The same thing told two or three times, on one page, along one path, or again in a later week (4). See week11.intro.2 and week12.making.1.brief.1.

9. What is the site's own is owned late, or only in part (3). See week12.reading.3.auden.paras.2 and week12.making.1.source.1.
