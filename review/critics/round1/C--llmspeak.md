# Part C, weeks 1 to 3. Lens: writing that reads as machine-made

Read in full: every entry of weeks 1, 2 and 3, including labels, test items, the chart key and the word-list. Findings are in the order of the text. Where a fix would repeat a long stretch of the original, only the changed words are written out.

## Week 1

### week1.sub
- where: Dante: Inferno, Purgatorio
- problem: House rule: titles of books in italics. The sub line of each of the three weeks (also week2.sub, week3.sub) and every .where label print titles in roman type.
- fix: flag only. If these fields can carry italics, italicize the titles. If they cannot, leave them and record the exception.
- weight: could

### week1.intro.1
- where: in the prose Auden set or in verse
- problem: Clipped past clarity. This is the first paragraph of the course and "the prose" is the site's shorthand; a new student has to guess that it means a translation.
- fix: Write "in the prose translation Auden set or in Longfellow's verse."
- weight: could

### week1.intro.2
- where: Begin Inferno III by heart on the first day
- problem: Brevity has cost the verb. A canto is learned by heart; it is not begun by heart. The title is also in roman type here and in italics in the paragraph above.
- fix: First sentence: "Start learning *Inferno* III by heart on the first day." Leave the second sentence as it is.
- weight: should

### week1.intro.3
- where: goes with it. It is short.
- problem: Two pronouns in six words with different referents: the first "it" is the week's reading, the second is the opera. The opera is not named. The closing three-word sentence is a beat added for effect, and "short" is vaguer than the figures given at week1.reading.2.paras.1.
- fix: "The first of the nine operas, Gluck's *Orpheus*, is also set this week. Its libretto is thirteen pages."
- weight: should

### week1.reading.2.paras.1
- where: This is the first. Read the libretto, which is
- problem: Five sentences that average seven words, the second of them four words long. The staccato calls attention to itself, and the first two sentences repeat week1.intro.3. Farther on, "hear the opera through with the words open" is elliptical and old-fashioned: "the words" has to be decoded as the libretto named one sentence earlier.
- fix: "This is the first of the nine operas that the list sets as reading. Read the libretto, which is thirteen pages, then listen to the whole opera with the libretto open. It lasts about an hour and a half." (If week1.intro.3 takes the page count, drop it here.)
- weight: should

### week1.reading.2.auden.paras.1
- where: He names it once, as a date.
- problem: Gnomic. "As a date" does not tell the reader what Auden did with the opera until the two sentences after it have been read. Plainness has become compression.
- fix: Replace the sentence with: "He mentions it once, to mark the start of a period."
- weight: should

### week1.reading.2.auden.paras.2
- where: his reason bears on the title of this course
- problem: Signpost. The clause tells the student that what follows is relevant and says nothing itself. The quotation that ends the paragraph makes the link to the course title without help. The same move, with the same verb, opens week3.questions.2.auden.paras.3.
- fix: Cut the clause after the comma, so that the sentence stops at "ended."
- weight: should

### week1.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: He meets it turned around, as guilt and despair.
- problem: A run of five sentences of nine to fourteen words, three of them one thought chopped up. "Turned around" is vague: the reader cannot tell what has been turned. Farther on, "on this account" can be read as "for this reason."
- fix: Join the second, third and fourth sentences: "A man may try to forget it, but that does not get rid of it; it comes back to him as guilt and despair." In the next sentence write "on this view."
- weight: should

### week1.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: over Dante's gate in this light
- problem: Stock phrase. "In this light" asserts a connection without saying what it is, and the colon then delivers the wording of the inscription where the reader expects Auden's reading of it.
- fix: "Auden applies this to the inscription over Dante's gate, which says that Hell was made by Power, Wisdom and Love."
- weight: should

### week1.questions.2.auden.paras.2
- where: Animals cannot fall. The tempter's promise,
- problem: A three-word sentence dropped in for the beat, and the chopping has removed the "because" that holds the argument together. As it stands the student gets three assertions in a row and must supply the logic. The last clause also lands as an aphorism.
- fix: Join the last two sentences: "Animals cannot fall, because the tempter's promise, [quotation kept], is in the future tense, which animals do not have."
- weight: should

### week1.questions.2.auden.paras.3
- where: and lets spirit go innocent
- problem: "Go innocent" is not a current idiom. It is made on the pattern of "go free" and reads as a put-on antique turn.
- fix: "and holds spirit innocent"
- weight: could

### week1.making.1.table.key.rows.9.who
- where: false counsellors
- problem: British spelling in a text that is otherwise American ("neighbors" in row 8, "center" in row 10).
- fix: "false counselors"
- weight: should

### week1.making.1.source.1
- where: Charles Miller remembers Auden drawing diagrams on the blackboard
- problem: The first sentence gives its source and page. This one names a witness and no book or page, so the claim rests on nothing the student can check. The about page cites Miller by page.
- fix: flag only. Add the title and page of Miller's memoir in the form used for Pearce, or cut the sentence.
- weight: could

## Week 2

### week2.intro.2
- where: The translation is one that Auden is known to
- problem: "The translation" assumes the student already knows what the two exercises are. "Is known to have set" is a stiff passive doing the work of a source, and the status of the second exercise is left unsaid.
- fix: "There are two exercises this week: a translation of a kind Auden set at Michigan, and one of the site's own."
- weight: should

### week2.reading.3.items.2.label
- where: Creon’s edict; the Guard
- problem: This label and items.5.label use a curly apostrophe. Every other apostrophe in the part is straight, including the same section title where it is repeated at week2.test.items.14.where. Mixed forms are a mark of text assembled from more than one pass.
- fix: flag only. Use one form throughout. With curly double quotes in use, the curly apostrophe is the consistent choice.
- weight: could

### week2.words.where
- where: Book II, II.3 Aequam, memento
- problem: Reads as fields joined by a program: the book number is given twice. The same form is at week2.test.items.15.where and week2.test.items.17.where, while week2.test.items.16.where uses a different one. The comma inside the Latin also needs checking against the edition.
- fix: "Odes II.3 (Aequam memento)," and the same form for the three test labels: "Odes I.24 (Quis desiderio)," "Odes II.13," "Odes III.5 (Caelo tonantem)."
- weight: should

### week2.test.items.7.where
- where: 4. The return of Agamemnon
- problem: After a passage question the label gives the section alone (also items 9, 11 and 13). After a plain question it gives the play and then the section (items 8, 10, 12 and 14). The student sees both forms in one test.
- fix: Give the play first every time, as in items 8 and 12: play, then section number and title.
- weight: could

### week2.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: is as a rule one he could not have
- problem: Without commas the words run together and have to be read twice. Two sentences earlier, "and it is addressed to" spends five words where one will do.
- fix: "The wrong he does is, as a rule, one that he could not have avoided." And earlier: "The tragic hero is a warning to the ordinary citizens of the chorus."
- weight: could

### week2.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: drew a conclusion that we would not draw
- problem: Signpost. The sentence announces a conclusion in place of stating it, and its other half (that we would not agree) is said again by the first sentence of the next paragraph. "On his account" can be read as "for his sake." The Greek word is spelled with a y here, while the quotation in the reveal for the second question spells it the usual way.
- fix: Merge the first two sentences: "The Greeks, Auden says, concluded that anyone caught in such a place was already guilty of something else." Settle on one spelling of the Greek word for the site's own sentences.
- weight: should

### week2.questions.1.auden.paras.4
- where: a situation that makes a man guilty by itself
- problem: Misplaced modifier. "By itself" attaches to "guilty," and the reader has to go back to move it.
- fix: "a situation that by itself makes a man guilty"
- weight: should

### week2.questions.2.text
- where: In Odes I.3 Horace prays for the ship
- problem: House rule: titles of books in italics. The question itself is not at issue. *Odes* is italic at week2.intro.1 and week2.words.cite and roman here, twice in the reveal (week2.questions.2.auden.paras.2 and paras.3) and at week2.making.1.brief.2.
- fix: Italicize the title in all four entries: "*Odes* I.3," "*Odes* I.14," "*Odes* I.11."
- weight: should

### week2.questions.2.auden.paras.3
- where: Odysseus is trying to get home. Jason would be
- problem: Three sentences of seven, six and nine words. The second and third are examples of the first, and nothing joins them to it. This is the clearest case in the part of short sentences that ought to be one.
- fix: "A voyage is then a necessary evil: Odysseus is only trying to get home, and Jason would be glad to find the Fleece nearer."
- weight: should

### week2.questions.2.auden.paras.4
- where: Keep Horace's view by you until
- problem: "By you" is a British turn in a text that is meant to be American and plain. It reads as a put-on voice.
- fix: "Keep Horace's view in mind until *Moby-Dick*."
- weight: could

### week2.making.1.short
- where: with a word-list and nothing else
- problem: Refrain. "And nothing else" ends this line, ends the first paragraph of the brief (brief.1) and ends the first sentence of the second rule (rules.2): three times on one screen. By the third it reads as a tic.
- fix: Here write "Eight lines of Horace and a word-list." Keep the phrase only at rules.2, where it is the instruction.

### week2.making.1.brief.1
- where: from German, French or Latin, from whichever of the
- problem: Two "from" phrases stacked one on the other, and then the refrain again at the end of the sentence.
- fix: "At Michigan Auden asked each student to translate a poem from whichever of German, French and Latin that student had not studied, with no help but a dictionary."
- weight: should

### week2.making.1.brief.2
- where: It gives each word in the order of the
- problem: Padding across entries. This last sentence explains how the word-list is laid out, and week2.making.1.translate.wordsIntro.1 explains it again in other words directly above the list. The opening four-word sentence begins with "This" and has nothing to point to. The title is in roman type.
- fix: "The poem is *Odes* I.11, in which Horace addresses a woman who has been to the astrologers. The word-list below takes the place of a dictionary." Cut the last sentence.

### week2.making.1.rules.3

### week2.making.1.rules.4

### week2.making.1.translate.after.1

### week2.making.2.brief.1

### week3.questions.1.auden.paras.4

### week3.questions.2.auden.paras.2

## Checked and found clean

- No em-dashes, no exclamation marks, no rhetorical questions. En-dashes stand only in ranges of numbers.
- None of the inflated words on the list (explore, capture, rich, deep, powerful, crucial, journey, lens, landscape, "a kind of," "a sense of"). No hedging adverbs. Dates are in American form.
- The fault of this part is the opposite one: plainness pushed until it is a manner. The sentences are short as a rule (171 sentences of running prose, mean 14 words, 37 of eight words or fewer, 13 of twenty-five or more), and the same few moves recur.

## Patterns

1. "Come" and "go" as the all-purpose verb, 13 times in running prose (literal movement in the stories and "Goes with" in the word-list not counted). Singly most are harmless. Together they are the voice's tic, and one causes a misreading. Seen at week3.questions.2.auden.paras.1 and week1.intro.3. Remedy: where the verb is a stand-in, use the exact one (writes about, is set, is placed).

2. Attribution wedged after the opening noun phrase ("[subject], he says, ..."), 12 times. It opens the second paragraph in five of the six question reveals. Seen at week1.questions.1.auden.paras.2 and week3.questions.2.auden.paras.2 (twice in that one paragraph). The attribution is needed; the fixed position is not. Remedy: open some paragraphs with "Auden argues that," or attribute once and let the next sentences stand.

3. Short sentences in a run where one sentence is wanted, or a short sentence set down as a beat, in 9 entries. Seen at week1.reading.2.paras.1 and week2.questions.2.auden.paras.3. The cost is to the argument: the "because" or "and so" between the pieces is dropped, as at week1.questions.2.auden.paras.2.

4. A denial and its correction as two sentences ("X does not A. It does B."), 7 times, five of them in week 3 (one runs the other way, assertion first). Seen at week1.questions.1.auden.paras.2 and week3.questions.1.auden.paras.3. In most cases the contrast is Auden's own and someone does hold X, so the matter may stay. The repeated shape is the house rule's banned figure with a period in the middle. Remedy: keep the real contrasts and vary the form ("instead of," a single sentence, or the positive alone).

5. British or antique turns in a text meant to be American and plain, 6 times. Seen at week1.making.1.table.key.rows.9.who and week2.making.1.rules.4. The others are at week1.reading.2.paras.1, week1.questions.2.auden.paras.3, week2.questions.2.auden.paras.4 and week3.questions.2.auden.paras.2.

6. Sentences or clauses that announce and do not say, 5 times. Seen at week3.questions.2.auden.paras.3 and week1.reading.2.auden.paras.2 (both "bears on ... this course"). The others are at week2.questions.1.auden.paras.3, week3.words.after.1 and week3.questions.1.auden.paras.2.

7. Book titles in roman type in running prose, 5 entries. Seen at week1.intro.2 and week2.questions.2.text. The sub lines and the .where labels do the same and are flagged once, at week1.sub.

8. Labels of one kind in more than one form, 10 labels of three kinds: references to Horace, sections of the plays, apostrophes. Seen at week2.words.where and week2.test.items.7.where.

9. The refrain "and nothing else," 3 times in one exercise. Seen at week2.making.1.short and week2.making.1.brief.1.

10. A promise of what the student will find, 2 times, both with "find out." Seen at week2.making.2.brief.2 and week3.making.1.brief.2.
