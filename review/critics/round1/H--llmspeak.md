# H, week 15: findings of the critic for machine-made writing

House rules were checked first. The week has no em-dashes, no exclamation marks and no rhetorical questions. Its dates are in American form and its double quotation marks are curly. Breaches of the other rules are among the findings below, which follow the order of the text. Where a fix keeps part of a sentence unchanged, only the changed part is written out.

### week15.sub
- where: Kafka: The Castle, Chapters 7 to 18.
- problem: House rule: titles of books and plays go in italics. Neither title is italic here or in week15.title, and in week15.intro.3 the canto is given as "Paradiso XXXIII" in roman. Every week's heading does the same, so this may be a limit of the heading field.
- fix: flag only. The editor must decide whether headings and canto references are an exception to the italics rule. If the rule applies to them, set *The Castle* and *The Family Reunion* in italics in both headings and write *Paradiso* XXXIII in the intro.
- weight: could

### week15.intro.1
- where: The last week of reading.
- problem: The week opens on a verbless fragment and two more short sentences, a staccato that draws attention to itself. That Kafka left the book unfinished and that he died are one fact split in two.
- fix: This is the last week of reading, and what is left of *The Castle* comes to about 180 pages. Kafka died in 1924 without finishing it, and Max Brod had the manuscript printed in 1926.
- weight: should

### week15.intro.1
- where: with a door closing on K.
- problem: The paragraph ends on an image placed there for effect. The student needs to know where the text stops and that it breaks off. The door adds nothing to that and gives away the last page.
- fix: The 1930 translation follows that first edition and breaks off, unfinished, at the end of Chapter 18.
- weight: could

### week15.intro.3
- where: The exercise is a chart that Auden wrote
- problem: Outside this critic's lens, but it will stop a student. The three intro paragraphs name the reading, the exercise, the canto and the examination and leave out the opera, which the reading list then sets at about four and a half hours of listening.
- fix: Add a sentence: "The last of the nine operas is set this week as well, and it takes about four and a half hours." Flag for the editor: the other opera weeks may need the same line.
- weight: should

### week15.reading.3.paras.1
- where: Then hear the opera through with the words open.
- problem: Mock-antique plainness. "Hear through" stands in for "listen to all of," and words cannot be open; a libretto can. The same sentence heads all nine opera notes, so the student meets the mannerism nine times.
- fix: Read the libretto first, then listen to the whole opera with the libretto open in front of you. (Change it in all nine notes.)
- weight: should

### week15.reading.3.paras.2
- where: Wagner wrote the words, and he wrote these first.
- problem: Gnomic. The reader has to work out that "these" means the words of this one opera, and the sentence is balanced for its sound. The paragraph then closes on three flat sentences in a row, one of them five words long.
- fix: Wagner wrote his own librettos, and of the four in the *Ring* he wrote this one first. For the close, join the last two sentences: They are sung in German, and the English translation printed here gives this one the title *The Twilight of the Gods*.
- weight: should

### week15.reading.3.paras.3
- where: and the opera allows for that
- problem: A clause that reassures and announces. The next sentence says the same thing with the facts in it.
- fix: You are reading only the end of the story, but three passages tell part of what has gone before: the Norns in the Prelude, then Waltraute in Act 1 and Siegfried in Act 3.
- weight: should

### week15.reading.3.auden.paras.2
- where: We are given to understand
- problem: The site says "you" to the student everywhere else. This "we" is Auden's pronoun carried over from his sentence, with a stock phrase attached. The same leak is in week15.questions.3.auden.paras.3 ("mean us to think") and week15.questions.3.auden.paras.4 ("We are not told").
- fix: The audience, he says, is led to expect that something new will be built, and is not shown how, or by whom. In the two later places write "the audience" for "us" and "we."
- weight: could

### week15.reading.3.auden.paras.3
- where: What is odd about these people is that
- problem: A wind-up before the point, and an unclear pointer. The sentence before is about people in any tragedy, so "these people" reads as them. Only two sentences later does it turn out that Wagner's characters are meant.
- fix: Wagner's characters know beforehand that their mistakes are mistakes.
- weight: should

### week15.reading.3.auden.paras.4
- where: say the same and give it a name
- problem: A signpost with two loose ends: the same as what, and a name for what. It also leaves the praise of Wagner in the next sentence standing as the site's own judgment, because the attribution there covers only the name of the heresy.
- fix: Fold the first two sentences into one: In the notes to *New Year Letter*, which came out that spring, Auden ranks Wagner first among the Romantic artists, because he takes furthest the Romantic heresy, [quotation kept]
- weight: should

### week15.reading.3.auden.paras.4
- where: Another note explains the word.
- problem: Five short sentences in a row, two of them five words long, with a mirrored pair split in two for the punch. "The word" is singular, yet two words are then defined, and the second, temptation, has not been used, so the student cannot tell why it is there.
- fix: The operas, the note says, are masterpieces as studies in tribulation and absurd as plots. Another note gives the distinction: a tribulation can only be endured, while a temptation is a conflict that has to be settled by an act of will. Flag for the editor: say in a clause how temptation bears on plot, as Auden's note presumably does, or drop the second definition.
- weight: should

### week15.test.items.9.where
- where: Chapter 15: Amalia’s Secret
- problem: The apostrophe here is curly. The same heading in week15.test.items.8.where has a straight one, and so has every other apostrophe in the week.
- fix: Make the two headings match. Flag for the editor: the week has curly double quotation marks and straight apostrophes throughout. If that is not on purpose, make the apostrophes curly everywhere.
- weight: should

### week15.questions.1.auden.paras.1
- where: the way to read them is beside
- problem: Compressed until the grammar creaks. "Is beside" reads as a slip.
- fix: End the sentence: ...the quest, and should be read beside the older quests.
- weight: should

### week15.questions.1.auden.paras.1
- where: To be exceptional now is to be reflective
- problem: The paragraph closes on a mirrored aphorism in the site's own voice. The claim is Auden's and carries no attribution, and the student has to unpick a cleft that reverses a clause four sentences back.
- fix: Auden's explanation is that the exceptional man is now the reflective one, and to him even what is necessary looks arbitrary.
- weight: should

### week15.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: The review ends on the reader.
- problem: A six-word signpost, and a coy one. It announces the next sentence instead of saying it.
- fix: At the end of the review Auden turns to the reader: an industrial civilization, he says, makes everyone a K. [quotation kept]
- weight: should

### week15.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: Its account of the failure is one sentence
- problem: A stock frame for setting up a quotation. "The failure" has nothing to point back to: no sentence so far has said that K. fails.
- fix: Keep the first sentence. Then: There he says why K. fails: [quotation kept]
- weight: should

### week15.questions.1.auden.paras.6
- where: The same essay warns against all of this.
- problem: A dramatic opener with a vague object. "All of this" could be the review, the two essays, or the reading of Kafka in general.
- fix: The same essay warns against interpreting Kafka at all.
- weight: should

### week15.questions.2.gloss.1
- where: Brod's note stands at the end of the 1930
- problem: Outside this critic's lens, but it will stop a student. The hint sends him to a note that the site does not print, which week15.questions.2.auden.paras.1 says only after the paper is in. The tag "he says there" is one of four this week, and here it is used of Brod.
- fix: Brod's note, at the end of the 1930 volume, is not printed here. In it he says that God asked of Abraham what was a crime, and that the official asks of the girl something plainly immoral.
- weight: should

### week15.questions.2.auden.paras.2
- where: it stood to this century as Dante's poem stood
- problem: "This century" was the twentieth when Auden spoke. Carried into the site's own sentence, it now points at the present one.
- fix: Change two words: ...that it stood to the twentieth century as Dante's poem stood to the fourteenth.
- weight: should

### week15.questions.2.auden.paras.3
- where: He took a side about twenty years later
- problem: An approximation where the handbook's voice asks for a number. Every other source this week is dated in the prose. The essay the week leans on most is "a later essay" under the first question and "about twenty years later" here, and the source lines give no year for the book it is in.
- fix: flag only. Supply the year: "He took a side in [year], in the essay on Kafka in *The Dyer's Hand*." Give the same year in week15.questions.1.auden.paras.4.
- weight: should

### week15.questions.2.auden.paras.3
- where: took the people of Kafka's castle for agents
- problem: The place is "the Castle" in the set question, in the heading of this entry and in the two paragraphs before, and "the castle" twice in this paragraph.
- fix: flag only. Choose one form for the place in Kafka's story and keep to it. Lowercase is right for the castle in Němcová's novel.
- weight: could

### week15.questions.2.auden.paras.4
- where: the letter was no command from heaven
- problem: The entry closes on a turned phrase, and the phrase is the site's own verdict on the set question, with nothing from Auden behind it.
- fix: Keep the first sentence. For the second: If Auden is right about the officials, Brod's comparison does not hold. Flag for the editor: the inference is the site's, and the house voice asks that the site's own steps be marked as such.
- weight: should

### week15.questions.3.gloss.1
- where: Decide whether these change, or Harry does.
- problem: Terse to the point of a riddle, in a hint the student reads before he writes. "These" could be the Furies of Aeschylus, named in the sentence before, or the Eumenides of Eliot's play, which the hint never names.
- fix: Decide whether Eliot's Eumenides change in the course of the play, or whether it is Harry who changes.
- weight: must

### week15.questions.3.auden.paras.1
- where: He put it last on the list.
- problem: A seven-word sentence set after an admission that nothing has been found, to hint at a meaning the site does not state. The handbook's voice reports or stays silent; it does not nudge.
- fix: cut. If the place on the list is offered as evidence, say in plain words what it is evidence of.
- weight: should

### week15.questions.4.auden.paras.1
- where: What follows comes from things he published in 1941.
- problem: The signpost is wrong twice. "He" reads as Charles Miller, the subject of the sentence before. And the last paragraph of the entry rests on a class meeting and on something from 1945, so the entry is not all from 1941 and not all from print.
- fix: The next two paragraphs come from things Auden published in 1941.
- weight: should

### week15.questions.4.auden.paras.2
- where: The two words stand side by side
- problem: The two words are not named. The reader must look back to the heading and guess, and the pair that the next sentences give, fate and free will, is not the pair in the course title.
- fix: flag only for the facts, since the wording depends on what the article has. If the pair meant is fate and free will, cut the sentence and begin: In the article of March 1941 he says that until the middle of the nineteenth century all art rested on two contrasts.
- weight: should

### week15.questions.4.auden.paras.2
- where: His example is Antigone.
- problem: Nine sentences in a row of one shape, most of them eight to fifteen words, with a four-word sentence among them and then "She," "She," "The." The plainness has turned into a drumbeat.
- fix: The first sets chaos against order and fate against free will; the second sets the churl against the hero. The churl is [quotation kept], and the hero pits his will against accident and so makes order for himself and for other people. Heroism has a price: [quotation kept] His example is Antigone, who must choose between the law and what she owes her brother, knows that she cannot do both, makes her choice, and wills her own death. The disorder, he says, lies in her situation and not in her.
- weight: should

### week15.questions.4.auden.paras.3
- where: he is the third son, and his success
- problem: The sentence on the fairy tale is in week15.questions.1.auden.paras.1 in almost the same words, with Faust and Peer Gynt after it in both places. A student who writes on both questions reads one summary twice.
- fix: flag only. Each panel has to stand alone, so the editor may let it be. If not, cut the summary here to one sentence and point back to the first question.
- weight: could

### week15.questions.4.auden.paras.3
- where: is marked out and cannot find out for what
- problem: Terseness for show: the inverted "Last comes," two "out"s in six words, and a bare "for what." Its twin is "cannot tell in what" in week15.questions.1.auden.paras.1.
- fix: K. comes last. He is marked out too, and cannot discover for what purpose. In the earlier entry, add "way" after "in what."
- weight: should

### week15.questions.4.auden.paras.3
- where: Several of these stages are books on the list
- problem: Two approximations, "several" and "nearly," where the site can count, and a slip of category: a stage is not a book.
- fix: Faust, Peer Gynt and K. all come from books on the list, and the list has them in that order. Flag for the editor: confirm the three names and the order against the list.
- weight: should

### week15.questions.4.auden.paras.4
- where: gave the theme to a piano and an orchestra
- problem: Coy. The phrase plays on the musical sense of "theme" and can be read only by a student who remembers week 13. It does not say what Auden did. The next sentence has a loose pointer of its own: "the two sides" of what is not said, and the names that follow are two kinds of tragedy.
- fix: In class he played the slow movement of Beethoven's Fourth Piano Concerto, with the piano as the man and the orchestra as his fate, and asked whether the piano came back too late (week 13). In the second sentence, for "found names for the two sides" write "named two kinds of tragedy."
- weight: must

### week15.making.1.brief.2
- where: It has nine pairs of terms.
- problem: Four short sentences in a row, with the tense going from past to present to past to present. The closing "and nothing more" is there for emphasis, and the note under the chart says the same thing in full.
- fix: Donald Pearce copied the chart as Auden wrote it: nine pairs of terms, one of each pair in the upper line and its partner below. He gives the terms and no explanation.
- weight: should

## Patterns

Counts are for week 15 only, over 38 entries of running prose and 159 sentences. Most frequent first.

1. A pointer with nothing clear to point to: a demonstrative, a pronoun or "the X" where the noun is wanted. 11 places. See week15.reading.3.auden.paras.3 ("these people") and week15.questions.3.gloss.1 ("these change"). The others: "wrote these first" (reading.3.paras.2); "give it a name" and "the word" (reading.3.auden.paras.4); "the failure" (questions.1.auden.paras.3); "all of this" (questions.1.auden.paras.6); "this century" (questions.2.auden.paras.2); "things he published" (questions.4.auden.paras.1); "the two words" (questions.4.auden.paras.2); "the two sides" (questions.4.auden.paras.4). This is what the order to be brief has cost. Each one saves three or four words and makes the student stop.

2. A topic sentence that announces a point and does not make it. 11 places. See week15.questions.1.auden.paras.2 and week15.reading.3.auden.paras.4. Eight have findings above. Three are left alone as ordinary transitions: the openers of week15.reading.3.auden.paras.3, week15.reading.3.auden.paras.5 and week15.questions.1.auden.paras.4. Six of the eleven are also counted under pattern 1, for one reason: a sentence that only gestures at what comes next has to use a vague pointer.

3. An entry that closes on a short sentence or a turned phrase. 8 of the 38 entries. See week15.questions.2.auden.paras.4 and week15.questions.3.auden.paras.1. Five have findings above. Three are left alone because each states a fact or gives an instruction: "what the book did to Auden" (questions.1.auden.paras.6), "does not say that he built the course" (questions.4.auden.paras.3), "Then he announced the examination" (making.1.brief.1). They still add to the habit, and the editor may want to recast one of them.

4. Coy or mock-antique wording. 6 phrases in 5 entries. See week15.reading.3.paras.1 and week15.questions.4.auden.paras.4. The others: "is beside" and "in what" (questions.1.auden.paras.1), "ends on the reader" (questions.1.auden.paras.2), "Last comes" with "for what" (questions.4.auden.paras.3).

5. A paragraph about Auden that ends on a quotation. 5 of 23, and a sixth ends two words after one. See week15.questions.1.auden.paras.2 and week15.questions.1.auden.paras.3, which stand next to each other and are each two sentences long. Ending on his words suits a panel that reports him, so no finding is entered for the move itself. Recasting those two, as proposed above, breaks the run.

6. A run of short sentences of one shape. 5 runs. See week15.questions.4.auden.paras.2 (nine sentences) and week15.reading.3.auden.paras.4 (five). The others: week15.intro.1, week15.reading.3.paras.2, week15.making.1.brief.2. Over the whole week 35 of the 159 sentences have eight words or fewer. The long paragraphs elsewhere vary well, so the fault is local.

7. The tag "he says there." 4 times. See week15.reading.3.auden.paras.1 and week15.questions.4.auden.paras.2; also questions.1.auden.paras.1 and questions.2.gloss.1. It occurs thirteen times in the site as a whole. Where the source is named in the sentence before, "he says" is enough.

8. A vague quantity or date where the site knows the figure. 4 places in 3 entries. See week15.questions.2.auden.paras.3 ("about twenty years later") and week15.questions.4.auden.paras.3 ("several," "nearly"); also "a later essay" in week15.questions.1.auden.paras.4.

9. Auden's "we" and "us" in the site's own sentences. 3 places. See week15.reading.3.auden.paras.2 and week15.questions.3.auden.paras.4; also questions.3.auden.paras.3.

Two notes that reach beyond this week. The verbless opener of week15.intro.1 is a standing formula: the first intro sentence of weeks 5, 7, 12 and 14 is a fragment of the same kind. The sentence about hearing the opera through is in all nine opera notes, in seven of them word for word. A fixed instruction may repeat, but its wording has to be sound, which is why it has a finding above.

Looked for and not found in week 15: em-dashes, exclamation marks, rhetorical questions, hype, inflated diction, lists of three made by reflex (each list of three counts real things), and "not X but Y" turned for effect (the three turns on "and not" are Auden's own or belong to the set question).
