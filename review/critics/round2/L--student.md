# Part L3 (week 15), read as the student

### week15.intro.2
- where: using the link for borrowing one
- problem: The clause can be read as saying that a copy is given with the reading, which the sentence before has just denied. The date of the play is left for the student to work out by subtraction.
- fix: "If you did not get a copy last week, get one now. The note printed with the reading gives a link for borrowing one online." (week14.intro.5 has already told the student to borrow or buy it.) Then: "The play was first staged and printed in 1939, two years before the course, and it is the newest of the required books." (1939 is what the entry's own arithmetic gives; confirm it.)
- weight: should

### week15.reading.3.paras.2
- where: Bayreuth staged all four together
- problem: Bayreuth and Siegfried are named with no word on what or who they are. The paragraph also never says that the "single opera" planned in 1848 is the one the student is about to read, so one work looks like two.
- fix: Replace everything after the first sentence with: "Wagner wrote the words himself, in German. He began in 1848 with a plan for one opera about the death of the hero Siegfried, which became this one, and then wrote the words of the other three, working backward through the story. The four were first staged together in August 1876 at Bayreuth, the town in Bavaria where a theater had been built for them."
- weight: should

### week15.reading.3.paras.3
- where: Waltraute, who is a Valkyrie
- problem: Waltraute is explained by two names the student does not have yet, a Valkyrie and Brünnhilde, and Siegfried is still not introduced. The one thing to do while listening is the tail of a paragraph about something else, and its first words can be misread as the idiom "who knows what."
- fix: "...Waltraute, one of the Valkyries (the warrior daughters of the god Wotan), who comes to her sister Brünnhilde, the heroine, in Act 1; and the hero, Siegfried, in Act 3." Then a paragraph to itself: "While you listen, notice which characters know what is going to happen, and what each of them does about it." Use the same wording in week15.reading.3.auden.paras.7.
- weight: should

### week15.reading.3.auden.paras.2
- where: will be built again
- problem: A world that is new cannot be built again. The student cannot tell whether the old world is to be restored or a different one made.
- fix: Cut the last word of the clause, so that it ends "a new world will be built". If the lecture means the old world restored, write "that the world will be rebuilt".
- weight: could

### week15.reading.3.auden.paras.3
- where: and suffer for them later
- problem: Two "them"s six words apart have different owners, the characters and the mistakes. The paragraph announces Auden's second point, and paras.7 sends the student back to it by that name, but nothing above is numbered as the first.
- fix: "It would not be odd, he says, if the characters made mistakes and paid for those mistakes afterward." Number the first point where it is made, by opening week15.reading.3.auden.paras.2 with "Auden's first point is that this reading breaks down in the last opera."
- weight: should

### week15.reading.3.auden.paras.4
- where: The notes to Auden’s long poem
- problem: The student is not told that the notes are Auden's own, and "the note" then does the saying three times, so it is not clear who holds the opinion. The site's explanation of the two kinds of necessity comes last, after the sentence about Fricka that depends on it, and nothing shows why this is "the same point" as the one about what the characters know.
- fix: Open with "Auden wrote notes of his own to *New Year Letter*, the long poem he published in the spring of 1941, and two of them bear on Wagner." After that make Auden the subject: "In one he puts Wagner ... because Wagner carries furthest what he calls the Romantic heresy, [quotation kept]"; "he finds"; "he adds". Move the site's sentence on the two terms up so that it follows the quotation, and end it with the link to the paragraph before, still marked as the site's: "On that reading, characters who know better and go on all the same put the first above the second." Flag: "first among" can mean earliest or greatest. Say which the note means.
- weight: should

### week15.reading.3.auden.paras.5
- where: when they are taken as plots
- problem: The first sentence defines two terms, tribulation and temptation, and the second uses only one of them. "Plots" stands where the student expects the other term, so the contrast the paragraph set up is never closed, and paras.7 then asks the student to use it.
- fix: Flag only. If the note sets the operas as studies in tribulation against the operas as dramas of temptation, say so: "and absurd when they are taken as dramas of temptation, in which someone has a choice to make." If the note's own word is "plots", add the link as the site's: "As this site reads the note, a plot needs a temptation, a conflict the character could settle either way."
- weight: should

### week15.reading.3.auden.paras.7
- where: Compare what you noticed with
- problem: The two things to compare with are four and two paragraphs back, with the paragraph on Wagner's theory of opera standing between them and this sentence. Nothing says whether the comparison is to be written down.
- fix: "Compare what you noticed with what Auden says the characters know (the third paragraph of this block) and with what he says about tribulation (the fifth)." Then add whichever is true: "You do not need to write anything," or the place to write it.
- weight: could

### week15.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: were never exceptions
- problem: Earlier in the paragraph the two are the examples of heroes who had to become exceptional, and here they were never exceptions, with only "By that measure" to carry the reversal. The clause about taking the arbitrary for the necessary gets no plain-word gloss, so the student cannot see in what way K. is their opposite.
- fix: Last sentence: "By that test, Auden concludes, Faust and Peer Gynt only looked exceptional, since neither of them reflects, and they stand for the ordinary person." After the sentence on their easy time, add as the site's: "As this site reads the phrase, they treat their own whims as if those whims were fate."
- weight: should

### week15.questions.1.auden.paras.5
- where: everything a wrong choice would
- problem: The difference between a wrong guess and a wrong choice is the point of the sentence and the student is left to work it out, with a "him" that could be K. or the earlier hero. Odysseus comes with no word on who he is, and a student who knows that he did listen to the Sirens, tied to the mast, will stop at that sentence.
- fix: "K. can only guess what he ought to do, Auden says, and when he guesses wrong he pays as much, and has to feel as responsible, as if he had known what was right and chosen against it." For the other sentence, check the essay and consider "Odysseus, the hero of Homer's *Odyssey*, knows that the Sirens are to be resisted."
- weight: should

### week15.questions.1.auden.paras.6
- where: and that alone is his guilt
- problem: The quotation that follows stands as a sentence with no speaker, and the last sentence has no verb of saying in it or in the one before, so the student cannot tell whether the closing gloss is Auden's or the site's. "The old hero" can also be read as an aged one.
- fix: "The earlier hero, Auden goes on, ..." and "In Auden's words, [quotation kept]." Then, if the gloss is his: "K. wants, Auden says, to be somebody as other people are, and that is refused him." If it is the site's, begin it "As this site reads the sentence,". Flag: the editor must say which.
- weight: should

### week15.questions.1.auden.paras.7
- where: They say nothing about Ahab
- problem: The first paragraph of this block reports Auden setting K. and the Castle beside Ahab and the Whale, so "nothing about Ahab" is not so, and his remark to the class that ties the book to Dante's poem is printed only under question 2, though it is this question that asks about Dante. The instruction for the reply is the last sentence of a paragraph that starts as a report, and "what the book did to Auden" is coy.
- fix: End this paragraph after the quotation. New paragraph: "Take the paragraphs above as Auden's own response to the book. This site chose them for what they say about K. On Ahab they give only the comparison that Pearce recorded, and on Dante's quest they give nothing." Keep the last sentence as it is. Consider adding to the first paragraph of the block the sentence from week15.questions.2.auden.paras.2 that reports what Auden told the class about the book and Dante's poem.
- weight: should

### week15.questions.2.gloss.1
- where: unearned and out of reach
- problem: The sentence that defines grace, the one term the student needs in order to judge Brod's comparison, ends in three phrases stacked after "which", and the last of them has no clear owner. It has to be read twice.
- fix: Split it after "grace" and give the definition a sentence to itself: "He explains grace as God's guidance of human destiny, which sends favors and punishments alike and can be neither earned nor reached."

### week15.questions.2.auden.paras.2

### week15.questions.2.auden.paras.3
- where: a piece of Kafka’s background
- problem: The student is not told why a Czech novel bears on Kafka at all, so the step from Němcová's castle to Kafka's has nothing under it. "Auden took a side" also comes before the student has been shown a second side.
- fix: Opening: "Auden came down against the reading of the Castle as grace in an essay of 1962, [title kept], in his book *The Dyer's Hand*." Second sentence: "Brod's life of Kafka, in a later edition, had by then pointed to a Czech novel that Kafka knew, Božena Němcová's *The Grandmother*, as a source of *The Castle*, and Auden uses the novel to rule out some readings of Kafka's book." Missing fact to confirm: that Brod says Kafka knew the novel and calls it a source. If Brod says something else, put that link in its place. In the fourth sentence write "had read the book wrongly".
- weight: should

### week15.questions.3.auden.paras.3

### week15.making.1.rules.3
- where: an author counts as one book
- problem: The rule does not say what happens when a student names an author in one box and one of that author's books or characters in another. Shakespeare has four plays on the list, so "Shakespeare" and "Hamlet" could be one book or two, and the student would have to ask.
- fix: Flag only: the editor must decide the rule. If the stricter one: "An author counts as one book, and that author's books and characters do not count again if you use them as well." If the looser one: "An author counts as one book, apart from any single book of that author's that you also use."
- weight: should

## Patterns

1. A step is left out of the report of Auden, so the student cannot follow it or tie it to the question asked (7). See week15.reading.3.auden.paras.5 and week15.questions.3.auden.paras.3.
2. A name or term is used before the student is told who or what it is (6). See week15.reading.3.paras.2 and week15.questions.4.auden.paras.3.
3. A pointer misleads or dangles: a bare week number in parentheses that reads as a date, a page or a noun with nothing to point to, a "second point" with no first (6). See week15.questions.2.auden.paras.2 and week15.questions.2.auden.paras.4.
4. An instruction comes at the tail of a paragraph about something else, or is not said to be the work of the reply (6). See week15.questions.3.auden.paras.5 and week15.questions.1.auden.paras.7.
5. One entry is at odds with another, or with its own count (4). See week15.questions.1.auden.paras.7 and week15.questions.4.auden.paras.2.
6. It is not clear whose opinion a sentence gives (2). See week15.reading.3.auden.paras.4 and week15.questions.1.auden.paras.6.
7. Week 2 let the student read one of two Greek tragedies, and week 15 twice refers to one of them as if every student had read it (2). See week15.questions.3.gloss.1 and week15.questions.4.auden.paras.2.
