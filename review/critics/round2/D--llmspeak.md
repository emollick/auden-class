# Part D (weeks 1 and 2): writing that reads as machine-made

### week1.intro.1
- where: and the final examination is on it
- problem: "on" comes twice, and "it" can be taken for the list, which would mean the examination covers every book. This is the sentence that opens the term.
- fix: "The list begins with the *Divine Comedy*, and the final examination is on that poem."
- weight: should

### week1.reading.2.paras.1
- where: This is the first of the nine operas
- problem: The words repeat week1.intro.3, which stands a few lines above on the same page. In this paragraph three of the five sentences are also two statements joined by ", and".
- fix: Keep the count here and trim the intro to "Gluck's opera *Orpheus* is also assigned this week." For the joins, write "You may listen to either of the two versions Gluck made."
- weight: could

### week1.reading.2.paras.2
- where: notice the condition he is given
- problem: The last person named is Dante, so "he" points at the wrong owner (house rule 10). The comma before "and what" splits one instruction in two.
- fix: "While you listen, notice the condition Orpheus is given and what makes him break it."
- weight: should

### week1.reading.2.auden.paras.1
- where: Nothing by Auden about this opera
- problem: The first sentence says nothing has been found and the second says Auden names the opera twice, so the reader stops at a contradiction. The clause about what follows repeats the heading, and "One of them" promises a second piece that the paragraph never names.
- fix: Replace the first two sentences with: "Auden names *Orpheus* in two pieces of 1948, an article for *Vogue* and the introduction to a book of Greek writers, and both times only to mark where a period starts. Nothing more by him about this opera has been found." Then open the third sentence "The introduction, [essay title kept], says that".
- weight: must

### week1.reading.2.auden.paras.1
- where: The same essay says that
- problem: Two sentences running hang a claim on an essay that "says that", with the subject restated in full. The dates then trail in a loose "which is", where "which" has a plural owner and a singular verb.
- fix: Open the fourth sentence "It also says that". End the fifth "from Gluck's *Orpheus* in 1762 to Verdi's *Otello* in 1887."
- weight: could

### week1.reading.2.auden.paras.2
- where: Auden suggests why good operas have become rarer
- problem: The first sentence announces the third, which then repeats its words; apart from the name of the book it says nothing (house rule 8). Four sentences carry five attributions, and the second packs three insertions into 42 words. The name of the age stands inside quotation marks in the first paragraph and is used twice here without them. "free people are always good ones" is a pronoun swap that left a lame "ones".
- fix: Replace the first three sentences with: "[Essay title kept] is an essay in Auden's book *The Dyer's Hand* (1962). It dates opera's great years from Mozart to Verdi and says that liberal humanism was at its height in the same years, when people believed without question in freedom and progress. If good operas are rarer in his own time, the cause may be that people have learned they are not as free as the humanists supposed and have come to doubt whether being free makes people good." Leave the sentence on free will and the quotation as they are.
- weight: must

### week1.words.after.1
- where: the hero Tennyson made of him
- problem: Tennyson comes in with no introduction (house rule 11), and "the hero ..., the hero ..." repeats the noun for the cadence. A student who does not know the poem cannot tell what Tennyson did.
- fix: Replace the words after the comma with: "and that the Victorian poet Tennyson made him one in a poem of his own."
- weight: should

### week1.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: which is this site’s and not Auden’s
- problem: The reader is told twice that the link is not Auden's, first when he "leaves the reader to connect" and again in "and not Auden's", an "X, and not Y" tail. The sentence before runs to 40 words on an "and".
- fix: End the second sentence at "get out." Then write: "He does not say how the remark follows from the rest. This site's way of connecting the two is that the damned", and go on as now.
- weight: should

### week1.questions.2.auden.paras.1
- where: He quotes them while writing about Caliban
- problem: The second sentence repeats the verb of the first to add one fact, and "a character" is an introduction in form only. The next paragraph turns to "a merely natural creature", and nothing has told the student that Caliban is one.
- fix: "[Essay title kept] is an essay by Auden on masters and servants in literature. Writing there about Caliban, the wild islander enslaved by the magician Prospero in Shakespeare's *The Tempest*, he quotes three lines from Virgil's speech in *Purgatorio* XVII." The editor may describe Caliban in other words; the need is to say what he is.
- weight: should

### week1.questions.2.auden.paras.2
- where: He says that animals cannot fall
- problem: Both sentences of a two-sentence paragraph open on an attribution, and the first is the stiff "Auden's comment is that". One attribution meets house rule 6 for the pair.
- fix: Open the first sentence "Auden comments that". In the second, cut "He says that" and begin "Animals cannot fall".
- weight: should

### week1.questions.2.auden.paras.3
- where: The lines Auden quotes put the fault in
- problem: This sentence says again what the last sentence of the first paragraph said, with "quotes" for the fourth time in the block and an "X, and none in Y" tail. The site's ownership then arrives after its reading, as a closing formula.
- fix: Cut this sentence and the two after it. End the paragraph: "Auden does not himself apply the lines to the question. This site does, and takes them to mean that people can be blamed for a love they have chosen."
- weight: should

### week1.making.1.table.key.rows.8.what
- where: A river of boiling blood. A wood
- problem: Three fragments of one shape name the three places and do not say what is done in the first and the third, which is what the column asks. The row below does the same job with participles after "In the same order".
- fix: "In the same order: sunk in a river of boiling blood; turned into trees in a wood, where those who wasted their own goods are hunted by dogs; kept on burning sand under a rain of fire."
- weight: could

### week1.making.1.table.key.after.1
- where: punish incontinence, which here means lack of self-control
- problem: Two "which" clauses hang on one sentence, the second a gloss wedged in after the first.
- fix: "The second, third, fourth and fifth circles lie outside the city of Dis and punish incontinence, that is, lack of self-control."
- weight: could

### week2.intro.1
- where: two Greek tragedies: read one
- problem: A three-word command is followed by a statement that ends in a two-word command after a colon, and the clipped rhythm calls attention to itself. The two plays are not named, so the paragraph cannot be followed without the line above it.
- fix: Replace the first two sentences with: "Finish the *Comedy* by reading the *Paradiso*. Then read one Greek tragedy, either the *Agamemnon* of Aeschylus or the *Antigone* of Sophocles, as the list allows."
- weight: should

### week2.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: guilty of something else. That something is hubris
- problem: The noun is held back for a sentence and then delivered. That is the staged reveal of house rule 3, done with a period in place of the colon. The tense also shifts from "was" to "is".
- fix: Run the two sentences together after "was already": "guilty of a further fault, hubris, which is the confidence", and go on as now.
- weight: should

### week2.questions.2.auden.paras.1
- where: opens with a dream that Wordsworth describes
- problem: The dream is not explained and is not used again, and Wordsworth is not introduced (house rule 11). The clause does no work for a student writing on Horace.
- fix: cut, from "opens" to "and then", so that the sentence goes from "The first of them" straight to "sets out".
- weight: could

### week2.questions.2.auden.paras.2
- where: In ordinary times, he says, society is pictured
- problem: "he says" stands in the same slot, after an opening phrase, in three of the five sentences. The last sentence also comes late: it is the other half of the contrast made in the third, and the example stands between them.
- fix: Move the last sentence up to follow the third and take out its "he says", which the third sentence then covers. Open the sentence on I.14 with "As an example of the ship in danger Auden quotes".
- weight: should

### week2.questions.2.auden.paras.3
- where: Jason, who is sailing to fetch the Fleece
- problem: Jason and "the Fleece" are given as if the student knew the story (house rule 11). In the same paragraph "then" can be read as a word of time, and "cross it" has to reach back to "sea" inside an idiom (house rule 10).
- fix: Three small changes. "Jason, the Greek hero sent to bring back the Golden Fleece, would be glad if it were nearer." Before that, "a voyage is therefore a necessary evil". At the end, "the attempt to cross the sea".
- weight: should

### week2.questions.2.auden.paras.4
- where: until you reach *Moby-Dick*
- problem: The novel is named without its author, though week 1 gives Kierkegaard's book its author and a description (house rule 11).
- fix: "until you reach Herman Melville's novel *Moby-Dick* in weeks 11 to 13."
- weight: could

### week2.making.1.brief.1
- where: from whichever of German, French and Latin
- problem: The reader has to stop and work out "whichever of" three languages "the student had not studied". The phrase also supposes that every student had studied exactly two of them.
- fix: "At Michigan Auden made each student translate a poem from a language the student had not studied, which had to be German, French or Latin, and allowed no help but a dictionary."
- weight: should

### week2.making.1.source.1
- where: he took a poem of Rilke’s
- problem: The sentence is there to show which language Chapman worked from, and a reader who does not know Rilke learns nothing from it (house rule 11). "took" has just been used of courses, in another sense.
- fix: flag only. If Chapman's account gives the language, write it in, for example: "Chapman says that the poem he chose was a German one, by Rilke."
- weight: could

### week2.making.2.brief.1
- where: each group of three, called a tercet
- problem: The term is taught and then never used. In its place "three" serves as a noun five times across the exercise ("each three", "the threes", "four threes", "every three", "one three"), a quaint word where the text has just supplied the ordinary one.
- fix: In the first sentence write "grouped in threes, called tercets". After that use the term: "each tercet" twice in the second sentence, with "called a tercet" cut; "kept the tercets" in brief.2; "four tercets" in rules.1; "every tercet" and "one tercet into the next" in rules.3.
- weight: should

## Patterns

1. Attribution piled up. Of the 58 sentences in the Auden paragraphs, 35 carry a verb that attributes ("says", "adds", "writes", "quotes", "calls", "According to" and the like), and in 12 of the 17 paragraphs two or more sentences in a row each carry their own. Twenty-two sentences open on "Auden", "Auden's", "He" or "According to", and 17 of them are among the 33 sentences of week 1. By my count house rule 6 could be met with about two thirds as many. See week1.reading.2.auden.paras.2 and week2.questions.2.auden.paras.2. The cure is to let one verb cover the sentence after it, and to stop announcing a claim before making it.
2. Two full statements joined by ", and": 18 sentences. Each passes by itself, but the build repeats until it is heard. See week1.reading.2.paras.1, where three of the five sentences have it, and week2.making.1.source.1.
3. A name brought in with no introduction, or with an empty one (house rule 11): 6, namely Tennyson, Caliban, Wordsworth, Jason, Melville and Rilke. The two plays in week2.intro.1 are not named at all. See week1.words.after.1 and week2.questions.2.auden.paras.3.
4. The site's ownership stated as a tail: 4. Three blocks close on it and one carries it in mid-sentence, and the wording moves between "this site's" and "the site's". See week1.questions.2.auden.paras.3 and week2.making.1.source.1.
5. A paragraph closed on a quotation from Auden: 3. Each is fair by itself. See week1.reading.2.auden.paras.2 and week2.questions.1.auden.paras.4.
6. A block that opens "Auden wrote": 3 of the 6 Auden blocks. See week1.words.after.1 and week2.questions.1.auden.paras.1.
7. An "X, and not Y" or "X, and none in Y" tail: 2. See week1.questions.1.auden.paras.3 and week1.questions.2.auden.paras.3.
