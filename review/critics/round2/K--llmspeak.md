# Part K (week 14), lens: writing that reads as machine-made

### week14.intro.1
- where: This week you read Rilke's one novel
- problem: House rule 11. Rilke and Kafka get no first name and no word of introduction in the week's opening, and the student is not told that Rilke was a poet until the exercise.
- fix: This week you read the one novel of the poet Rainer Maria Rilke, about 190 pages, and the first six chapters of *The Castle*, by the Prague writer Franz Kafka, about 95 more.
- weight: should

### week14.intro.3
- where: Auden gave his class until the following Tuesday
- problem: A pointer cut loose from its noun. No day or lecture has been named, so the reader cannot tell what the Tuesday follows.
- fix: Auden assigned the whole book at one lecture, to be read by the Tuesday after. (The editor should confirm against Pearce that the Tuesday is the one after that lecture.)
- weight: should

### week14.questions.1.text
- where: the notebook shows Malte to have had
- problem: The list is of cities seen, illnesses, deaths watched. A person does not "have" those, and the earlier sentence already uses the right verb. The odd verb comes back in the reply prompt of auden.paras.4.
- fix: End the question: "...and say which items on it Malte has lived through, so far as the notebook shows." In auden.paras.4 write "take one item that you said Malte has lived through".
- weight: could

### week14.questions.1.auden.paras.1
- where: Auden put this book on the list, and nothing
- problem: Two facts that pull against each other are joined with "and". The sense is "but".
- fix: Change ", and nothing" to ", but nothing".
- weight: should

### week14.questions.1.auden.paras.1
- where: remembered that one lecture that fall went on to
- problem: Four uses of "that" in one sentence, and the first two can be read as "that one lecture". The sentence also runs three clauses together and ends on a vague "it".
- fix: Donald Pearce, a student in the class, remembered that in one of the fall lectures Auden went on to Rilke's *Duino Elegies*. Pearce was too tired that night to take down what Auden said about them, and that part of the lecture is lost.
- weight: should

### week14.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: and Auden, pointing back to it, called it
- problem: Two uses of "it" side by side with different owners (the earlier book, then the passage), and "passage" is used twice in the one sentence. The reader has to sort out which is which.
- fix: Read had quoted the entry in an earlier book. Auden referred his readers to that book and called the entry a fine passage on [rest of the sentence unchanged].
- weight: should

### week14.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: Auden said what he thought Rilke had found
- problem: The sentence only announces the next one, which house rule 8 forbids. It also sets "said" beside "he says" and "Auden adds", so the tense changes between the first sentence and the second.
- fix: In September 1939 Auden reviewed an English version of the *Duino Elegies* for *The New Republic*. A poet's standing problem, he says there, is [rest of the paragraph unchanged].
- weight: should

### week14.questions.1.auden.paras.4
- where: That observation is this site's and not Auden's
- problem: A whole sentence spent on the disclaimer, in the mirrored "X and not Y" form, when one phrase in the sentence before would do the job. The same flat disclaimer sentence opens questions.2.auden.paras.5.
- fix: Begin the paragraph "As this site reads it, Malte's list is mostly of things seen and heard" (keep the colon and the six examples), and cut the second sentence.
- weight: could

### week14.questions.1.auden.paras.4
- where: about waiting for memories to come back in mind
- problem: "Keep" and "in mind" are nine words apart, so the reader takes "come back in mind" as one phrase and has to start the sentence again.
- fix: For this week's fourth question, on how a poem gets written, keep in mind Malte's sentence about waiting for memories to come back.
- weight: must

### week14.questions.1.auden.sources.2
- where: *New Verse*, April–May 1936
- problem: House rule 1 keeps the en-dash for ranges of numbers. This one joins two month names.
- fix: flag only. Write "April and May 1936", or let the rule admit date ranges in citations.
- weight: could

### week14.questions.2.gloss.1
- where: A little before it Malte has written a note
- problem: The sentence has "it" twice with two owners, first the retelling and then the note, and "you will need it" is tacked on with "and".
- fix: You will need the note about loving and being loved that Malte writes in the margin a little earlier.
- weight: should

### week14.questions.2.auden.paras.1
- where: What is on record is what he thought
- problem: A "what is ... is what ..., and what" frame that announces the next three paragraphs and says little itself. "What he made of him" then sets two pronouns with different owners side by side.
- fix: Nothing by Auden about this ending has been found. Auden did review two books of Rilke's for *The New Republic* in the two years before the course, and Rilke appears in a long poem that Auden published in 1941.
- weight: should

### week14.questions.2.auden.paras.2
- where: Auden will not have this called defeatism
- problem: A quaint turn of phrase where the ordinary verb would do, and "this" has no single owner (Rilke's reason, or the attitude it defends).
- fix: Auden denies that this attitude is defeatism.
- weight: should

### week14.questions.2.auden.paras.2
- where: to others and to himself unless he asks
- problem: House rule 12. The writer here is any writer, and the paraphrase gives "himself", "he" and "his" four times in the one sentence.
- fix: Writers, he says, can do harm to others and to themselves unless they ask, far more humbly than they have been asking, what sort of people they are and what their real work may be.
- weight: should

### week14.questions.2.auden.paras.3
- where: not understanding was his whole occupation
- problem: The clause is hung on a long sentence with "and", and "by his own account not understanding" is read wrongly at first pass. "His" could be Auden's or Rilke's.
- fix: End the sentence at "Munich" and go on: "By Rilke's own account, his whole occupation in those years was not to understand."
- weight: must

### week14.questions.2.auden.paras.3
- where: to refuse that payment with one's eyes open
- problem: House rule 7: the paraphrase borrows "one". The sentence is also four clauses strung on "and ... and ... and", too long to follow in one reading.
- fix: Put a period after "hatred" and replace the rest: "To refuse that payment knowingly, he adds, is itself an act, and it takes great courage."
- weight: should

### week14.questions.2.auden.paras.3
- where: He recommends Rilke, in the second war, as
- problem: The phrase between commas sits where it reads as "Rilke in the second war".
- fix: Begin "With a second war under way, he recommends Rilke as" and keep the rest.
- weight: should

### week14.questions.2.auden.paras.4
- where: Auden also saw a danger in him
- problem: A topic sentence that states Auden's mind with nothing behind it but the poem that follows, and "him" has no owner in this paragraph. "Liking cooled further in later years" does the same: it sums up in advance what the 1956 remark shows.
- fix: Cut the first sentence and begin "In *New Year Letter*, the long poem Auden published in 1941, the Devil hands" (rest of that sentence unchanged). Then cut the clause about his liking cooling and begin the last sentence "In a lecture of 1956 Auden said [rest unchanged]".
- weight: should

### week14.questions.2.auden.paras.5
- where: set the son's plea beside both of Auden's Rilkes
- problem: Three faults in two sentences. The first sentence only announces the second. "Auden's Rilkes" is a coy plural the student must decode. The alternatives that follow run to forty words, with "another life that Auden admired" misattached, a mock-antique "shall", and "his" far from the son.
- fix: This site asks you, in your reply, to set the son's plea beside the two views of Rilke that Auden took. Say whether the son is asking his family for what Auden admired in Rilke in 1939, a refusal to interfere in another person's life, or whether his plea is closer to the wish quoted by Auden's Devil in 1941.
- weight: must

### week14.questions.3.text
- where: a useful guide and counsellor
- problem: British spelling in the site's own type, against house rule 13. The gloss says the words are Auden's, but they stand without quotation marks.
- fix: flag only. Spell it "counselor", or keep the recorded spelling and say in the gloss that the spelling is the source's.
- weight: could

### week14.questions.3.auden.paras.1
- where: Donald Pearce was a student in the class, and
- problem: The introduction of Pearce has been bolted to an unrelated statement with "and". It reads as a repair, and the reader waits a full clause to learn why he is named.
- fix: The earliest connected notes kept by Donald Pearce, a student in the class, come from the lecture at which Auden assigned *The Castle*.
- weight: should

### week14.questions.3.auden.paras.1
- where: he questioned: modern psychology, he said, finds many selves
- problem: The attribution is made twice in one sentence and a third time in the next. After "he questioned" and the colon nobody doubts whose view follows.
- fix: Replace "he questioned: modern psychology, he said, finds" with "he questioned on the ground that modern psychology finds". Keep "he said" in the next sentence.
- weight: should

### week14.questions.3.auden.paras.2
- where: only in disguise. It is in fact, he said
- problem: With the quotation set before "only in disguise", the sentence can be read as saying the book is a disguised satire, which is the opposite of what Auden told the class.
- fix: Auden told the class that [quotation kept] was only the book's disguise. (The next sentence stands.)
- weight: must

### week14.questions.4.auden.paras.2
- where: by chance alone, and is more like an act
- problem: A contrast that Auden drew has been flattened into "does not ..., and is ...", which looks like a "not X but Y" repaired by rule. "Getting in touch ... does not come" is also loose grammar. House rule 2 allows the plain "but" here, since will and chance are the answers anyone would give.
- fix: The poet reaches it, he said, neither by will alone nor by chance alone, but by something more like an act of faith.
- weight: should

### week14.questions.4.auden.paras.3
- where: and Kafka pictures the link between them
- problem: Two statements are joined with "and" into a sentence of 53 words that then runs on through "and ... or ... with ... and". The reader loses the telephone exchange before the end.
- fix: Put a period after "unconscious" and begin the next sentence "Kafka, he said, pictures the link" (rest unchanged).
- weight: should

### week14.questions.4.auden.paras.4
- where: one that every poet asks at many points
- problem: "Heard in ... one that" keeps the reader waiting across a whole quotation for the noun, and "heard in" is a fancy stand-in for the verb of saying.
- fix: Auden said that every poet, at many points in writing a poem, asks Wordsworth's question, [quotation kept]. He put it as a caller on a bad telephone line would: [quotation kept]
- weight: could

### week14.questions.4.auden.paras.5
- where: the essay he printed twenty years later
- problem: An author publishes an essay; a press prints it. Elsewhere in this week "printed" is used rightly of what the site prints, so here it reads as a quaint substitute.
- fix: Write "the essay he published twenty years later".
- weight: could

### week14.making.1.rules.4
- where: Do not look at any translation until you have
- problem: Rule 2 has already barred published translations. Of four rules, two give the same order.
- fix: Rule 2: "Work from the word-list alone. Use no dictionary, and do not look at a published translation until you have finished." Rule 4: "When you finish, the site shows the translation that Jessie Lemont published in 1918."
- weight: should

### week14.making.1.translate.wordsIntro.1
- where: marked as a command is giving an order
- problem: The sentence explains a word by its synonym and tells the student nothing about what to write.
- fix: Where a verb is followed by "A command", the line uses the form that gives an order: for legen, "lay".
- weight: should

## Patterns

Most frequent first. The second question's block (questions.2.auden.paras.1 to 5) is the weakest stretch of the part and holds eight of the findings. The intro apart from two sentences, the test items, the glosses to questions 3 and 4, the word-list and the note shown after the translation are clean.

1. A pronoun or pointer with no single owner nearby ("it", "him", "his", "this", "the following"): 9. See week14.questions.1.auden.paras.2 and week14.questions.2.gloss.1. Also intro.3, questions.1.auden.paras.1, questions.2.auden.paras.1 to 5.
2. Statements joined with "and" that should be two sentences, or that need "but": 8. See week14.questions.3.auden.paras.1 and week14.questions.4.auden.paras.3. Formulas, not yet a tic within this week but worth counting across weeks: the flat disclaimer sentence ending "is this site's", 3 (week14.questions.1.auden.paras.4, week14.questions.2.auden.paras.5, and making.1.source.1); the closing "In your reply," 2 (the same two blocks), while the blocks for questions 3 and 4 give the student nothing to reply to; "has been found" 3 (week14.questions.1.auden.paras.1, week14.questions.3.auden.paras.4), each time for a different fact, so leave it unless other weeks open the same way.
