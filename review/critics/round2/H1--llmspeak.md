# Part H1 (week 9), lens: writing that reads as machine-made

Findings are in the order of the text. Quotations are not copied, and a title that the text sets in quotation marks is shown as [title kept]. Where a fix keeps a quotation it is shown as [quotation kept]. Where a fix keeps a run of the present wording it says "the rest as it stands".

### week9.intro.1
- where: the drive to Mokroe
- problem: Outside this lens, but it should not be passed over. The name of the village is the answer to week9.test.items.6.q, so the first paragraph of the week gives a test answer away.
- fix: In place of those four words write "Mitya's drive out of town", and change "Mitya's arrest" after it to "his arrest". Or replace test item 6.
- weight: should

### week9.reading.2.paras.1
- where: The English printed here calls it
- problem: "The English" stands by itself for the English translation, a clipped phrase that makes the student supply the noun. If the reader prints the Italian beside the English the phrase can stand.
- fix: The English translation printed here is titled *The Lost One*.
- weight: could

### week9.reading.2.auden.paras.1
- where: The most that can be done
- problem: Seven sentences carry five attributions (the essay says, he says, he says, Auden's example is, he adds), and "he says" comes in two sentences running, where the first already covers the second under house rule 6. The opening also names Auden only inside a gloss on the book, so each later "he" has to reach back into an aside.
- fix: Reorder the first sentence so that Auden's name leads: begin "Auden's essay [title kept], in", follow with the book and its gloss (with "he" for "Auden"), and go on with "says that" and the rest as it stands. In the fifth sentence cut ", he says," so that "done" is followed directly by "is to hint".
- weight: should

### week9.reading.2.auden.paras.2
- where: as a novel does, as people
- problem: Two "as" phrases stand back to back, with "as well as" after them, and the reader has to take the sentence twice to see which "as" compares and which describes.
- fix: So opera cannot do what a novel does and show people who could turn out good or bad (the rest as it stands).
- weight: should

### week9.reading.2.auden.paras.3
- where: which puts its people where speech
- problem: Three clauses have been joined into one sentence, and the last of them is loose: "where" names no place, and "its people" is a quaint stand-in for the characters named in the paragraph before.
- fix: End the first sentence at "probable." Begin the next with "A good one is a melodrama," and finish it: which puts its characters in situations where speech would not be enough. In the last sentence write "the characters talk" to match.
- weight: should

### week9.test.items.2.where
- where: Book VIII, Chapter 1
- problem: The place is given in two forms that take turns down the test. The seven odd-numbered items give part, book with its title and chapter with its title; the seven even-numbered items (2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14) give book and chapter number only. It looks like two templates.
- fix: Give the seven even-numbered items the long form the odd ones have: Part, Book with its title, Chapter with its title. Or cut all fourteen down to book and chapter number.
- weight: should

### week9.questions.1.gloss.1
- where: where the telling breaks off
- problem: "The telling" is a quaint word where the ordinary one would do, and the first sentence says the night "is" a chapter.
- fix: In the first sentence write "is told in" for "is". In the second write "the story" for "the telling".
- weight: should

### week9.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: shows that one member has fallen
- problem: In a paragraph about a murder "has fallen" can be read as "has been killed", and the rest of the sentence still makes sense that way, so the student may never see that the point is guilt. The last sentence also opens with "What it gives the reader … is", an emphatic turn where a plain subject and verb would do.
- fix: The murder shows that one member is no longer innocent, and until (the rest as it stands). Last sentence: It gives the reader, he says, the illusion of (the rest as it stands).
- weight: must

### week9.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: He puts the difference in a
- problem: The sentence only announces the quotation, and "the difference" has nothing in this paragraph to be a difference between. The two kinds of book were set apart in the paragraph before.
- fix: He states the difference between the detective story and the work of art: [quotation kept]
- weight: should

### week9.questions.1.auden.paras.4
- where: and it has the body
- problem: "It has … and it has" is doubled for the beat and "the body" is coy. In the next sentence "the others" has nobody to be other than; the people meant were last named two paragraphs back.
- fix: Try the formula on *The Brothers Karamazov*, which has the closed family and the murdered man. Watch what becomes of the innocence of the rest of the family as the inquiry goes on.
- weight: should

### week9.questions.2.text
- where: Can a man be guilty of
- problem: A breach of house rule 12, reported as the brief asks; the lens otherwise leaves the questions alone. The question is about a person in general and has "a man" and "he". If the wording is on record as Auden's it needs quotation marks instead.
- fix: Can a person be guilty of something they did not do?
- weight: should

### week9.questions.2.auden.paras.1
- where: The essay has an account of
- problem: The second sentence only frames what is coming, and uses "account" twice to do it. The third ends on "the having is what makes him", a turn made for emphasis with a quaint noun in it.
- fix: Second sentence: In the essay Auden confesses to the kind of guilt the question asks about. Third sentence: after "tempt him" write ", he says, but" for ", though", and end the sentence with "and having them makes him feel guilty."
- weight: should

### week9.questions.2.auden.paras.2
- where: a person becomes, he says, that
- problem: "he" follows straight on "a person" and for a moment belongs to it. The paragraph has no "Auden" in it to steady the pronoun.
- fix: Write "Auden says" for "he says" in that sentence.
- weight: could

### week9.questions.2.auden.paras.3
- where: Notice that he does not claim
- problem: "it" has two possible owners, the offer and the guilt, and the sentence makes sense with either, so the student cannot tell which of them Mitya declines. The paragraph also opens on a cleft that ends in a preposition, and the disclaimer stands as a formula sentence ahead of the reading it disclaims.
- fix: Open with: Auden thinks that readers of detective stories want to escape this guilt. For the third and fourth sentences: As this site reads the novel, Mitya could take what the daydream offers. He says that he did not strike the blow, and if he did not, he could leave the guilt to whoever did. Close with: Notice that he turns the offer down, and that he accepts (the rest as it stands).
- weight: must

### week9.questions.2.auden.paras.4
- where: which says that a person would
- problem: The block ends on a verse of Scripture from which the site draws nothing, and an ending of that sort is a pose. The clause before the colon may also say once what the quoted words then say again (the quotation is masked in my copy).
- fix: flag only. Either move the sentence up to follow the first sentence of week9.questions.2.auden.paras.1, where the essay is introduced, or cut it. If the quoted words can stand without help, cut the "which says" clause and keep the quotation.
- weight: could

- problem: A breach of house rule 11. Campion is given no word of introduction at his first mention in the week; the student learns when he wrote only from the date in the next paragraph.
- fix: One Saturday he gave them a poem by the Elizabethan poet Thomas Campion (the rest as it stands).
- weight: could

### week9.making.1.answer.after.1
- where: Then look at where the argument
- problem: The second sentence tells the student to look for the turn and the third says at once where it is, so the second only announces the third (house rule 8).
- fix: Keep the first sentence. Then: It changes direction at Campion's third stanza, which begins a contrast that his fourth finishes.
- weight: should

### week9.making.1.answer.after.2
- where: how those two lines keep
- problem: "Those two lines" points at lines the note has not named, and "that is how" dresses a plain fact as a small reveal.
- fix: Campion sounds [first word kept] as one syllable and [second word kept] as two, and the two lines in which those words stand keep the measure only if the words are sounded so.

### week9.making.1.source.1
- where: The choice of the second
- problem: Two short sentences do one job, and the second is the "is this site's" formula that also carries the disclaimer in week9.questions.2.auden.paras.3. One sentence with an active verb makes the disclosure once.
- fix: Join the last two sentences: put a comma after "removed" and write "and this site chose the second and third."
- weight: could

## Patterns

1. A verb of saying in nearly every sentence (10 in the 13 sentences of the three paragraphs on opera, 5 of them in the 7 sentences of the first). See week9.reading.2.auden.paras.1 and week9.reading.2.auden.paras.3. The frame "Auden's example is" also opens a sentence twice, in week9.reading.2.auden.paras.1 and week9.questions.1.auden.paras.3, with "one of his examples" between them.
2. A quaint or clipped plain word where the ordinary one would do (5): "The English", "its people", "the telling", "the body", "the having". See week9.questions.1.gloss.1 and week9.questions.2.auden.paras.1.
3. A pointer with no owner in its own paragraph, or with two (4), and one word with two senses (1): "the difference", "the others", "it" after "claim", "those two lines", and "fallen". See week9.questions.1.auden.paras.3 and week9.questions.2.auden.paras.3. Both findings marked "must" are of this kind.
4. An emphatic turn where a plain subject and verb would do (4 flagged): "What it gives … is", "the having is what", "This guilt is what", "that is how". See week9.questions.1.auden.paras.2 and week9.questions.2.auden.paras.3. A fifth, "it is society that" in week9.questions.1.auden.paras.1, earns its emphasis and is not flagged, but the reader hears five.
5. A sentence that only frames or announces the next (3). See week9.questions.1.auden.paras.3 and week9.making.1.answer.after.1; the third is in week9.questions.2.auden.paras.1.
6. The same gloss on *The Dyer's Hand* in the same words (3), and the essay on detective stories introduced twice in nearly one sentence. See week9.reading.2.auden.paras.1 and week9.questions.1.auden.paras.1; the third is in week9.questions.2.auden.paras.1. This is no fault if a student sees one block alone. A student who reads the note on the opera and then one of the two blocks on the essay meets the gloss twice. No finding is entered for it.
7. A paraphrase followed at once by the quotation, which may then say the same thing a second time (3 possible; it cannot be judged with the quotations masked). See week9.reading.2.auden.paras.1 and week9.questions.2.auden.paras.4; the third is in week9.reading.2.auden.paras.3. The editor should check each pair and keep one half where they match.
8. "X is this site's" as a sentence by itself (2). See week9.questions.2.auden.paras.3 and week9.making.1.source.1.
