## Patterns

Most frequent first.

1. A repair from the first round that damaged the sentence it touched (things joined on "and" or on commas, a cut that dropped the link between two sentences, a marker put where it misleads): 6. See week3.words.after.1 and week4.questions.2.auden.paras.1; also week3.questions.2.auden.paras.1, week3.questions.2.auden.paras.4, week4.questions.1.auden.paras.2, week4.questions.2.auden.paras.2.
2. The report of Auden stops short of the question the student answered, or reaches it late, so the reply the student must write has little to hold on to: 4. See week3.questions.1.auden.paras.4 and week4.questions.2.auden.paras.3; also week3.questions.1.auden.paras.3, week4.questions.3.auden.paras.3.
3. Something a new reader needs before starting, or would wrongly assume, that the entry does not settle: 3. See week4.intro.1 and week4.making.1.brief.2; also week3.intro.1.
4. A pronoun with two possible owners: 3. See week4.questions.3.auden.paras.3 and week4.questions.1.auden.paras.2; also week3.test.items.3.ask (four entries).
5. A source or date the student cannot follow up: 2. See week3.questions.2.auden.paras.1 and week3.making.1.source.1.

What gave no trouble: the reading lists, the two settings, every .q question in both tests, the rules of the dictionary exercise and, but for one word, the key to the sixteen words. I found no em-dash, exclamation mark, hedge, rhetorical question or British spelling in the part, and no place where the paraphrase borrows Auden's "I", "we" or "one".
