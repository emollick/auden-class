# Part I (weeks 11 and 12), read as the student who has to use it (round 2)

Most of the questions, hints and test items can be acted on as written, and the tone is plain throughout. What gets in the way is concentrated in the reports of Auden: pronouns that could belong to Auden or to a character, and names dropped without a word on who they are. Two instructions could not be carried out as written, the rules of the exercise in week 11 and the last paragraph on the sermon.

## Week 11

### week11.intro.1
- where: This week you read the first third of
- problem: The book is never introduced. Nothing in weeks 11 and 12 gives Melville's first name, says that the book is a novel or gives its date, though the opening of week 14 introduces its two authors in a few words each.
- fix: First sentence: "The reading this week is the first third of *Moby-Dick*, the novel that Herman Melville published in 1851. It comes to about two hundred pages." Keep the rest.
- weight: should

### week11.intro.2
- where: copy of the list that has been published
- problem: The paragraph reports how the book is marked on a document and never says the plain thing I need to know, that the book is not required here. "you will find it divided into three parts" reads as a fact about Adams's book, when it is the site's schedule that divides it.
- fix: "*The Education of Henry Adams* comes just before *Moby-Dick* on Auden's reading list. Like *Faust*, it is marked [quotation kept] on the published copy of the list, so it is not required. If you choose to read it, the site splits it into three parts, one for each week you spend on *Moby-Dick*." (Editor: confirm that "not required" is how the site treats it.)
- weight: should

### week11.intro.3
- where: you need the canto you learned in weeks
- problem: "need the canto" sounds like having it open, and the same sentence then says to work from memory. I am also left unsure whether I may go over the canto before I start, since the exercise's first rule only says not to open it.
- fix: "This week's exercise is on Canto V of the *Inferno*, which you learned in weeks 4 to 6. You will do it from memory, with the canto closed." Then one more sentence that the editor must settle: either "Go over the canto before you open the exercise" or "Do not go over it first."
- weight: should

### week11.test.items.14.q

- problem: "raises" is the whalemen's word for sighting and is not explained. The whale's name is lower case here and capitalized everywhere in week 12.
- fix: Change "raises" to "sights" and capitalize "White Whale".
- weight: could

### week11.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: which Auden says his readers share
- problem: "his readers" could be Auden's, Ishmael's or Melville's. Further on, "it costs him no effort" contradicts what I read in Chapter 13, where Queequeg dives into a winter sea; the effort meant must be a struggle with himself.
- fix: Second sentence: "Ishmael starts with two fixed notions, and Auden says that readers of the book start with them too:" and then the two notions as they stand. Fifth sentence: "On the boat to Nantucket Queequeg saves a man who has just insulted him, and Auden's point is that he does it without any struggle with himself." (Editor: check against the pages cited that this is the effort Auden means.)
- weight: should

### week11.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: the act by which Ishmael accepts him
- problem: The question asked whether Ishmael's reasoning is sound, and this sentence is the only place where the report touches the idol. It gives Auden's view of the act and nothing on the argument, so I cannot tell where Auden and I differ.
- fix: flag only. If pp. 113–115 show that Auden passes over the argument, say so in one added sentence, for example: "Auden does not examine the paragraph of reasoning, and he judges the act by what it does for the two men." What is missing: whether Auden says anything about the reasoning itself.
- weight: could

### week11.questions.2.text
- where: delight belongs to the man who stands forth
- problem: "the man" is the site's paraphrase and stands outside the quotation, so house rule 12 applies to it. The closing question already says "one person".
- fix: Second sentence: "He ends by saying that delight belongs to whoever stands forth [quotation kept]."
- weight: could

### week11.questions.2.gloss.1
- where: in the opening paragraph of the sermon
- problem: Followed exactly, the hint sends me to the wrong paragraph: the sermon opens with a single sentence in which Father Mapple gives out his text, and the statement about obeying comes at the end of the long paragraph after it. "the last page of the chapter" means nothing in a reader that has no pages.
- fix: "Chapter 9. Father Mapple speaks of obeying God at the end of the first long paragraph of his sermon, just before he begins the story of Jonah. He speaks of delight in the last paragraph he says aloud, close to the end of the chapter."
- weight: should

### week11.questions.2.auden.paras.2
- where: which was the only gift he had
- problem: "which" hangs between the Word and the hearing of it, so I could not tell what the gift was. "the Word" also arrives with a capital letter and no gloss.
- fix: In the first sentence write "the Word of God" at the first mention. Fourth sentence: "Inside the whale he loses the one gift he had, for he can no longer hear the Word at all." (Editor: check that the gift Auden means is the hearing.)
- weight: could

### week11.questions.2.auden.paras.3
- where: He does not expect Peer to follow that
- problem: Auden never saw "the question", so the two statements should be called Father Mapple's; the play and its author are not named, and nothing recalls who the Button-moulder is. "He" comes straight after "His answer", which was Auden's, and "follow" can mean understand or obey.
- fix: "Auden grants that Father Mapple's two statements look like a contradiction. He answers with a speech from Ibsen's *Peer Gynt*, the play you read in week 7. The Button-moulder, who has come to melt Peer down, tells him that being oneself means slaying oneself. The Button-moulder doubts that Peer will understand this and says it again in other words, that being oneself means carrying out, in everything, what the Master intended."
- weight: should

### week11.questions.2.auden.paras.4
- where: and the application is this site’s
- problem: This is the paragraph I am meant to act on, and I read it three times. "the application" points to something not yet stated; the closing "Ask" does not say whether it is the task for my reply; the three ways the ego goes wrong are packed into one sentence of about sixty words, with the second in inverted order, so that I must count back to find "the third"; and I could not picture a self "left in balance".
- fix: "The answer depends on a distinction that Auden goes on to draw. A person, he says, is made up of a self and an ego. The self is whatever has been given to the person. It has powers of which it knows nothing, it cannot begin anything, and all it wants is to be left undisturbed. The ego is the part that decides. Auden says there are three ways in which it can go wrong: first, by refusing to look honestly at the self it has; second, by choosing a false god that it thinks it can manage over the true God; third, by knowing the self and what is asked of it and disobeying all the same, from weakness or from defiance. Remember the third way, and defiance most of all, when you come to Ahab. Auden himself does not assign either of Father Mapple's statements to the ego or to the self, and the suggestion that follows is this site's. In your reply, say of each statement whether the self it speaks of is the self a person is given or the ego that decides." (Editor: "left undisturbed" is my guess at "left in balance"; check it against the source. If the last sentence is meant only as a hint, say so.)
- weight: must

### week11.questions.3.auden.paras.1
- where: at which the spectator thinks what a pity
- problem: The second sentence carries two "it"s with different owners, and the third gives the matching thought for Christian tragedy without saying whose thought it is. Shakespeare then appears with no word on why he is there.
- fix: Keep the first sentence. Then: "He begins by calling Greek tragedy the tragedy of necessity, from which the spectator comes away thinking it a pity that things had to happen as they did. Christian tragedy he calls the tragedy of possibility, and there the spectator's thought is [quotation kept]. The Christian tragedies he has in mind are Shakespeare's and Melville's. He does not mean that either writer held the creed, only that their idea of what a person is comes from it." (Editor: confirm that Shakespeare is the essay's other example.)
- weight: should

### week11.questions.3.auden.paras.2
- where: he does it to himself
- problem: The paragraph ends on a colon and a five-word clause set for the beat, and "it" has to be worked out as the punishing. "He chooses" follows a sentence in which the last man named is Auden.
- fix: Last sentence: "Ahab chooses wrongly and keeps to his choice, and the punishment that follows, Auden says, is one he brings on himself."
- weight: should

### week11.questions.3.auden.paras.3
- where: Auden notes that he does not say
- problem: After "Auden notes that", each "he" reads first as Auden, and the last sentence, "He says to the whale", has the same fault, so I took Auden to be addressing the whale. Job is named with no word on who he is, "perhaps" floats between the husband and the monk, and "by what he could do" is too vague to picture.
- fix: Keep the first two sentences. Then: "His guess is that the loss told Ahab to stop being someone set apart from other people by his abilities, and to become either a happy husband and father or a monk. Ahab does neither. Auden notes that Ahab never says his punishment was just, and never says, as Job does in the Bible, that he will trust the one who slays him. Ahab tells the whale that it is the guilty one and that it shall be punished."
- weight: must

### week11.questions.3.auden.paras.4
- where: and for that reason willed to be miserable
- problem: "The price" opens the paragraph with nothing said of what is being paid for. In the third sentence "willed" comes twice with two subjects, so on a first reading it is God who wills to be miserable.
- fix: "Auden says that Ahab's own nature pays for this defiance. Nothing in that nature, he observes, wants to chase a whale around the world when Ahab is old and maimed, and by Ahab's own admission it wants peace, a family and happiness. Ahab acts as though he knew that God wanted these things for him too, and chose for that very reason to be miserable at every moment." Keep the last sentence as it is. (The paragraph now names Auden as the speaker in each of its four sentences; the fix drops one, and house rule 6 is still kept.)
- weight: should

### week11.making.1.brief.2

- problem: The exercise is titled with the word "emend" and nothing in the brief says what it means. The clause about the version trails after the changed words, so for a moment the changes seem to have been made in a version.
- fix: "This exercise puts the same question about a canto you know by heart. To emend a passage is to find the words that are wrong and put back the right ones. The last lines of Canto V of the *Inferno* are printed below in the version you chose for the cantos, and ten of the words have been changed."
- weight: could

### week11.making.1.rules.2

- problem: I do not know which book is meant, my version of the canto or Dante's Italian, or whether spelling counts.
- fix: "When you write a correction, capital letters and accents are ignored. In every other respect the word must be spelled as it is printed in your version of the canto."
- weight: should

### week11.making.1.rules.3
- where: until you have marked at least seven words
- problem: Ten words were changed and the page will not let me finish until I have marked seven, but the next rule tells me to mark a word only when I believe it is wrong and warns that wrong marks are counted. If I am sure of five, the two rules cannot both be kept, and nothing says which gives way.
- fix: flag only: the editor must decide whether the minimum of seven stays. If it stays: "To finish you must mark at least seven words and write a correction for each. If you are sure of fewer than seven, make up the number with your best guesses. The page shows which corrections were right only after you finish."

### week11.making.1.rules.4

- problem: "sound word" reads at first as a word about sound, and "alter" is a third verb for what the other rules call marking and correcting.
- fix: "Each unchanged word you mark is added up and reported with your result. Mark a word only if you believe it has been changed." (The result message in the app uses "altered" and "sound" in the same sense and would have to change with this rule.)
- weight: should

### week11.making.1.emend.after.1

### week11.making.1.emend.after.2

### week12.reading.3.auden.paras.2

### week12.reading.3.auden.paras.4

## Patterns

1. A person, book or term dropped without the few words house rule 11 asks for (11 entries). Melville and his book, the Button-moulder and Ibsen, Shakespeare, Job, the swain, Senta, the dream in Wordsworth and the wanderer of legend, Nietzsche, the Church Fathers, the *Iliad*, Kafka and the Grail. See week12.reading.3.paras.2 and week11.questions.3.auden.paras.3.

2. A pronoun or pointer with two possible owners, nearly always in a sentence that holds both Auden and the person he is writing about (11 entries). "he" after "Auden notes that", "His answer" followed by "He", "his readers", "willed" twice, "it" three times in one sentence. See week11.questions.3.auden.paras.3 and week12.questions.2.auden.paras.2.

3. An instruction I could not carry out exactly, or that does not say what happens next (8 entries). The minimum of seven marks against the warning not to guess; "Ask" with no word on whether it is the task for the reply; whether the canto may be gone over first; "find out what Melville does"; who judges the likeness to Melville. See week11.making.1.rules.3 and week11.questions.2.auden.paras.4.

4. One thing in two forms (4 entries). The whale's name with and without capitals, the ship as "her" and "its", "harpooners" beside Melville's spelling in the test, "mark" beside "alter". See week11.test.items.14.q and week12.reading.3.auden.paras.2.

5. A hint or paragraph that points by position and misses (3 entries). "the opening paragraph", "the last page", "the first twice", the first kind of meaning labelled only at the end of its paragraph. See week11.questions.2.gloss.1 and week12.questions.1.gloss.1.

6. A date the student needs and is not given (2 entries). The year of the novel, and the year behind "Late in life". See week11.intro.1 and week12.reading.3.auden.paras.4.

7. Auden named as the speaker in nearly every sentence of a paragraph (2 entries, the second not raised as a finding). Four times in four sentences in week11.questions.3.auden.paras.4, and four times in six in week12.questions.1.auden.paras.1. It is always clear who holds the opinion, and it would still be clear with half of them.

Facts my fixes depend on and that I do not have: whether Henry Adams is treated as not required (week11.intro.2); whether the canto may be reviewed before the exercise (week11.intro.3); what Auden means by the effort Queequeg is spared (week11.questions.1.auden.paras.2); the sense behind "left in balance" (week11.questions.2.auden.paras.4); whether Shakespeare is the essay's other example (week11.questions.3.auden.paras.1); which Ishmael is meant (week12.reading.3.auden.paras.1); the year of the Wagner essay (week12.reading.3.auden.paras.4).
