# Part G (weeks 7 and 8), read as the student

### week7.intro.1
- where: which is the longest book on the list
- problem: "Book" means a division of the novel and then the whole novel inside one clause, before a student who has not opened the novel knows it is divided into books. The sentence also carries the play, the novel, its length and its four weeks all at once.
- fix: This week you read Ibsen's verse play *Peer Gynt* and begin Dostoevsky's novel *The Brothers Karamazov*, the longest work on the list. The novel is divided into numbered books, and this week you read Books I to III. The whole novel takes four weeks, through week 10. [Leave the last sentence, on the opera, as it is.]
- weight: should

### week7.intro.3
- where: the canto you chose for yourself
- problem: Everywhere else (the By heart page, the examination, the Sources page) this is "the fifth canto" or "your fifth canto". Here it has a second name, and a student has to work out that the two are one thing.
- fix: Begin learning by heart this week your fifth canto, the one you chose for yourself. [Leave the second sentence as it is.]
- weight: could

### week7.reading.3.paras.3
- where: a new book that the list only recommends
- problem: One sentence lists four things and hangs a different aside on each of the last three, so it has to be read twice to sort what was on the list from what was not. "A new book" reads as new today; it was new in 1941.
- fix: For the lecture he assigned this libretto and the libretto of *Carmen* (the opera for week 8). He added two things that the list does not require: Shakespeare's *Romeo and Juliet*, which is not on it, and Denis de Rougemont's *Love in the Western World*, then a new book, which it only recommends. [First and last sentences unchanged.]
- weight: should

### week7.reading.3.auden.paras.2
- where: the two care so little who the other is
- problem: "The two" has no single "other", so the clause has to be taken apart and put together again.
- fix: For these words read "each cares so little who the other is". The rest of the sentence stands.
- weight: should

### week7.reading.3.auden.paras.3
- where: Auden holds that this is right
- problem: "This" could be what the audience sees, the consummation that never comes, or what Wagner intended, and "right" could mean proper or accurate. Only the sentences after it show that he means the stage picture is true of these lovers.
- fix: Auden holds that what the audience sees is the truth about these two.
- weight: should

### week7.questions.1.auden.paras.1
- where: to which the play gives several answers
- problem: The colon after "several answers" leads the student to expect the answers, and what follows it is the question.
- fix: He finds that one question, to which the play gives several answers, runs through the whole of it: who am I, and what is my real self?
- weight: could

### week7.questions.1.auden.paras.1
- where: that come near the animal's
- problem: The possessive has no noun. The sentence before gives the animal nothing to own except having no need to ask, so the student must supply "way of being itself". Two sentences on, "of one's own" borrows the generic "one" that house rules 7 and 12 exclude, and "the nearest" is said of two.
- fix: [The short sentence on the animal stays.] Auden names two ways of living in which a human being comes near to that state. The nearer is the second nature that heredity and custom give people. Next comes a life governed by a single overriding interest that is the person's own, like that of the peasant [rest of the sentence unchanged].
- weight: should

### week7.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: But Auden observes that the Peer of the play
- problem: "But" has nothing to push against. The paragraph before ends on Peer's wish to be an emperor, and nothing has said that Peer has strong appetites, so the student looks back for a contrast that is not there.
- fix: Cut "But" and begin the paragraph "Auden observes that".
- weight: could

### week7.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: where Peer plays at being each
- problem: "Where" is read at first for a place (in those two plays), which makes the sentence say that Peer acts in the plays a real poet would have written. It means "whereas".
- fix: Change "where" to "whereas".
- weight: should

### week7.questions.1.auden.paras.4
- where: can be true to it only by pretending
- problem: This sentence is the nearest the five paragraphs come to Auden's answer to the question the student has just written on, and "it" (the calling) hides the fact. The question and the human motto are about being true to oneself, and the reply the student must now write depends on seeing that this is Auden's answer.
- fix: In that clause read "people" for "anyone" and "true to themselves" for "true to it". The rest of the sentence stands. The editor should first check the essay at this point: if Auden speaks there of being true to oneself, this is his wording restored; if he speaks only of the calling, keep "it" and add a sentence saying what follows for the motto.
- weight: should

### week7.questions.2.auden.title
- where: What Auden wrote about the third
- problem: The question has just quoted a chapter title that gives Alyosha this very description, so the heading promises Auden on Alyosha. The first paragraph then says that nothing of the kind is recorded. The student is misled for a paragraph.
- fix: What Auden wrote about third sons in fairy tales
- weight: should

### week7.questions.2.auden.paras.1
- where: introduced a volume of the tales
- problem: "Introduced" can be taken as "presented", and "read one of them closely" as something Auden did in private, with "them" either the tales or the two authors. The student cannot tell that the tale in the next paragraph is one Auden discusses in his own introduction. Also, the sentence the student needs first, that no account records Auden on this novel, stands third, after two anecdotes.
- fix: For the last sentence: "In 1952 he wrote the introduction to a volume of tales by the brothers Grimm and by Hans Andersen, and in it he goes through one tale closely." If the heading is changed as proposed above, the order of the paragraph can stand. If it is not, move the third sentence to the head of the paragraph.
- weight: should

### week7.questions.2.auden.paras.4
- where: and often are so in what they think
- problem: "Are so" has to be traced back to "strong" across a clause, and the next sentence says "also" twice in six words. Both sentences were read twice.
- fix: People are seldom strong where they take pride in their strength. They are more often strong in something they think unimportant or count as a weakness. No one succeeds by will alone, since success takes luck as well. Auden's other name for luck is grace, and he says it is never refused to those who ask for it humbly.
- weight: should

### week7.questions.2.auden.paras.5
- where: the three reasons he gives why the hero passes
- problem: The instruction points at the wrong sentence. Two paragraphs up, "three reasons" are given for why the brothers ignore the dwarf, not for why the hero passes. What is said of the hero is three unnumbered qualities in the sentence after. A student who follows the pointer checks Alyosha against the brothers' faults. "Compare" and "check" also leave it unsaid that this is what the reply should contain.
- fix: Keep the first sentence. Then: "In your reply, take the three things Auden says of the hero in the third paragraph: he is humble enough to ask for help, he cares whether his father recovers, and he is well disposed toward everyone. Say which of them are true of Alyosha when Book III ends, and set that beside what you wrote."
- weight: must

### week8.intro.2
- where: the life and teaching of Father Zossima
- problem: Zossima is not introduced at his first mention in the week (house rule 11). The setting for the week's passage has the few words that are wanted here.
- fix: After "Father Zossima" add ", the elder of Alyosha's monastery".
- weight: could

### week8.reading.2.paras.1
- where: made for Vienna that fall by Ernest Guiraud
- problem: "That fall" points at a year the student has not been given. The date of the first performance does not come until the third paragraph, so the pointer has nothing to hold.
- fix: For "that fall" read "in the fall of 1875". (The third paragraph gives March 1875 for the first performance. The other way is to move that date up, after "At the first performance".)
- weight: must

### week8.test.items.12.q
- where: before he confesses had the mysterious visitor committed
- problem: A present tense and a past perfect are crossed in one clause, and the question has to be read twice to find its subject.
- fix: How many years pass between the mysterious visitor's murder and his confession?
- weight: should

### week8.questions.1.auden.paras.1
- where: to be stronger than one's neighbor
- problem: The paraphrase borrows the generic "one" (house rules 7 and 12), in a clause that has just said "people" and "themselves".
- fix: For "one's neighbor" read "their neighbors".
- weight: should

### week8.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: an attendant who only repeats his master's words
- problem: The student is not told why Auden changes the listener. The paragraph before says the Inquisitor needs no answer because his own experience has answered him. The point of the attendant, that he has had no such experience and so can be answered, is left to be guessed.
- fix: flag only. If the book gives the reason, add it after "his master's words", for example "and has not lived through what his master has". The editor must check this against pp. 48–58; I do not have the book.
- weight: could

### week8.questions.1.auden.paras.4
- where: though not the freedom of someone who could as
- problem: One sentence of thirty-one words describes the freedom that is not meant before the next says which freedom is meant, and the passive "what is offered" does not say who offers it. It was read twice.
- fix: [First sentence unchanged.] The reply grants that freedom is what Jesus offers. It sets aside the freedom of indifference, where a person might as well do this as that because no one thing matters more than the rest, and it names the freedom to know the truth. The Inquisitor claims to offer that too. [The quotation and the last sentence stay.]
- weight: should

### week8.questions.1.auden.paras.6
- where: founded it on miracle, mystery and authority
- problem: Three foundations are named and an answer is reported to two. A student counts them and wants to know what the reply says about mystery, the more so because this week's exercise singles out the same three words.
- fix: flag only. If the reply answers mystery, give its answer in one sentence between the sentence on miracle and the sentence on authority. If it does not, say so in the paragraph: "The reply says nothing about mystery."
- weight: should

### week8.questions.1.auden.paras.7
- where: Ask whether Dostoevsky's Prisoner could have made
- problem: The standing instruction over the reply box tells the student what to write unless these paragraphs ask for something else. "Ask whether" is an instruction to think. It does not say that these two questions are what the reply should answer.
- fix: For "Ask whether" read "In your reply, say whether".
- weight: should

### week8.questions.2.auden.paras.2
- where: because he asks to understand first
- problem: The question the student has just written on starts from Ivan's saying that he accepts God. This paragraph says that he does the reverse of believing first, and does not say how the two fit together, so the remark cannot be connected to the paper.
- fix: flag only; the reading is the site's and the editor must settle it. One wording that keeps both facts: "Ivan says that he accepts God, but he will not accept God's world until he has seen justice done in it. In that he does the reverse of what the definition says."
- weight: should

### week8.questions.2.auden.paras.3
- where: fated to be an exception and to suffer whatever
- problem: Two things. "To suffer whatever he did" is read first as "to undergo the things he did", though it means "to suffer no matter what he did". And Pascal comes in with no word on who he is (house rule 11), though the student read him two weeks ago and could be told so.
- fix: After "like Pascal" add "(whose *Pensées* you read in weeks 5 and 6)". For "to suffer whatever he did" read "to suffer no matter what he did".
- weight: should

### week8.questions.2.auden.paras.4
- where: Decide whether that difference makes his refusal
- problem: The same fault as in the paragraph that closes the first question: an instruction to think, where the student needs to know what the reply should say. The second half, about a book six weeks off, is a different kind of instruction and is hung on the first with "and".
- fix: For "Decide whether" read "In your reply, say whether". End that sentence at "weaker" and begin a new one at "Keep".
- weight: could

### week8.making.1.brief.1
- where: during the war, Auden and a colleague in philosophy
- problem: No year is given, and the student is left to place both "the war" and the college, which is named by one word. Most other pieces of Auden's in these two weeks carry a date.
- fix: Add "College" after the college's name, and for "during the war" read "during the Second World War". If Mandelbaum's essay gives the year in which the Navy unit was taught, use the year instead; I do not have it.
- weight: could

## Patterns

1. A sentence that had to be read twice because of its load or its word order: several asides on one list, a clause or a word placed where it misleads, tenses crossed. 9 findings. See week7.reading.3.paras.3 and week8.questions.1.auden.paras.4.
2. A pointing word or connective with nothing, or the wrong thing, to point at ("that fall", "this", "it", "the three reasons", "But"). 7 findings. See week8.reading.2.paras.1 and week7.questions.2.auden.paras.5.
3. Paragraphs on Auden that do not meet the question just asked, or do not deliver what their heading or first sentence leads the student to expect. 4 findings. See week7.questions.2.auden.title and week8.questions.2.auden.paras.2.
4. A closing instruction that says to compare, ask or decide, where the student needs to be told what the reply should contain. The reply box defers to these paragraphs, so the wording matters. 3 findings. See week8.questions.1.auden.paras.7 and week8.questions.2.auden.paras.4.
5. A person, place or occasion at first mention in the week with no few words of introduction or no date. 3 findings. See week8.questions.2.auden.paras.3 and week8.making.1.brief.1.
6. The generic "one's" carried into the paraphrase, against house rules 7 and 12. 2 findings. See week7.questions.1.auden.paras.1 and week8.questions.1.auden.paras.1.

Where the faults lie: 19 of the 26 findings are in the paragraphs that report Auden. The other seven are in the week introductions (3), the notes printed with the operas (2), one test question and one exercise brief.
