# Part H2 (week 10), lens: writing that reads as machine-made

Findings are in the order of the text. Nothing that the text has in quotation marks is copied here; where a fix keeps a quotation it is shown as [quotation kept].

### week10.intro.1
- where: about three hundred and twenty pages
- problem: The page count is spelled out at length here and again in week10.intro.2, while weeks 14 and 15 give theirs in figures. The long form reads as plainness put on.
- fix: "This is a long week, about 320 pages." In week10.intro.2: "is under 25 pages."
- weight: could

### week10.intro.2
- where: Rimbaud wrote it at eighteen
- problem: House rule 11. At his first appearance in the week's prose Rimbaud gets an age and a date, but no first name and no word on who he was.
- fix: Begin the sentence "Arthur Rimbaud, a French poet, wrote it at eighteen" and let it run on as it stands.
- weight: should

### week10.reading.3.paras.2
- where: between the two scenes of
- problem: "Scenes" has two meanings in one sentence. The act is said to have two, and the parenthesis then points to Scene 7, so a student following the printed libretto is stopped.
- fix: Say that many performances play the overture "during the change of set in the middle of the second act", and keep the parenthesis. The editor should check this against the numbering in the libretto printed.
- weight: could

### week10.reading.3.auden.paras.1
- where: is about the third *Leonore* overture
- problem: The opening sentence only announces (house rule 8). It names the book and the subject and keeps back what Auden says.
- fix: Open with the claim: "Auden says in *The Dyer's Hand*, his book of essays of 1962, that an opera-lover does not mind", and so on as the second sentence now runs. In the last sentence put "the third *Leonore* overture" for "this overture".
- weight: should

### week10.reading.3.auden.paras.2
- where: His reason is in the sentence
- problem: A paragraph of one sentence, held back and delivered after a colon so that Auden's maxim lands as a punch line. The pointer to "the sentence before" is no use to a student who does not have the book open.
- fix: Do not set it by itself. Run it on as the last sentence of the paragraph above: "His reason is a rule he states just before: [quotation kept]"
- weight: could

### week10.questions.1.auden.paras.1
- where: but the record does not say
- problem: "On record" and "the record" come in consecutive sentences, and the hint under the question has already said "not on record" once. Two of the four sentences exist only to say what is not known.
- fix: "Auden gave the question in 1942–43, his first year at Swarthmore College, as the whole final examination of a course on Elizabethan literature. The record has no answer of his and does not say which devil he meant, though a course on the Elizabethans points to the one in Marlowe's play *Doctor Faustus*." The last sentence stays as it stands.
- weight: should

### week10.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: Neither the poem nor the note
- problem: The closing sentence is a disclaimer that repeats the block. The paragraph before has said that no answer is on record, and this paragraph's first sentence has given the date. It is the second of three "this site" statements in four paragraphs.
- fix: cut
- weight: should

### week10.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: The lasting idolatry of Auden’s own
- problem: An abstract noun phrase is left hanging on "is of possibility". Auden is named and then "he says" follows in the same clause.
- fix: "Auden says that his own age makes an idol of possibility," and the rest of the sentence as it stands.
- weight: should

### week10.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: Auden’s point about actors is
- problem: The attribution has been wedged into the second half of the sentence as a clumsy noun phrase. It is not needed, because house rule 6 is met by the sentence before, and five of the six sentences in the paragraph carry a verb of saying.
- fix: "Mephisto is an actor, and actors have no name of their own." The sentence before, as revised above, carries the attribution for both.
- weight: should

### week10.questions.1.auden.paras.4
- where: that the visitor is only Ivan
- problem: The noun is said twice to dodge a pronoun, and the visitor is called both Ivan himself and one side of him.
- fix: "Ivan calls the visitor one side of himself, and the stupidest side."
- weight: should

### week10.questions.1.auden.paras.4
- where: fits Auden’s phrase for Mephisto
- problem: The three words after this are Auden's and stand in quotation marks in the paragraph above. Here they are run in without marks, as if they were the site's.
- fix: End the clause at "Mephisto" and drop the three words, or set them in quotation marks.
- weight: should

### week10.questions.2.auden.paras.1
- where: The evidence for what he thought
- problem: The sentence announces the paragraphs to come and repeats the heading over the block. Its "what … is what" is hard to take in, and "and a few remarks" is tacked on after a comma.
- fix: "What he thought of it shows in what he required of his classes and in a few remarks."
- weight: should

### week10.questions.2.auden.paras.2
- where: She says that Auden held
- problem: Three subject-first sentences of ten to fifteen words stand in a row (house rule 8), and "she says" comes in two sentences running.
- fix: "Dorothy Farnan, a graduate student at Michigan in 1941, did not take the course and reports what members of it told her. By her account Auden thought memory work a first-rate training for the mind, and of the class she says, using his first name: [quotation kept]"
- weight: should

### week10.questions.2.auden.paras.4
- where: and a like gratitude in
- problem: "The same resistance" and "a like gratitude" have nothing to be the same as or like, because the block has not said that Auden's class resisted or was grateful (house rule 10). "A like" is an antique word, and the balanced pair closes the block on a flourish.
- fix: "He says that he asked classes of his own to do the same, and that they resisted at first and were grateful in the end."
- weight: should

### week10.questions.3.auden.paras.1
- where: As a man of action, Auden
- problem: With the verb of saying wedged in behind it, the opening phrase attaches to Auden for a moment. "Auden" twice and "reviewed … review" fill two short sentences, and "a life of him" is a bookish way to say a biography.
- fix: "Reviewing a biography of him in 1939, Auden wrote that as a man of action Rimbaud [quotation kept]"
- weight: should

### week10.questions.3.auden.paras.2
- where: the hero of his own epic
- problem: House rule 12: an artist in general is "his". The number also shifts, from "the artist" here to "Artists of this kind" two sentences on.
- fix: "and that artists became the heroes of their own epics." Flag: the editor may judge that the argument is about men and leave it.
- weight: could

### week10.questions.3.auden.paras.2
- where: as their authors projected in imagination
- problem: One sentence carries three names, an aside to the student about next week, a phrase that parses two ways, and after a colon an abstract "less … than". It cannot be followed at one reading.
- fix: "He reads Faust, Don Juan and Captain Ahab as their authors' pictures of themselves. (Ahab is the captain in *Moby-Dick*, the novel you begin next week.) Such a figure, he says, does a thing mainly to learn what doing it feels like."
- weight: must

### week10.questions.3.auden.paras.2
- where: the subject of the experiment
- problem: No experiment has been named, so "the experiment" has no owner. "Artists … the one who" changes number, and "Auden says that" is wedged in where two clauses join.
- fix: "Artists of this kind, he says, experiment on themselves and keep the record," and the second half as it stands, without its "Auden says that".
- weight: should

### week10.questions.3.auden.paras.3
- where: he had taken himself for
- problem: "He" has two owners in one sentence: "he says" is Auden, and a few words later "he" is the artist. The sentence also drops from "They" to one artist called "he".
- fix: "They came to see, he says, that artists were not the religious heroes they had taken themselves for, but only explorers of possibility, like Ishmael," and the rest as it stands.
- weight: should

### week10.questions.3.auden.paras.4
- where: the story that Rimbaud chose to
- problem: The sentence reads as if Rimbaud put a stop to a story, because "stop" has lost its object. The next sentence then tells "the story" over again.
- fix: "In 1956 Auden took up what he calls the Rimbaud Myth, the tale of a great poet who gives up poetry by choice and not for lack of anything more to say. He says that it [quotation kept]"
- weight: must

### week10.making.1.rules.4
- where: take more trouble over the German
- problem: "Take more trouble over" is a British turn of phrase, and the handbook is meant to be in American English.
- fix: End the sentence "and put more work into the German in week 14."
- weight: could

### week10.making.1.translate.cite
- where: except that the magazine set
- problem: The exception is stated from the wrong side. It says what the magazine did and leaves the student to work out what this page does.
- fix: End the sentence "except that the Latin word, in italics in the magazine, is in roman type here."
- weight: could

## Patterns

1. A verb of saying in nearly every sentence: 23 of the 24 sentences that report Auden's prose (week10.reading.3.auden.paras.1 to 3, week10.questions.1.auden.paras.3, week10.questions.3.auden.paras.2 to 4) carry "Auden says", "he says", "he calls", "in his account" or the like. House rule 6 is met by one in the sentence or the one before. See week10.reading.3.auden.paras.3, where three sentences have three and the "he says" in the last can go, and week10.questions.3.auden.paras.2, where it is five of five. In three places the wedged verb damages the sentence; those are among the findings above.

2. Two statements joined by ", and": 17 sentences by my count. Most read naturally. See week10.reading.3.paras.1, where two unrelated facts are joined ("*Fidelio*, Beethoven's only opera, runs about two hours" does it in one clause), and week10.questions.1.auden.paras.3.

3. Statements of what is not on record: 5 in 4 entries (week10.questions.1.gloss.1, week10.questions.1.auden.paras.1 twice, week10.questions.2.auden.paras.1, week10.making.1.source.1). Each is honest and wanted. Only the doubling inside week10.questions.1.auden.paras.1 is a fault.

4. "This site" disclaimers: 4 in the part, and 3 of them in the four paragraphs of the block on the devil (week10.questions.1.auden.paras.1, paras.2 and paras.4). Two entries close on the same clause about the site's choice (week10.questions.1.auden.paras.1 and week10.making.1.source.1). Cutting the one in paras.2 is enough.

5. *The Dyer's Hand* glossed as a book of essays of 1962: 3 times in the week (week10.reading.3.auden.paras.1, week10.questions.1.auden.paras.3, week10.questions.2.auden.paras.3), twice in the same words. House rule 11 asks for the gloss once a week. If each block has to stand by itself, shorten the second and third to "*The Dyer's Hand* (1962)".

6. "He" or "his" for the artist or the poet in general: 3 (week10.questions.3.auden.paras.2, paras.3 and paras.4). The findings above deal with each.

7. Not found in this part: an em-dash, an exclamation mark, a rhetorical question, a hedge or a hype word of the site's own, a reflex group of three. The formula "In your reply, say whether" comes once and is no tic here. The test questions, the instructions for the exercise (week10.making.1.rules.1 to 3) and the rows of the word list that I could read are clean.
