# Part F (weeks 5 and 6), lens: writing that reads as machine-made

### week5.intro.2
- where: about two and a half hours of listening
- problem: The length of the opera is given here and again, in the same words, in the first line of the note on the opera. Meanwhile nothing in week 5 names the composer until the exercise speaks of how Mozart cast it; week 6 names him in its opening paragraphs and gives the length once, in the note.
- fix: "The opera this week is Mozart's *The Magic Flute*." Leave the second sentence as it is.
- weight: could

### week5.reading.4.paras.1
- where: That version cuts the spoken scenes short and has
- problem: Three clauses are chained with "and" and "so", and "there" has no plain owner. The closing sentence about the songs then seems to take back what the paragraph said about the Italian version, and its passive hides who did the translating. "Songs" and "talk" are quaint words in an opera note.
- fix: for the last two sentences write: "The Italian version shortens the spoken scenes and has them sung, and the English printed here does the same. You will therefore hear more speech than is printed. The words of the arias, though, were translated from the German." Also "between the arias and ensembles" earlier in the paragraph. (Editor: confirm the last fact and say who made that translation if it matters.)
- weight: should

### week5.reading.4.auden.paras.2
- where: He imagines Caliban answering Prospero’s offer of an education
- problem: The Caliban sentence hangs after the verdict with nothing to tie it to Papageno, so the student cannot tell why it is there. The short verdict before it is the mirror of the close of the paragraph above, and the pair reads as a set piece.
- fix: keep the first two sentences, then: "Auden likens that refusal to Caliban thanking Prospero for the offer of schooling and telling him that speech and clothes do not suit him and that the jungle is where he would sooner stay. He finds the opera too hopeful, as he found the play too gloomy." (Editor: check against pp. 134–135 that the Caliban remark is made about Papageno.)
- weight: should

### week5.reading.4.auden.paras.3
- where: With Chester Kallman, the American poet with whom he
- problem: The gloss added for Kallman puts two "with" phrases and a "he" ahead of the subject, so the sentence makes three starts before it says anything.
- fix: "Auden later made an English version of *The Magic Flute* with Chester Kallman, the American poet who was his partner in writing opera libretti. It was broadcast on American television in January 1956."
- weight: could

### week5.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: Auden’s conclusion, as Pearce has it:
- problem: A verbless label and a colon stage the quotation as the paragraph's last word; the same close is used in week 6 (see Patterns). Earlier, "done again" is a cramped way to say a second Polonius, and "now" leaves the reader to guess that it means in this last play.
- fix: three changes. For "which is now a plain thing" write "and that love here is a plain thing". For the Gonzalo clause write "Gonzalo, he said, is another Polonius, the old counselor in *Hamlet*, but one whom this play respects". For the last sentence write "Pearce gives Auden's conclusion as [quotation kept]".
- weight: should

### week5.questions.1.auden.paras.4
- where: and that his innocence was a savage’s
- problem: A possessive with no noun after it reads as a phrase bent to avoid quoting the source. It sounds gnomic where the rest of the paragraph is plain.
- fix: after "to himself" write "and lived there in the innocence of a savage."
- weight: could

### week5.questions.2.gloss.1
- where: Jonson expected the objection and answers it in the
- problem: "The objection" points at nothing. The question above asks whether the play is a comedy; nobody has yet objected to anything, so the student has to work out what Jonson is answering.
- fix: keep the first sentence. For the second write: "Jonson expected readers to object that so harsh an ending does not suit a comedy, and he answers them in the Epistle printed before the play."
- weight: should

### week5.questions.2.auden.paras.1
- where: an essay on the Elizabethan stage, printed in *The
- problem: The first sentence runs to fifty-two words: two glosses on the essay before the subject arrives, then the claim, then a relative clause with a parenthesis inside it. It reads as three sentences joined in revision.
- fix: "[essay title kept], in *The Dyer's Hand* (1962), is an essay about the Elizabethan stage. Auden says in it that Jonson's debt is less to Latin comedy than to the morality play of the Middle Ages. In a morality the vices and the virtues fight over a figure who stands for all mankind. The best-known example is Everyman." Leave the second sentence of the entry as it is.
- weight: should

### week5.questions.2.auden.paras.4
- where: This site suggests that you compare the last scene
- problem: The site announces that it is making a suggestion, where the handbook's own imperative would do the work. An instruction is not a reading of Auden and needs no owner's label; week 6 gives the same sort of instruction without one.
- fix: cut the first five words of the second sentence and begin it at "Compare".
- weight: should

### week5.questions.3.auden.paras.1
- where: In two essays Auden speaks of the wager, and
- problem: A one-sentence paragraph that only announces the two that follow, in a mirrored "in two, in both" frame. The next two paragraphs also open with "In", so the block starts the same way three times.
- fix: cut. In the paragraph that follows, write "Auden" for the first "he".
- weight: should

### week5.questions.3.auden.paras.2
- where: He allows each a use.
- problem: A five-word sentence whose only job is to announce the two that follow, in a clipped tone the rest of the paragraph does not have.
- fix: cut the sentence, and begin the next one "The wager, he grants, does remind people that", with the rest unchanged. The sentence on the leap is then covered by the one before it.
- weight: should

### week5.questions.3.auden.paras.3
- where: He is thankful for that ignorance, he says, because
- problem: Two sentences in a row carry "he says", after "he writes" and "he finds" in the two before. The last one is not needed: a sentence that reports what Auden is thankful for is already his.
- fix: cut the second "he says" and leave the sentence otherwise as it is.
- weight: should

### week5.making.1.brief.1
- where: He offered the students projects to suit what they
- problem: The last three words of the sentence are vague. They could mean the students' occupations, their writing, or their work in the class, and the reader learns which only from the sentence after.
- fix: "He offered different projects to students according to what they wrote."
- weight: could

### week5.making.1.rules.4
- where: You can then finish, and the site shows how
- problem: The second half of the sentence repeats what the paragraph just above the rules has already told the student, in nearly the same words. The table that follows is headed with the same words a third time.
- fix: keep the first sentence. For the other two write "Fill at least sixteen of the twenty-two, and then you can finish."
- weight: should

### week5.making.1.table.key.intro.1
- where: Several of its parts are like parts in the
- problem: The fifty-nine-word second sentence draws the likenesses in the site's own voice, unmarked, and the next sentence gives Auden's pairings, so the same point is made twice before the table makes it a third time. The site's list also brings in Papageno, whom Auden's pairing here leaves out, and "For comparison" repeats the paragraph that sent the student here.
- fix: cut the second sentence. Begin the first "These are the voices" in place of its first six words. Leave the third sentence as it is. If the editor wants each likeness spelled out, the third column of the table is the place for it.
- weight: should

### week6.intro.3
- where: in conversation that his friend Alan Ansen wrote down,
- problem: Two phrases and a "his" stand in front of the subject, so Auden is "his" before he is named in this block (house rule 5). It is the same front-loaded gloss as the Kallman sentence in week 5.
- fix: keep the first sentence. For the second write: "A few years after the course Auden told his friend Alan Ansen, who kept a record of their talk, that the greatest writers of aphorisms were Pascal, Baudelaire, Nietzsche, Blake and Kafka."
- weight: should

### week6.reading.7.paras.2
- where: While you listen, watch the servant. Decide which of
- problem: The student is told to watch during a recording, the servant has no name, and "the two" has only one member in sight. The second sentence is clipped to a riddle that sets up Auden's point below, which reads as coy.
- fix: "While you listen, follow Leporello, Don Giovanni's servant, and decide which of the two men is the master."
- weight: should

### week6.reading.7.auden.paras.2
- where: and that the statue perhaps ought to have dragged
- problem: "Perhaps" after "begins to suspect" hedges a statement that is already hedged, and house rule 3 bars the word. Earlier in the entry "only together with his" stops on a possessive with no noun, the same clipped turn as the savage's innocence in week 5.
- fix: cut "perhaps". At the end of the first sentence add the noun, so that it closes "together with his servant".
- weight: should

### week6.questions.1.auden.paras.1
- where: and a millionaire who steals eleven cents is.
- problem: The sentence stops on a bare "is" that has to mean the opposite of the "is of no interest" before it, and it is joined by "and" where the sense is "but". The reader has to go back. Three sentences on, "in which Auden said the other two meet" has the verb of saying wedged into the relative clause, so that it reads as if Auden spoke inside the religious.
- fix: after "Auden said," write "but a millionaire who steals eleven cents is interesting." And: "The third way is the religious, and in it, Auden said, the other two meet."
- weight: must

### week6.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: and of an ethical religion the God of the
- problem: The sentence drops its verb in the second half and pairs a religion with a God, so the two halves do not match. In the last sentence "calls it the call" jingles, and the colon sets the quotation up as the closing line.
- fix: "He takes the Greek gods as his example of an aesthetic religion and the God of the Greek philosophers as his example of an ethical one." For the last sentence: "It is the call of duty, which he puts in these words: [quotation kept]"
- weight: should

### week6.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: Auden’s instance is the command to Abraham to leave
- problem: The paragraph says twice that Abraham is Auden's example, once in its opening sentence and again in its fourth, in two different nouns. "Auden's instance" is also one of ten possessive labels of this kind in the two weeks (see Patterns).
- fix: for the opening sentence write "Auden writes of such a call again in [essay title kept], an essay in *The Dyer's Hand* (1962)." For the fourth write "His example is God's command to Abraham to leave his own country."
- weight: should

### week6.questions.2.auden.paras.1
- where: Auden wrote about Kierkegaard more than once.
- problem: The first sentence clears the throat and the second ends by announcing doubts that the next paragraph then states. It is the same two-line signpost that opens the block on the wager in week 5.
- fix: "In 1968 Auden reviewed the first volume of Kierkegaard's *Journals and Papers*." Run it on into the paragraph that follows, which already begins with the review.
- weight: should

### week6.questions.2.auden.paras.2
- where: to be alive, whatever one suffers, is a blessing
- problem: "One" is Auden's pronoun carried into the paraphrase (house rule 7). The "He" that opens the next sentence comes straight after "Auden says" and can be taken for Auden. The last sentence hangs two relative clauses on Bonhoeffer, one inside the other.
- fix: write "whatever a person suffers". Begin the next sentence "Kierkegaard heard one theme". End the last sentence at "1945" and go on: "If God grants people some great earthly happiness, Bonhoeffer wrote, [quotation kept]"
- weight: should

### week6.questions.2.auden.paras.3
- where: and counted it a fault in himself and no
- problem: Three faults in one entry. "As much" and two uses of "it" point back across the paragraph break at a quotation; "and no virtue" is mock-antique; and the last sentence nests four "that" clauses, ends with a "he" that could be Auden or Kierkegaard, and keeps the pronoun "one" (house rule 7).
- fix: for "said as much" write "said this". For the second sentence: "What amazes Auden in the portrait is the evidence that Kierkegaard knew he could not, and counted that a fault rather than a virtue." In the last sentence stop after "neighbors" and go on: "Kierkegaard, he adds, says that this is what a person ought to be, and admits that the knight is beyond his understanding."
- weight: should

### week6.questions.3.auden.paras.1
- where: Reading Racine silently he compares to reading the score
- problem: The object is put first in an inversion nobody writes by hand. "One" and the generic "his head" carry a pronoun of Auden's and a "he" for a reader in general into the paraphrase (house rules 7 and 12). Three sentences hold four verbs of saying.
- fix: second sentence: "Shakespeare, he says, can move readers who do not even hear the lines in their heads." Third: "He compares reading Racine silently to reading the score of an opera that the reader can hardly play, and holds that nobody who has not heard a great performance of *Phèdre* can form a fair idea of it."
- weight: should

### week6.questions.3.auden.paras.2
- where: He contrasts Racine’s play with the Greek play on
- problem: The opening sentence only announces the comparison that the next sentence makes (house rule 8). Further on, "one of his characters" comes straight after "Auden" and reads as Auden's characters.
- fix: cut the first sentence and begin "In the *Hippolytus* of Euripides, the Greek play on the same story, Auden recognizes a world akin to Shakespeare's," with the rest unchanged. Further on write "one of Racine's characters sneezing".
- weight: should

### week6.questions.3.auden.paras.3
- where: A dramatist in such a society, he says, has
- problem: ", he says," is wedged into three sentences of this paragraph at the same point, and the first of the three only announces the two "If" sentences. The opening sentence introduces the book again two paragraphs after its first introduction, and the phrase set after the title makes the book, not the essay, sound as if it were on the Elizabethan stage.
- fix: open "In [essay title kept], an essay in the same book about the Elizabethan stage, Auden doubts whether". For the announcing sentence and the two after it: "A dramatist in such a society, he says, can make the character who suffers noble and innocent, and then the effect is pathos and not tragedy. Or the sinners can bring the suffering on themselves, and then they cannot be noble, since it is sinning that makes a person ignoble." For the last sentence: "Auden adds that the story of Oedipus would still be tragic if a journalist told it, but that Macbeth's would be a police-court case and Phaedra's [quotation kept]"
- weight: should

### week6.questions.4.auden.paras.2

- fix: "Auden concludes, [quotation kept]"

### week6.questions.4.auden.paras.3

- fix: first sentence: "Auden was then on Blake's side against dualism of every sort." End the second sentence at "teaches" and go on: "The Devil's first move, the note says, is to divide the whole from its parts, or one part from the rest." (Editor: check this against the note. ### week6.questions.4.auden.paras.4

### week6.questions.5.auden.paras.3

End the paragraph with the pointer, beginning it "For a comparison of this site's own," and leaving the rest as it is. ### week6.questions.5.auden.paras.4

- fix: in the opening sentence write "compares". ## Patterns

Counts are for the whole of part F, which has 34 paragraphs that report Auden.

1. The verb of saying wedged between commas in mid-sentence (", he says," and its kin): 22 times in the 34 Auden paragraphs, 14 of them the bare ", he says,". It turns into a tic where it comes twice or three times in one paragraph: week6.questions.3.auden.paras.3 (three), week5.questions.3.auden.paras.3 (two sentences running). Rule 6 is met by one such verb in a sentence or the sentence before; where the sentence already says what Auden doubts, finds or is thankful for, a second one is surplus.

2. Paragraphs that open with "In": 16, which is 14 of the 34 Auden paragraphs and the first paragraph of both exercises. Most are "In [title], an essay in [book], Auden [verb]". See week5.questions.3.auden.paras.1 to paras.3 (three in a row) and week6.questions.3.auden.paras.1 and paras.3. Start some with the subject or with the claim.

3. Possessive labels in place of a verb ("Auden's conclusion", "word", "verdict", "phrase", "instance", "example", "His example"): 10. Three stand in one paragraph at week6.questions.1.auden.paras.3; see also week6.questions.4.auden.paras.2. They read as a way of dodging pattern 1 and are no lighter.

4. Sentences that announce what the next sentence or paragraph says: 9. Seven are flagged above, for instance week5.questions.3.auden.paras.1 and week6.questions.2.auden.paras.1. Two milder ones are not flagged because they count out what follows: week5.questions.2.auden.paras.2 (first sentence) and week6.questions.1.auden.paras.1 (second sentence).

5. *The Dyer's Hand* introduced afresh with its date: 9 times, and in two styles. Week 5 writes the year into the sentence; week 6 puts it in parentheses. See week5.questions.1.auden.paras.3, which repeats the wording of week5.reading.4.auden.paras.1, and week6.questions.3.auden.paras.3, which introduces the book a second time inside one block. Choose one style and introduce the book once in each block.

6. A paragraph arranged so that a quotation is its last line: 7 of the 14 paragraphs that quote. See week5.questions.1.auden.paras.2, and week6.questions.1.auden.paras.2 and paras.3, which do it twice running. Two of the seven add a label and a colon to stage it.

7. Glosses stacked in front of the subject, so that the sentence starts two or three times: 6. See week5.reading.4.auden.paras.3 and week6.intro.3; also week5.questions.2.auden.paras.1, week6.reading.7.auden.paras.1, week6.questions.2.auden.paras.4 and week6.questions.5.auden.paras.4. These look like the first-mention introductions of rule 11 dropped in wherever the name fell.

8. A possessive with its noun left out, which reads as clipped or coy: 4. See week5.questions.1.auden.paras.4 and week6.reading.7.auden.paras.2; the other two are the label "this site's" at week5.making.1.source.1 and week6.questions.5.auden.paras.3.

9. Pronouns the house rules bar in paraphrase: "one" 3 times (week6.questions.2.auden.paras.2 and paras.3, week6.questions.3.auden.paras.1) and a generic "his" once (week6.questions.3.auden.paras.1).

10. The same thing told to the student three times in the week 5 exercise: what is shown at the end (week5.making.1.brief.3, week5.making.1.rules.4 and the heading of the table), and the likenesses between the two casts (twice in week5.making.1.table.key.intro.1 and again in the table).

11. Barred hedges: 2. See week6.reading.7.auden.paras.2 and week6.questions.4.auden.paras.4.

12. A change of tense inside one paragraph about one text: 2. See week6.questions.4.auden.paras.4 and week6.questions.5.auden.paras.4.

Not found in this part: em-dashes, exclamation marks, rhetorical questions in the site's own prose, a closing formula repeated from block to block, or hype. "This site" appears four times; two are flagged and two are doing necessary work.
