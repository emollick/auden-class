# V2, faithfulness to the sources (weeks 4 and 5)

Second, independent check of every statement in V2.md about what Auden or Donald Pearce wrote, said or did. Evidence is given as scan file and line numbers (for the Pearce captures: file, line and character offsets), with the source described in my words. No source text is reproduced here.

Short names used below. "gap2" is `research/A_pearce_gap2.txt`, the auxiliary capture that holds Pearce pp. 138 to 145, which `auden/pearce.txt` lacks. Essay titles are shortened or left to the label (the Balaam essay, the Greeks essay), so that nothing the text has inside quotation marks is copied.

Result: 12 findings (1 must, 5 should, 6 could). Several hold two faults, because the form allows one finding to an entry. All 10 quotations match their sources. Every page citation is right as far as it goes; two entries lean on a page or a source they do not list.

## Findings, in the order of the text

### week4.questions.1.gloss.1

- problem: Pearce records four tasks set at the end of that lecture, where the gloss has two (p. 143; gap2 line 1, chars 10444–10839): likenesses of saint and villain, likenesses of Iago and Falstaff, why Shakespeare was so taken with this cast of mind, and how he regards it in *The Tempest*.

### week4.questions.2.auden.paras.2
- where: the situation of the Greek hero you read about
- problem: Two things here are not in the note cited (dh.txt 21423–21436, pp. 451–452). The parenthesis rests on the Greeks essay of 1948 (*Forewords and Afterwords*, p. 20; fa.txt 986–991), which the entry does not list, and its "him" assumes a male hero though the student may have read *Antigone*; and the sentence after it, on what Hamlet's situation fails to do, is the site's own bridge given to Auden, where the note goes straight from Hamlet's wish to his being unable to act.
- fix: Parenthesis: (In the essay of 1948 reported under week 2, Auden says that in Greek tragedy a situation can by itself make the hero guilty.) Then one sentence in place of the two that follow it: Hamlet, who wants to be what that hero is, cannot act, Auden says, because he can only [quotation kept], as a player does, trying one possibility after another. Add a second source line to the entry: the same essay and book as week4.questions.3.auden.sources.2, with p. 20.
- weight: should

### week5.reading.4.auden.paras.2
- where: He imagines Caliban answering Prospero’s offer of an education
- problem: In the essay the imagined answer is a comparison for Papageno's refusal of the trials, which is as if Caliban had answered so (dh.txt 6474–6481, p. 134); placed after the verdict on the opera, it reads as a separate fancy and the likeness is lost. The essay's proviso, that the two get on only while each leaves the other alone (dh.txt 6483–6496, pp. 134–135), is dropped as well.
- fix: Put the verdict first, as the essay does. In the first sentence of the paragraph write "Auden says" for "he says," and after "quarrel" go on: so long as each leaves the other alone, and he calls that too hopeful. Cut the short sentence that now carries the verdict. In place of the sentence that begins "He imagines," write: That refusal, he says, is as if Caliban had thanked Prospero for the offer of an education and turned it down, on the ground that clothes and speech were not for him and that the jungle suited him better.
- weight: could

### week5.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: the guilty are sorry mostly because they have been caught
- problem: Two details of the essay are changed in this sentence: it excepts Alonso, who alone seems truly sorry, and it calls Prospero's pardon contemptuous (dh.txt 6175–6178 and 6188–6191, pp. 128–129). The last sentence of the paragraph also keeps nine of the essay's words in a row, from "it never occurs," outside quotation marks (dh.txt 6208, p. 129).
- fix: For this sentence: According to that essay, Alonso alone among the guilty seems truly sorry. The rest repent as people do who have been punished and frightened, and Prospero pardons his enemies with contempt, knowing that he has them at his mercy. For the last sentence: Prospero is cold, and the thought that he might himself have done wrong and need pardon never crosses his mind.
- weight: should

### week5.questions.1.auden.paras.4
- where: Prospero taught him, and then made a slave of
- problem: Two things the essay says against Caliban are left out: it grants that he is brutal and corrupt (dh.txt 6216), and it has the teaching stop at his attempt to rape Miranda, after which Prospero, who will not send him away, makes him a slave (dh.txt 6224–6239, pp. 129–130). Without them the enslaving has no cause, and a student replying to Auden may charge him with overlooking what he in fact says.
- fix: First sentence: The essay grants that Caliban is brutal and corrupt, and still gives him the better of the argument. In place of the sentence that begins "Prospero taught him": Prospero tried to educate him, gave it up when Caliban tried to rape Miranda, and then, instead of letting him go back to the forest, kept him as a slave and ruled him by fear.
- weight: must

- weight: could
