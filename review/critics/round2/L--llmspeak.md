# Part L (week 15), lens: writing that reads as machine-made

### week15.intro.1
- where: what is left of *The Castle* is about
- problem: The week opens by joining two facts that have no link (it is the last week; 180 pages remain) with "and". The same weld recurs in six more entries and is the clearest track of the revision (see Patterns).
- fix: In this last week of reading you finish *The Castle*, of which about 180 pages remain.
- weight: could

### week15.intro.2
- where: given with the reading, and the play is about
- problem: Where to borrow the play and how long it is are welded with "and" though neither bears on the other. The next sentence is welded the same way, so the paragraph ends on two sentences of one shape.
- fix: Get a copy at the start of the week; the reading gives a link for borrowing one. The play runs to about 75 pages. It was two years old when Auden assigned it, which makes it the newest of the required books.
- weight: should

### week15.reading.3.paras.3
- where: It is the last of the four operas
- problem: "It" opens the paragraph with its noun two paragraphs back, and the paragraph before has already spoken of "the end of the story" without saying what story. All four sentences are then built "clause, and clause", and the last one joins the first performance to the language the opera is sung in.
- fix: Move the paragraph up to stand second, before the listening task, and write: *Götterdämmerung* closes the cycle of four operas called *The Ring of the Nibelung* and is the only part of the cycle on the list. Wagner wrote the words himself, in German, and wrote those of this opera first. The other three grew backward from a single opera about Siegfried's death that he planned in 1848. Bayreuth staged all four together for the first time in August 1876.
- weight: should

### week15.reading.3.auden.paras.1
- where: gave a lecture at Columbia University called
- problem: The participle after "Columbia University" attaches to the university when it belongs to the lecture, and the sentence names the source without reporting anything. In the next sentence "put broadly" is a hedge; if the qualification is Auden's it should be given as his, and if it is the site's it should go.
- fix: Make the two sentences one: In [title kept], a lecture given at Columbia University in September 1940 and printed the next year, Auden says that the subject of the *Ring* is an old order coming to its end.
- weight: could

### week15.reading.3.auden.paras.4
- where: say the same and give it a name
- problem: A teaser. It promises a name and leaves the student to work out which of the next phrases is the name, and "the same" and "it" both point back across the paragraph break to nothing in particular.
- fix: The notes to Auden's long poem *New Year Letter*, which came out in the spring of 1941, make the same point about Wagner's characters. [Then "One note puts Wagner first" and the rest unchanged. The paragraph is the longest in the part; a break before "Another note distinguishes" would help.]
- weight: should

### week15.reading.3.auden.paras.5
- where: Auden added a point about the music
- problem: The first sentence only announces the second, which then states Wagner's theory with nobody saying it. The last sentence takes "repeats with approval a remark of", an appositive, a relative clause and a colon to say that Auden agrees with Shaw, and its "he" comes straight after a sentence about what Wagner did.
- fix: Cut the first sentence and begin with the theory. Then: From *Die Meistersinger* (1868) on, Auden says in a review of 1969, Wagner dropped the theory altogether [rest unchanged]. Auden agrees with Bernard Shaw, the playwright and former music critic, that *Götterdämmerung*, with its poisoned drinks, conspirators' ensemble and massed chorus, is close kin to Verdi's opera *Un Ballo in Maschera* (1859). [If the account of the 1851 theory is also Auden's, say so in that sentence.]
- weight: should

### week15.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: Faust and Peer Gynt are exceptional too

After the quotation, begin "K. is exceptional" in place of "He is exceptional". If the review does not use the word of the fairy-tale hero, cut "too" and leave that sentence alone. Rule 11 also wants "Goethe's Faust and Ibsen's Peer Gynt" at this first mention.

### week15.questions.1.auden.paras.6
- where: and in that alone, Auden says, lies his guilt
- problem: Two verbs of saying in adjacent sentences, where the first already covers the second, and the inverted "in that alone ... lies" is mock-antique in the site's voice. The paragraph then closes on a cleft and an intensifier ("What K. is after is ... exactly what").
- fix: [First sentence unchanged.] K. starts as an [quoted word kept], with no gifts or deeds to show for it, and that alone is his guilt. [Quotation kept.] K. wants to be somebody in the way other people are, and that is what he is refused.

## Patterns

1. Welded sentences: 12 sentences in 7 entries. Two statements with little or no link are joined by ", and", most often where two short sentences were made into one. Four stand in a row in week15.reading.3.paras.3; see also week15.intro.2 and week15.questions.2.auden.paras.2. For scale, ", and" occurs in 58 of the part's 193 sentences. Where the halves are unrelated, give each its own sentence or make one depend on the other. In week15.intro.3, which has no finding above, "which takes" would do for ", and it takes".

2. Source-first openers: 10 of the 26 Auden paragraphs. The paragraph opens with a sentence that names a lecture, review, essay or topic and reports nothing, and the report starts in the second sentence, three times with "According to the ...". See week15.questions.1.auden.paras.4 and week15.questions.4.auden.paras.2. The fixes above merge four. Two more merges would break the run: open week15.questions.1.auden.paras.5 with "[title kept], an essay in Auden's book *The Dyer's Hand* (1962), puts", and week15.questions.3.auden.paras.3 with "Reviewing Eliot's collected poems and plays in 1953, Auden grants".

3. Pointers cut loose from their nouns: 9 in 7 entries ("It", "Its" twice, "He", "he" twice, "his", "the same", "it"). See week15.questions.2.auden.paras.3 and week15.reading.3.paras.3. Most follow a quotation, a parenthesis or a moved paragraph, which is where a revision leaves them.

4. Statements of what is not on record: 9 in 9 entries, three of them with "has been found". Three of the four question blocks open on one; see week15.questions.3.auden.paras.1 and week15.questions.4.auden.paras.1. Each is honest and the house voice asks for it, so this is for the editor to weigh: at the head of block after block the site sounds as if it were apologizing. One pair is plain repetition: week15.making.1.brief.2 and week15.making.1.table.after.1 give the same reason for the missing key. Shorten the second to "No key follows, because Pearce recorded none."

5. The same thing said twice: 4, and 2 more that can stand. The four are week15.questions.2.gloss.1 against week15.questions.2.auden.paras.1; inside week15.questions.1.auden.paras.7; week15.questions.3.auden.paras.5 against paras.3 of that block; and the pair on the key in item 4. The two that do some work are the listening task quoted back in week15.reading.3.auden.paras.6 and the running time given in both week15.intro.3 and week15.reading.3.paras.1.

6. Names with no introduction at first mention in the week (rule 11): 4. Klamm in week15.words.setting, Oedipus in week15.reading.3.auden.paras.3, Faust and Peer Gynt in week15.questions.1.auden.paras.2, *Pilgrim's Progress* in week15.questions.2.auden.paras.1.

7. Attribution stacked: 3 paragraphs. The sayer is named in sentence after sentence where one naming would cover two. See week15.questions.1.auden.paras.6 and week15.questions.1.auden.paras.7 (Auden five times in six sentences). In week15.reading.3.auden.paras.4 a note is the subject of six verbs.

8. Lists of three standing close together: 3. week15.reading.3.auden.paras.5 has one in each of two consecutive sentences, and week15.reading.3.auden.paras.4 has the site's own "desire, a curse or the past". The matter may dictate the first two (the rules of the theory, the features of the opera). Flag only: check that each item is in Auden's review, and drop one example from the gloss if two will do.

9. "exactly" as an intensifier: 2. week15.questions.1.auden.paras.6 and week15.questions.3.auden.paras.3, where "suggest exactly that" can be "suggest it".

Not found in this part: em-dashes, exclamation marks, hype words, or "not X but Y" turned for effect. The diction is plain. The faults are in how sentences were joined and in what the pronouns point to.
