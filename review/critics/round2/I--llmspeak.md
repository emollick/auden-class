# Part I (weeks 11 and 12), lens: writing that reads as machine-made

Findings are in the order of the text. Quotations are not copied; where a fix keeps one it is shown as [quotation kept].

### week11.intro.2
- where: you will find it divided into three parts
- problem: The clause tells the student what will be found and hides who did the dividing (this site, since Adams did not write his book in three parts). "Three" is counted twice in one line, and the book has no word of introduction at its first mention in the week (house rule 11).
- fix: *The Education of Henry Adams*, the autobiography of the American historian, comes just before *Moby-Dick* on the reading list. Like *Faust*, it is marked [quotation kept] on the published copy of the list. For a student who chooses to read it, this site has divided it into three parts, one for each week of *Moby-Dick*.
- weight: should

### week11.questions.1.auden.paras.1
- where: three lectures that Auden gave in 1949
- problem: Auden's name is tucked into the aside and the main clause is left with "he", so the pronoun carries the sentence and the name does not. It reads as a phrase swapped in to satisfy the naming rule. The same shape opens week11.questions.3.auden.paras.3 and week12.reading.3.auden.paras.3.
- fix: In *The Enchafèd Flood*, his three lectures of 1949 on the sea in Romantic writing, Auden reads all that happens before the Pequod sails as a set of tests.
- weight: should

### week11.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: which Auden says his readers share
- problem: "his" has three possible owners here (Ishmael, Auden, Melville), and the student cannot tell whose readers are meant.
- fix: Ishmael starts with two fixed notions, which Auden says the reader of the book shares: [the two notions as they stand]. If Auden means Melville's first readers, write "which Auden says Melville's readers shared".
- weight: should

### week11.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: the act by which Ishmael accepts him
- problem: "him" comes straight after "for Auden" and can be read as Auden. "For Auden" is also a weak marker of whose reading this is; a plain verb of saying does the job and lets the sentence run forward.
- fix: Auden says that when Ishmael joins in the worship of the idol he accepts Queequeg, and that the acceptance proves Ishmael fit for the voyage.
- weight: should

### week11.questions.1.auden.paras.4
- where: Auden’s explanation of why the tests come first is
- problem: The attribution has been turned into a nine-word noun phrase that the sentence has to carry before it says anything. This is the mark of a verb of saying added in revision.
- fix: The tests come first, Auden says, because the voyage is an exploration for Ishmael and for any reader who travels with him.
- weight: should

### week11.questions.2.auden.paras.1
- where: has been called a magnificent irrelevance
- problem: Two faults. The two-word phrase reads as somebody's own wording taken over without marks, while the other half of the same sentence is quoted (house rule 14 wants the report in the site's words). And both sentences carry an attribution when the first covers the second, while "put it where he did" points at a place the paragraph never names.
- fix: Auden says in *The Enchafèd Flood*, [the gloss as it stands], that Father Mapple's sermon has been called a fine digression but that it is [quotation kept]. Melville put it early, before the Pequod sails, so that readers would have a standard for judging what Ahab and the rest say and do afterward. (If the two words are Auden's and the editor wants them, put them in quotation marks.)
- weight: should

### week11.questions.2.auden.paras.2
- where: at all, which was the only gift he had
- problem: "which" has no noun to hold: the gift is the hearing, and the nearest noun is "the Word". After it come two sentences in a row that start with "He", the first only four words long and set there for the beat.
- fix: Inside the whale he loses the one gift he had, the power to hear the Word. Then join the last two sentences: begin "Instead of despairing he repents and trusts" and leave the rest as it stands.
- weight: should

### week11.questions.2.auden.paras.3
- where: His answer is a speech you read
- problem: A short sentence that only announces the next one (house rule 8). The play and its author are not named anywhere in the week (rule 11), and "He" changes owner from Auden to the Button-moulder two sentences on.
- fix: Auden grants that the two statements look like a contradiction, and he answers with a speech from Ibsen's *Peer Gynt*, which you read in week 7. There the Button-moulder tells Peer that to be oneself is to slay oneself. Since the Button-moulder does not expect Peer to follow, he puts it another way: [the restatement as it stands].
- weight: should

### week11.questions.2.auden.paras.4
- where: and the application is this site’s
- problem: "the application" has no owner. Nothing has been applied when the phrase arrives, and the application itself comes only in the sentence after, so the disclaimer reads as a formula dropped in ahead of the thing it disclaims. The opening words, about a distinction Auden "goes on to draw", are a signpost and can go.
- fix: Open with: Auden then distinguishes the ego from the self, which together make a person. Close with: Auden does not apply the distinction to Father Mapple's two statements. This site suggests that you do, by asking of each one whether it speaks of the self that is given or of the ego that decides.
- weight: should

### week11.questions.3.auden.paras.1
- where: calling Greek tragedy the tragedy of necessity, at which
- problem: The clause that follows is an indirect thought with two loose "it"s; it is hard to read and it shadows the wording of the quotation in the next sentence. The paragraph then ends on a mirrored pair ("does not mean" and "He means"), and Shakespeare arrives with no play named.
- fix: He begins by calling Greek tragedy the tragedy of necessity, which leaves the spectator sorry that things had to go as they did. Christian tragedy is the tragedy of possibility: [quotation kept]. By Christian he means that the writer's idea of what a person is derives from the creed, whether or not the writer held it. (If Shakespeare stays, name the play that Auden sets beside *Moby-Dick*.)
- weight: should

### week11.questions.3.auden.paras.2
- where: comes to be punished: he does it to himself
- problem: A colon stages a reveal and a five-word clause is set at the end of the paragraph for the beat (house rules 3 and 8). "Does it" has only a passive verb to point back to.
- fix: He chooses wrongly and keeps to his choice, so the punishment that follows is one he inflicts on himself.
- weight: must

### week11.questions.3.auden.paras.3
- where: Auden notes that he does not say that he
- problem: "Say" or "says" comes five times in six sentences and belongs to two speakers, Auden and Ahab, and "he" changes owner with it. The sentence located nests three clauses of saying, and the "He" that opens the last sentence can be taken for Auden. "Perhaps" doubles the hedge already made by "He guesses" (rule 3).
- fix: In *The Enchafèd Flood*, his three lectures of 1949 on the sea in Romantic writing, Auden asks what the loss of the leg was telling Ahab to become. The question, he says, can be answered only in the negative: [quotation kept]. His guess is that the leg told Ahab to give up being set apart from other people by what he could do, and become a happy husband and father, or a monk. Ahab does neither. Auden notes that Ahab never admits he has been justly punished and never declares, as Job does, that he will trust the one who slays him. Ahab tells the whale that it is the guilty one and shall be punished.
- weight: must

### week11.questions.3.auden.paras.4
- where: The price, in Auden’s account, is paid by
- problem: "The price" opens the paragraph without a word of what is being paid for, and it is an abstract, portentous opener. All four sentences then carry their own attribution, which is more than rule 6 asks, and "the same" in the third has to reach back over a clause for its owner.
- fix: Open with: Auden says that Ahab's own nature pays for the defiance. In the second sentence cut "Auden observes that" and the "that" which follows "and", and write "by Ahab's own admission". In the third write "willed these things for him" in place of "willed the same for him". Leave the last sentence as it stands.
- weight: should

### week11.questions.3.auden.sources.1
- where: the second volume of Auden’s collected prose
- problem: The gloss explains "Volume II" as "the second volume" and says "prose" twice, so it tells the student nothing the title has not already said. The same words stand again in week11.making.1.source.1.
- fix: It is reprinted in Auden's collected *Prose, Volume II*, pp. 258–261. In week11.making.1.source.1: ... is printed in Auden's collected *Prose, Volume II*, pp. 467–470. If the book needs more of an introduction, the editor, press and year would be the useful one, as the entries for *New Year Letter* and for Miller's memoir already give them.
- weight: should

### week11.making.1.rules.2
- where: otherwise the word must be the book’s own
- problem: A quaint possessive where the ordinary instruction would do. "The book" also has no clear owner, since the student is working in one of three versions of the canto.
- fix: Capital letters and accents do not count in a correction, but otherwise the word must match the one in your version of the canto.
- weight: could

### week11.making.1.rules.4
- where: Every sound word that you alter is counted
- problem: "Sound word" stops the reader. "Sound" is read first as a noun, and the meaning wanted (a word that was not changed) has to be worked out from the rest of the sentence. A quaint word where a plain one is needed, in an instruction that carries a penalty.
- fix: Begin "Every unchanged word that you alter" and leave the rest of the sentence as it stands.
- weight: should

### week11.making.1.emend.versions.temple.where
- where: Inferno V
- problem: The title of a long poem is not italic here or in the two entries like it (versions.longfellow.where, versions.italian.where), though the cite lines beside them have it italic (house rule 9).
- fix: *Inferno* V
- weight: could

### week11.making.1.emend.after.2
- where: so only memory will catch them
- problem: The clause repeats, almost word for word, a sentence in the entry just before it, and "of the first kind" has already said as much. The second sentence then closes the entry on a balanced aphorism about drift and words, and it tells the student what the result means just before the reflection question asks the student to work that out.
- fix: Keep the first sentence as far as "kind", end it there, and cut the rest of the entry. If a second sentence is wanted, make it an instruction: Go back to each line where you missed a change and learn it again.
- weight: should

### week12.reading.3.paras.2
- where: notice how much Senta knows about the Dutchman

### week12.reading.3.auden.paras.2

### week12.reading.3.auden.paras.3
- where: and that suffering was all he could express

- fix: In the notes to *New Year Letter*, his long poem of 1941, Auden says that Wagner, in expressing the sufferings of failure, is [quotation kept]. Cut the clause that follows the quotation and leave the rest of the paragraph as it stands.

### week12.reading.3.auden.paras.4

### week12.questions.1.auden.paras.1
- where: and so, Auden says, does everyone in one way
- problem: The paragraph has four attributions in six sentences, and this one comes directly after "he says" in the sentence before, so rule 6 does not need it. In the second sentence "whole" is used twice in one clause in two senses, and "a case of anybody's life" is an odd idiom for a part that stands for the whole.
- fix: Third sentence: Whalemen live by killing, and so does everyone in one way or another. In the second sentence cut "whole" before "book", and for "makes it a case of anybody’s life" write "makes it stand for anybody's life".

### week12.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: The method is deliberate, he says, the one
- problem: The grammar gave way when the verb of saying went in. An adjective is followed, across "he says", by a noun phrase with nothing to attach to, and a third statement is hung on the end with "and".
- fix: Auden says that the method is deliberate, that it is the one the Church Fathers used when they interpreted Scripture, and that the reader grasps each correspondence by reasoning.
- weight: should

### week12.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: The third kind is the whale, and here alone
- problem: A slip of category. The first two kinds were named as kinds of meaning; the third is named by its single instance. "Here alone" is a literary inversion where a plain statement would serve.
- fix: The third kind is symbol in the true sense, and according to Auden the book has only one, the whale. (Leave the next two sentences as they stand.)
- weight: should

### week12.questions.1.auden.paras.4

### week12.questions.2.auden.paras.2

## Patterns

Most frequent first. Each count is a number of entries.

1. A pronoun or pointer with no clear owner (10). "His", "him", "he", "it", "which", "the same" and "the application" each reach for a noun that is too far off or is one of several. Most of these are tracks of the revision: a name was moved or a verb of saying added, and the pronoun was left behind. See week11.questions.1.auden.paras.3 and week11.questions.2.auden.paras.4.

2. Attribution stacked, or a sentence bent to hold it (8 of the 24 paragraphs that report Auden). Either every sentence has its own verb of saying, or the verb was pushed in and the grammar suffered. The verbs are also rotated (says, notes, observes, adds, grants, guesses, "in Auden's view", "in Auden's account", "for Auden"), and the rotation is itself a tell. One plain "Auden says" for every two sentences meets rule 6. See week11.questions.3.auden.paras.3 and week12.questions.1.auden.paras.1; also week11.questions.3.auden.paras.4 and week12.questions.2.auden.paras.4.

3. Introductions given to the wrong things (6 repeated, 6 missing). *The Enchafèd Flood* is glossed six times in two weeks, in three wordings (week11.questions.1.auden.paras.1, week12.questions.2.auden.paras.1), and the collected prose twice in the same words. Six names get no introduction at all: Adams, Ibsen's play, Shakespeare's play, Senta, Nietzsche, Blake (week11.intro.2, week12.reading.3.paras.2). Settle one wording for the lectures and use it once in each block that is shown by itself.

4. The same thing said twice (6). A clause or sentence restates its neighbor. See week12.reading.3.auden.paras.3 and week11.making.1.emend.after.2; also the two source lines on the collected prose.

5. Quaint, clipped or coy wording where the ordinary phrase is needed (4). See week11.making.1.rules.4 and week12.questions.2.auden.paras.2.

6. Small inconsistencies of form, not written up above (4). The ship is "her" in week11.intro.1 and "its" in week12.intro.1. The whale has capitals in week12.questions.2.text and none in week11.test.items.14.q. The spelling is "harpooneer" in week12.test.items.10.q and "harpooners" in week12.reading.3.auden.paras.2. Some source lines give publisher and year (week12.questions.1.auden.sources.2) and some give neither (week11.questions.1.auden.sources.1).

7. Auden named in the aside and "he" left as the subject (3). See week11.questions.1.auden.paras.1 and week12.reading.3.auden.paras.3.

8. An ending that poses (2) and a short sentence that only announces (2). See week11.questions.3.auden.paras.2 and week11.making.1.emend.after.2 for the first, week11.questions.2.auden.paras.3 and week11.questions.2.auden.paras.4 for the second.

Not found in this part: em-dashes, exclamation marks, rhetorical questions, hype words, or closing formulas of the "In your reply" kind. The test items and the questions put to the student are clean. Ten of the 24 Auden paragraphs, and both exercise briefs, open on a source or a date followed by "Auden" and a verb. That is the report form doing its work, and only the six-fold gloss in pattern 3 needs attention.
