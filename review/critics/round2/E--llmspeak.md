# Part E, weeks 3 and 4: writing that reads as machine-made

Findings are in the order of the text. Where a fix keeps a quotation, it is shown as [quotation kept]. Titles that the text has inside quotation marks are pointed to, not copied.

### week3.intro.1
- where: This week there is one book, Augustine’s
- problem: Augustine gets no introduction at his first mention in the week (rule 11). A student who has never met him learns only the name.
- fix: Open with: "This week there is one book, the *Confessions* of Augustine, a bishop in Roman North Africa who wrote it about the year 400. It is in thirteen parts called books." Leave the two sentences that follow as they stand.
- weight: should

### week3.words.where
- where: Thomas Gray: (the whole line)
- problem: The title of the poem stands bare here. In week3.words.cite the same title is in curly double quotes, as rule 9 requires.
- fix: Set the line as the cite has it: Gray's name, a comma, then the title inside curly double quotes.
- weight: should

### week3.words.after.1
- where: wrote at the time that instead of having
- problem: One sentence of 55 words carries Eberhart, the usual exercise, three things Auden did and an aside on the boys' age. The reader has lost the subject by the time the verb "find" arrives.
- fix: "The poet Richard Eberhart, who was on the staff, wrote at the time that Auden did not ask the class to paraphrase a stanza of this poem. Auden took out the key words and set odd or nonsense words where they had been. The boys were too young to know the poem and had to find the right words by feel."
- weight: should

### week3.test.items.2.q
- where: Which language did he hate to learn
- problem: In items 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12 "he" has no name on the screen, and its owner changes from one item to the next: in item 5 "he" is Ambrose, in item 6 he is Augustine. Item 14 gives the name.
- fix: Put the name in each of the six: "Which language did Augustine hate to learn as a boy?", "To which animals did Augustine and his friends throw the stolen pears?", and so on.
- weight: could

### week3.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: knowing the truth was the way out of it
- problem: "it" has two possible owners, evil and the body (rule 10).
- fix: "They put evil in the body, and held that to know the truth was to be free of evil." If the body is meant, end the sentence "was to escape the body."
- weight: should

### week3.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: the starting point is the act that is neither
- problem: The act is described here and never named. When paras.5 speaks of "the gratuitous act," the student has no way to tie that term to this paragraph.
- fix: Name it at this point: "the starting point is the gratuitous act, an act neither reasonable nor pleasant, done only to show that the people who do it are their own masters."
- weight: should

### week3.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: Their reasons and their appetites come second.
- problem: A seven-word sentence set at the end of the paragraph for the beat (rule 8). Its content belongs to the sentence before, which already has the verb of saying.
- fix: Join the two: "People act, Auden says, either [quotation kept] or [quotation kept], and their reasons and appetites come second."
- weight: should

### week3.questions.1.auden.paras.4
- where: took notes at the lecture on Augustine, and they
- problem: This entry has Pearce taking notes at the lecture. Week 4 (week4.questions.1.auden.paras.1) says nobody was allowed to take notes in the room and that Pearce wrote afterward. One of the two misleads the student. "survive in outline" is also vague: it does not say whether the notes are an outline or were printed as one.
- fix: "Donald Pearce, who was a student in the class, wrote down after each lecture what he could remember of it. For the lecture on Augustine his notes are an outline." The editor must confirm from Pearce that "an outline" is right.
- weight: must

### week3.questions.1.auden.paras.5
- where: but he made the connection in a lecture on
- problem: "the connection" has no owner. Nothing has yet been connected with anything, and the reader must wait for the next sentence to learn what is meant.
- fix: Replace the second sentence with: "The review does not mention the pears. Auden spoke of them three years later, in a lecture on *Othello* given in New York in 1947." Leave the first and last sentences as they stand.
- weight: could

### week3.questions.2.auden.paras.1
- where: At that point in the essay he is discussing
- problem: "that point" points at nothing the reader can see. The author of the novel is not given (rule 11).
- fix: Keep the first sentence. Replace the second with: "The passage comes in his account of Phileas Fogg, the hero of Jules Verne's novel *Around the World in Eighty Days* and a man who worships the clock."
- weight: could

### week3.questions.2.auden.paras.4
- where: That reading of the question is this site’s.
- problem: The disclaimer is a short sentence tacked on at the end of the block, after the student has already taken the reading as Auden's. One sentence can mark the reading as the site's from its first words.
- fix: Open the sentence before with "On this site's reading of the question," in place of its first six words, and cut this sentence.
- weight: should

### week3.making.1.brief.2
- where: It is assigned this week because Books X
- problem: The passive hides who assigns. Auden gave this exercise at Swarthmore and not in this course, so its place in week 3 is the site's choice, and the handbook is supposed to say so.
- fix: "This site assigns it in week 3 because Books X and XI of the *Confessions* are about memory and time."
- weight: could

### week3.making.1.rules.4
- where: Tell it as events
- problem: A cramped phrase. "it" is a day's doings, and a day cannot be told "as events." This is plainness overdone (lens 6).
- fix: "Say what happened and do not comment on it."
- weight: could

### week3.making.1.source.1
- where: Both say the events of the day.
- problem: Too compressed to follow. The student has to work out that the point is the day itself as against the day before. The note also stops without saying which version the exercise uses.
- fix: "In both, the class describes the events of the day itself. Humphrey Carpenter's biography of Auden has the day before, and the exercise here follows Carpenter." The editor must confirm that last clause.
- weight: should

### week4.intro.1
- where: wrote down afterward what he could of Auden’s lecture
- problem: "afterward" is cut loose. It depends on the ban on note-taking, which the student is told about only in the block shown after a paper is handed in. Also, "printed and tested" yokes two things: a play is printed, a student is tested. The words on Pearce come again, nearly the same, in week4.questions.1.auden.paras.1.
- fix: "Auden's list names only the second part of *Henry IV*, and that is the only part this site prints or tests you on. Donald Pearce, one of the students, wrote down what he could after each lecture, and his notes show that Auden lectured on the first part too. You do not need the first part for anything assigned this week."
- weight: should

### week4.test.items.1.where
- where: Act 3, Scene 2
- problem: The eight items that show a passage (1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15) give act and scene with no play, in a week that has three plays. "Act 5, Scene 2" stands for a different play in each of items 3, 9 and 15. The other seven items name the play. It reads as two templates.
- fix: Name the play in all fifteen, in the form item 2 already has: the play in italics, then act and scene. Flag only if the app prints the play's name beside the passage.
- weight: should

### week4.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: and that he has learned to turn this lack
- problem: "he" changes owner from one clause to the next. Here it is Iago; the next sentence opens with "He" and means Auden. Pascal has no introduction (rule 11).
- fix: First sentence: "Iago, Auden said, is cold and cannot love other people, and has learned to make a strength of this lack." Open the second: "Auden called him the only brilliant person in *Othello*, who has what the French writer Pascal calls" and go on as now.
- weight: should

### week4.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: and he took this to be significant: Iago is
- problem: A clause that only announces, followed by a colon that stages what comes next (rule 3). The sentence also spends two verbs of saying on one thought.
- fix: "No one else in the play shows any knowledge of the Bible, and Auden took this as a sign that Iago is [quotation kept], a man who wanted, at bottom, to be a saint."
- weight: should

### week4.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: He described each as keeping life at arm’s length
- problem: Four verbs of saying in six sentences, straight after five in the four sentences of paras.2. The verb has become a tic. Rule 6 is met when every second sentence has one.
- fix: Drop two. In the third sentence put "Each keeps" for its first five words and leave the rest. In the last sentence cut ", he said".
- weight: could

### week4.questions.1.auden.paras.4
- where: but in the parable he is a comic figure
- problem: A sentence of about fifty words holds a gloss in apposition, a gloss in parentheses, a "which" clause and two verbs of saying. "a comic figure for charity" can also be misread as a comic figure who serves charity.
- fix: End the sentence after "revels." Then: "In the parable he is a comic symbol of charity in its older sense of love, and Prince Hal stands for worldly justice."
- weight: should

### week4.questions.1.auden.paras.4
- where: who does anything, while the others only behave
- problem: The block ends on a compressed antithesis that the student cannot unpack. Nothing says how doing differs from behaving, so the sentence reads as a closing flourish, though the distinction is Auden's.
- fix: flag only. If the distinction stays, say it in plain words, for example: "and as the one person in *Othello* who acts on a plan of his own, while the rest only respond to what is done to them." The editor must check that gloss against the essay.
- weight: could

### week4.questions.2.auden.paras.2
- where: the situation of the Greek hero you read about
- problem: The parenthesis walks around its subject. Week 2 let the student choose between two plays, and "him" does not fit the one who chose *Antigone*.
- fix: "(In the Greek play you read in week 2, on Auden's account, the hero's situation was enough to make the hero guilty.)"
- weight: should

### week4.questions.2.auden.paras.3
- where: is according to the note the opposite of
- problem: The attribution is wedged between the verb and its complement with no commas, a track of the revision (lens 10). Don Quixote, first met in paras.1 of this block, has no introduction (rule 11).
- fix: "Don Quixote, the note says, believes he is a knight and cannot see himself as playing a part at all, which makes him the opposite of an actor." In paras.1, after his name, add "the hero of Cervantes's novel."
- weight: should

### week4.questions.3.auden.paras.1
- where: the book of essays he published in 1962, Auden
- problem: "he" comes before Auden is named in this block (rule 5), and three appositives are stacked ahead of the subject. The eight-word gloss on the book is the same, word for word, as in week4.questions.2.auden.paras.1.
- fix: "Auden published a book of essays, *The Dyer's Hand*, in 1962. One of them, [the essay's title in its curly quotes], is on the Elizabethan stage, and in it he writes: [quotation kept]" The two sentences that follow can stand.
- weight: should

### week4.questions.3.auden.paras.2
- where: In his essay on *Othello*, in the same
- problem: All three paragraphs of this block open the same way, with "In" and a source set ahead of the subject. The frame is counted under Patterns.
- fix: Open this one with the subject: "Auden's essay on *Othello*, in the same book, says that" and go on as now.
- weight: could

### week4.questions.3.auden.paras.3
- where: and that the suffering a modern audience finds tragic
- problem: Two claims are joined by "and that" although the second explains the first, and a "so that" clause trails after both. The essay named here has no introduction in this week (rule 11).
- fix: "Auden had already written about Othello in 1948, in his essay on the Greeks, [the essay's title in its curly quotes], where he called him a tragic hero in the modern sense. The suffering that a modern audience finds tragic, he wrote, is suffering the hero makes for himself and insists on, and so he dies unreconciled."
- weight: could

### week4.making.1.rules.2
- where: if a library gives you access to it
- problem: The condition hangs on the wrong clause: the dictionary gives the history of a word whether or not the student can reach it. "gives" comes twice in the sentence. The join broke the logic (lens 10).
- fix: "The *Oxford English Dictionary* does, and traces the history of each word as well, but you will need a library that subscribes to it."
- weight: should

### week4.making.1.source.1
- where: is known from Alan Ansen’s journal, as reported in
- problem: Ansen has no introduction (rule 11). The Kirsch edition is cited here as "edited by" and in week3.questions.1.auden.sources.3 as "reconstructed and edited by": two forms for one book.
- fix: "is known from the journal of Auden's friend Alan Ansen, as reported in" and then the citation in the form week 3 uses.
- weight: could

## Patterns

Most frequent first. Nearly all the faults sit in the paragraphs shown after a paper is handed in, 19 paragraphs in five blocks. The test questions, the rules of the two exercises and the key to the sixteen words read as a person wrote them. No em-dash, exclamation mark, hedge, hype word or rhetorical question was found in this part.

1. A verb of saying with Auden or "he" as its subject: 26 in the 19 paragraphs. It becomes a tic in week4.questions.1.auden.paras.2 (five in four sentences) and week4.questions.1.auden.paras.3 (four in six). Rule 6 needs only one in every second sentence.
2. The source made the speaker ("the review argues," "the essay credits," "the note says"): 8. See week3.questions.2.auden.paras.2 and week4.questions.2.auden.paras.3. It was brought in to vary the verb of saying, and it gives one speaker two names inside a paragraph, as in week3.questions.1.auden.paras.2. Pick one name for the speaker in each paragraph.
3. A paragraph or sentence that opens with "In" and a source or a year, ahead of the subject: 7. All three paragraphs of week4.questions.3.auden open this way, and week4.questions.1.auden.paras.4 has it twice.
4. A person or book with no few-word introduction (rule 11): 6. Augustine in week3.intro.1 and Pascal in week4.questions.1.auden.paras.2; also Verne, Don Quixote, the 1948 essay on the Greeks and Ansen.
5. A pointer with no clear owner: 6. "it" in week3.questions.1.auden.paras.2 and "afterward" in week4.intro.1; also "the connection," "that point," and "he" in two places.
6. A sentence of forty words or more, made by joining: 6. week3.words.after.1 (55 words) and week4.questions.1.auden.paras.4 (48 words).
7. An introduction repeated word for word: 5 uses of 2 formulas. The seven words that introduce Pearce come three times (week3.questions.1.auden.paras.4, week4.intro.1, week4.questions.1.auden.paras.1). The eight-word gloss on the 1962 book comes twice (week4.questions.2.auden.paras.1, week4.questions.3.auden.paras.1).
8. A paragraph or block that closes on a short sentence or a neat antithesis: 3. week3.questions.1.auden.paras.3 and week3.questions.2.auden.paras.4; also week4.questions.1.auden.paras.4.
