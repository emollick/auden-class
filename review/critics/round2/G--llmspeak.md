# Part G (weeks 7 and 8), lens: writing that reads as machine-made

### week7.intro.1
- where: is also assigned this week
- problem: "This week" stands four times in the three opening paragraphs (twice here, once each in intro.2 and intro.3), about ninety words in all. A person writing by hand would hear it by the second.
- fix: End this entry "Wagner's opera *Tristan und Isolde* is assigned as well." In intro.2 write "For the exercise you write a story or essay with a lie in each sentence." Keep the one that opens intro.1 and the one in intro.3.
- weight: should

### week7.intro.3
- where: Begin learning by heart this week the canto
- problem: The time phrase is wedged between the verb and its object, so the reader waits five words for "the canto".
- fix: "This week, begin to learn by heart the canto you chose for yourself."
- weight: could

### week7.reading.3.paras.2
- where: listen for what Auden pointed out to the class
- problem: The sentence only announces the one after it, and "it" in "played it to them" has to reach back over a clause to find the duet. The lecture is used here before paras.3 has said there was one, and the playing of the record is then told a second time there.
- fix: "In the second act's love duet, listen for how Wagner keeps the two voices apart, phrase after phrase, and brings them into unison only as the duet ends. Auden told the class that Wagner does this on purpose." Leave the playing of the record to paras.3.
- weight: should

### week7.reading.3.paras.3
- where: and a new book that the list only recommends
- problem: One sentence carries four readings and three asides, joined by "with", "which" and "and", and the reader loses count before the last title. "A new book" was true in 1941 and reads oddly now.
- fix: "For the lecture he assigned this libretto and the libretto of *Carmen*, which is the opera for week 8. He also assigned *Romeo and Juliet*, which the list does not include, and Denis de Rougemont's *Love in the Western World*, then a new book, which the list only recommends."
- weight: should

### week7.reading.3.auden.paras.1
- where: The obstacle strengthens desire by blocking it.
- problem: Two short sentences say one thing twice, and "The obstacle" arrives with a definite article before any obstacle has been named.
- fix: "The second is that passion needs an obstacle, because desire grows stronger when it is blocked." Then go on with the sentence about the world supplying none.
- weight: could

### week7.reading.3.auden.paras.2
- where: and he takes this to be one thing
- problem: Four of the five sentences name Auden as the source, and the last does it twice ("goes on to say", "takes this to be"). The second does a job the first has already done.
- fix: Recast the last sentence with one attribution. Open it "He takes the love potion to mean, among other things, that the two care", let it run on unchanged as far as "paired them", and end it there.
- weight: should

### week7.reading.3.auden.paras.3
- where: Auden holds that this is right.
- problem: A six-word sentence that exists to carry the verb of saying. "This" has no single owner (the staging, or the fact that the consummation never comes), and the reader is not told right in what way until the next sentence.
- fix: Join it to the next sentence: "Auden holds that what the stage shows is the truth about such lovers, since each of them professes a boundless willingness to give all and yet holds back in practice."
- weight: should

### week7.reading.3.auden.paras.3
- where: which he calls Nirvana
- problem: The colon after the name holds the definition back for effect at the close of the paragraph (house rule 3).
- fix: Put a comma in place of the colon after "Nirvana".
- weight: could

### week7.reading.3.auden.paras.4
- where: and that, he says, is why
- problem: "He says" stands in two sentences running. The first covers the second, which continues the same thought.
- fix: Cut the second "he says", so that the sentence ends "and that is why the lovers in it are doomed."
- weight: should

### week7.questions.1.auden.paras.1
- where: to which the play gives several answers
- problem: A colon stages a question in the site's own prose, and the question speaks in the first person, which rule 7 keeps for quotation. Further on, "of one's own" borrows the impersonal pronoun in the same way.
- fix: "He finds that the whole play asks one question, what a person's real self is, and answers it in several ways." Later in the paragraph: "a life governed by a single overriding interest, like that of the peasant" and so on. Since there are only two ways, "The nearer" is the right form for "The nearest".
- weight: should

### week7.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: But Auden observes that the Peer of the play
- problem: "But" has nothing to push against. The paragraph before ends on Peer's wish to be an emperor, and the step from there to his having no ordinary appetites is not given, so the reader looks back for a contrast that is not on the page. In the last sentence "where" is doing the work of "whereas".
- fix: Begin "Auden observes that". End the paragraph "whereas Peer only plays at being each of them."
- weight: should

### week7.questions.1.auden.paras.5
- where: adds that its structure cannot be called satisfying
- problem: "Calls" and "cannot be called" meet in one short sentence. The passive is there only to avoid a second verb of saying.
- fix: "Auden calls the play fascinating and says that its structure does not satisfy."
- weight: could

### week7.questions.2.text
- where: so a fairy tale begins
- problem: This concerns the lead-in, not the question. The opening fragment set off by a colon, with the storyteller's "so ... begins", is a mock-antique turn, and the colon stages what follows (house rule 3).
- fix: "Many fairy tales begin with a father and three sons, and the hero of such a tale is the third." The rest stands.
- weight: should

### week7.questions.2.auden.title
- where: What Auden wrote about the third
- problem: Coming straight after a question on Alyosha, the singular in this heading promises Auden on Alyosha. The first paragraph then says that nothing of the sort is on record.
- fix: "What Auden wrote about third sons in fairy tales"
- weight: should

### week7.questions.2.auden.paras.1
- where: What he thought about third sons is on record.
- problem: A short sentence turned against the one before it, whose only work is to announce the next (house rule 8).
- fix: Replace the last three sentences with "No account of the course reports what Auden said in class about *The Brothers Karamazov*, but in 1952 he wrote an introduction to a volume of Grimm's and Andersen's tales and went through one tale about a third son closely."
- weight: should

### week7.questions.2.auden.paras.4
- where: which he also calls grace, and he says that
- problem: The first sentence only announces the rules. The last is three clauses hooked on with "which" and "and", with "also" twice in six words, and "often are so" is stiff.
- fix: "Auden draws from the tale the rule that people are seldom strong where they pride themselves on it, and are often strong in what they think unimportant or reckon a weakness. He adds that nobody succeeds by will alone, since success takes luck as well. He gives luck a second name, grace, and says that it is never withheld from those who ask humbly."
- weight: should

### week7.questions.2.auden.paras.5
- where: Compare the three reasons he gives why the hero
- problem: The pointer has come loose. In paras.3 the "three reasons" are the reasons the brothers fail; what is said of the hero comes after them and is not called reasons. A student told to check how many "hold for Alyosha" may test him against the brothers' faults.
- fix: Second sentence: "Set the three things Auden says of the hero beside your paper, and see how many are true of Alyosha when Book III ends."
- weight: must

### week7.making.1.brief.1
- where: the first thing Auden assigned the class
- problem: The colon holds the assignment back for a reveal (house rule 3). An ordinary "that ... was" says the same.
- fix: "One boy remembered that Auden's first assignment to the class was a story or essay in which each sentence contained a lie."
- weight: could

### week8.intro.1
- where: Ivan is talking to Alyosha across a table
- problem: House rule 11, not a fault of style: the two brothers are named for the first time this week with nothing to say who they are.
- fix: "In both, Ivan, the second brother, is talking to Alyosha, the youngest, across a table in a tavern." Flag only, if the editor holds that week 7 has introduced them.
- weight: could

### week8.reading.2.paras.1
- where: the version made for Vienna that fall by
- problem: "That fall" has no year to belong to. The date of the first performance is not given until paras.3, two paragraphs on, so the pointer hangs.
- fix: Begin the second sentence "At the first performance, in Paris in March 1875,". Then in paras.3 replace the sentence on the first performance with "Bizet died three months after the first performance."
- weight: must

### week8.reading.2.auden.paras.1
- where: His example is Don José and Carmen.
- problem: A short sentence set last for the beat. It brings in as news what the first sentence of the paragraph has already told, that the comparison is with the lovers in *Carmen*.
- fix: Cut it. In the sentence before, add ", as with Don José and Carmen," after "in life".
- weight: should

### week8.reading.2.auden.paras.2
- where: would be an idol no longer
- problem: The paragraph closes on a turned phrase, with "no longer" moved to the end for the cadence. It reads as the writer's flourish laid over Auden's point.
- fix: "If the idol asked the same of its worshipper, it would stop being one."
- weight: should

### week8.reading.2.auden.paras.3
- where: where he would depend on nobody
- problem: The last sentence opens on a quotation with no verb of saying and then runs on in the site's own words. "He" has to be found inside the quotation, after two sentences in which "he" was Auden and then Nemo.
- fix: "Auden goes on, [quotation kept], where José would depend on nobody and answer to no officer."
- weight: could

### week8.questions.1.auden.paras.1
- where: He names four, which are
- problem: "Stronger than one's neighbor" borrows the impersonal pronoun (rule 7). The sentence about many books only announces the list, and "names four, which are" is a join left by the revision; with it the passage has a verb of saying in three sentences running.
- fix: End the sentence on Satan "is to be stronger than the next person." Make one sentence of the two that follow: "Auden says that the evidence for Satan's case is in many books, among them", then the four titles as they stand, then ", and that the case is [quotation kept]" with the closing words of the paragraph unchanged.
- weight: should

### week8.questions.1.auden.paras.2
- where: he has no need to, because all three
- problem: "He" comes straight after Auden's name and for a moment reads as Auden. Its owner is Jesus, a sentence back.
- fix: "In Auden's view Jesus has no need to answer, because" and the rest as it stands.
- weight: should

### week8.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: does what the Prisoner will not do
- problem: The opening is a turn made for effect: it sets Auden against the Prisoner before it says what Auden did. The second sentence then says again that he writes a reply. The closing sentence only announces the three paragraphs that follow (house rule 8).
- fix: First sentence: "Auden then writes out the reply that the Prisoner does not give." In the second, replace the clause from "and he writes" with "and the reply is addressed to that attendant." Last sentence, flag only: it is a signpost over three paragraphs and the editor may judge that it earns its place. If not, cut it and open paras.4 "The first of three sentences the reply takes up is that".
- weight: should

### week8.questions.1.auden.paras.4
- where: though not the freedom of someone who could
- problem: "Freedom" comes five times in three sentences, and the middle sentence, from "could as well do one thing as another" on, has to be read twice. "That" in "claims to offer that too" could be the freedom or the truth.
- fix: "The reply grants that what is offered is freedom. It means the freedom to know the truth, and it sets aside the freedom of a person who might do one thing as soon as another because nothing matters. The Inquisitor too claims to give people the truth."
- weight: should

### week8.questions.1.auden.paras.6
- where: saying that if experience contradicted any of its words
- problem: Two verbs of saying in one sentence ("ends by offering", "saying that"); the first adds nothing the second does not say. The paragraph also passes over the middle one of the three things the Inquisitor names, and a student who has just been promised the sentences "in turn" will look for it.
- fix: "The reply ends by saying that any of its words which experience contradicted would be false, whoever spoke them." Flag only on the second point: if the reply has nothing on that word, say so in a clause; if it has, report it.
- weight: should

### week8.questions.1.auden.paras.7
- where: wrote the reply in 1939 and never printed it
- problem: The first paragraph of the block has already given the year and said that the book was printed only after his death. The sentence is here to set up "the next year".
- fix: Cut it and begin "The year after writing the reply, Auden went back to"; the rest stands.
- weight: should

### week8.questions.2.auden.paras.2
- where: The third was:
- problem: The block is counting two remarks ("The first is" here, "The second is" in the next paragraph), and between them "the third" counts something else, the definitions. The reader meets first, third, second in that order.
- fix: "One of the six was:" and then the quotation as it stands.
- weight: should

### week8.questions.2.auden.paras.4
- where: This site, not Auden, applies the first temptation
- problem: This is the block's third statement that the application is not Auden's: paras.1 says he left nothing on the chapter, paras.2 says "This site applies", and now this. "The first temptation" and "the second temptation" also add another set of ordinals to a block that has numbered its remarks and a definition, and paras.3 never numbered the temptations.
- fix: Say it once, in paras.1: "This site sets beside the chapter two remarks he made on other occasions, one from the classroom in 1941 and one from an introduction of 1952, and the application of both to Ivan is its own." In paras.2 cut the words from "This site" to "Ivan," and run the "who" clause into the next sentence, so that it reads "Ivan, who says ... eyes, does the reverse of ..." as one sentence. Here begin "Ivan's case comes near what Auden calls demonic defiance, though it differs because", and further on put "keep demonic despair in mind" where the second ordinal stands.
- weight: should

### week8.making.1.brief.1
- where: Mandelbaum wrote down what the two of them
- problem: The sentence only announces the next one (house rule 8).
- fix: Replace it and the opening of the next with "Mandelbaum wrote that the men were to learn"; the three aims stand as they are.
- weight: should

### week8.making.1.brief.2
- where: which is to understand what you read
- problem: The "which is" clause repeats, nearly word for word, the phrase that closed the paragraph before. The last sentence gives the length and the task, and the first two rules give both again directly below.
- fix: First sentence: "This exercise is practice in the second of them." Keep the sentence on the piece to retell. Cut the last sentence.
- weight: should

## Patterns

1. Counting out loud (18). A number is announced and then ticked off with ordinals. "The first/second/third is" or "was" stands 8 times; a count is announced 10 times ("two points", "two human ways", "three reasons" twice, "some rules", "names four", "three of the Inquisitor's sentences", "two remarks", "six of them", "the second of the three"). See week7.reading.3.auden.paras.1 and week8.questions.1.auden.paras.3 to paras.6. In week8.questions.2.auden three numberings run at once (remarks, definitions, temptations). Once per block is a help; as the default way of reporting Auden it reads as a template.

2. A fact or phrase given twice within a few lines (7). week7.intro.1 to intro.3 ("this week" four times); week7.reading.3.paras.2 and paras.3 (the record played); week7.reading.3.auden.paras.1 (the obstacle); week8.reading.2.auden.paras.1 (the example); week8.questions.1.auden.paras.1 and paras.7 (the year, the book unprinted); week8.making.1.brief.1 and brief.2 (the second aim, word for word); week8.making.1.brief.2 and rules.1 (the length).

3. A sentence that only announces the next, or exists only to carry the verb of saying (7). week7.reading.3.paras.2, week7.reading.3.auden.paras.3, week7.questions.2.auden.paras.1, week7.questions.2.auden.paras.4, week8.questions.1.auden.paras.1, week8.questions.1.auden.paras.3, week8.making.1.brief.1.

4. Pointers without a clear owner (7). "That fall" (week8.reading.2.paras.1), "the three reasons" (week7.questions.2.auden.paras.5), and "this", "it", "he", "that" in week7.reading.3.auden.paras.3, week7.reading.3.paras.2, week8.questions.1.auden.paras.2, week8.reading.2.auden.paras.3 and week8.questions.1.auden.paras.4.

5. The verb of saying doubled (5 places). Most uses are what rule 6 asks for and should stay. The excess is where one sentence carries two, or two sentences running each carry one for a single thought: week7.reading.3.auden.paras.2, paras.3 and paras.4, week8.questions.1.auden.paras.1 and paras.6. For scale: week7.reading.3.auden.paras.2 to paras.4 have 14 such verbs in 17 sentences, and in week8.questions.1.auden.paras.3 to paras.7 "the reply" stands 10 times, 8 of them as the speaker. An "it" for two or three of those would ease the run.

6. A colon holding something back (4). week7.questions.1.auden.paras.1, week7.questions.2.text, week7.making.1.brief.1, week7.reading.3.auden.paras.3.

7. A turn or a closing beat made for effect (4). week7.questions.2.auden.paras.1, week8.reading.2.auden.paras.1, week8.reading.2.auden.paras.2, week8.questions.1.auden.paras.3.

8. "This site" as disclaimer (4 in the part). Three statements of one fact in week8.questions.2.auden (paras.1, paras.2, paras.4); the others, week7.questions.2.auden.paras.5 and week8.making.1.source.1, are single and fine.

9. First-person or impersonal pronoun carried into the paraphrase (3, rule 7). Two in week7.questions.1.auden.paras.1, one in week8.questions.1.auden.paras.1.

10. Fixed formulas, no one of them a fault, that show when the weeks are read in a row. The gloss "the book of essays Auden published in 1962", hung on the title of his 1962 collection, comes 3 times, twice in week 7 (week7.reading.3.auden.paras.2, week7.questions.1.auden.paras.1). So does the gloss on Pearce ("a student in the class"), again twice in week 7. A paragraph opens "In *Title*, the [book] Auden [made] in [year], he" 3 times, twice in week8.reading.2.auden (paras.1, paras.3). "Is also assigned this week" closes week7.intro.1 and makes up week8.intro.3. "This exercise asks you to do" stands in week7.making.1.brief.2 and week8.making.1.brief.2. Where rule 11 forces a second introduction inside one week, shorten it ("his essays of 1962", "Pearce's notes") or vary the opening.
