Every string was also seen in its place: I went through #/door, #/unit/1, #/unit/1/meet/1 and #/unit/1/meet/2 as a reader in the running site (own words in the gaps, an answer to each question, all three outcomes of the line-marking step, both boards, the record played). No console errors. Counts in "Patterns" come from scripts over the same lines. Not checked: anything against Pearce or Auden's own pages, which I do not have. Where a finding leans on a fact, it says so.

In brief. 40 findings: 3 must, 28 should, 9 could (three of the 40 are on strings that stand on these pages but were left out of the prose file; see the last section before "Patterns"). The door and the first meeting are no longer a slog, and several notes do say why a word is the right word. House rule 1 holds throughout: no em-dash and no exclamation mark, and every question mark belongs to a real question or a quotation.

## The door

### `door.spreads[0].steps[0].notes.0` (coral)
- where: "Coral is the one thing the sea grows that looks like bone: white, hard and branching."
- problem: "The one thing" is a superlative offered as fact, and "white" is doubtful for this poet: Sonnet 130 uses coral as the measure of red (for the facts critics to confirm). It is also the first of two adjective triples in adjacent notes. The last two sentences are a short pair made to close on, and their point ("does not rot") is made again in the note on *fade*.
- fix: "Coral is the thing the sea grows that looks most like bone, hard and branching, and here the sea is growing it out of a skeleton."
- weight: should

### `door.spreads[0].steps[0].notes.1` (pearls)
- where: "hard, round and bright. The song trades one for the other, and the trade is upward"
- problem: A second three-adjective list straight after "white, hard and branching," with "hard" in both. "Trades … and the trade is upward" repeats the noun for the rhythm.
- fix: "Pearls are round and bright as eyes are, and hard where eyes are soft. The song has exchanged his eyes for something a diver would bring up as treasure."
- weight: should

### `door.spreads[0].steps[0].notes.3` (suffer)
- where: "The other sense is still in the word: the change is done to him."
- problem: The note never names the other sense. What follows the colon ("done to him") is the first sense, *undergo*, said again, so the sentence promises a second meaning and delivers the same one. A careful reader stops here. The colon is also there to stage the clause.
- fix: "In Shakespeare’s English *suffer* could mean no more than *undergo*, to have a thing done to you. That is its sense here, though the word carried pain in it then as it does now."
- weight: must

### `door.spreads[0].steps[0].notes.4` (strange)

- problem: "Foreign" and "come from somewhere else" say one thing twice, and the note stops at the gloss without using it. Two of its three sentences are about a different word.
- fix: Keep the first two sentences. End: "*Strange* in his English also meant foreign, as in *a strange land*."
- weight: should

### `door.spreads[0].steps[0].notes.5` (knell)

## Patterns

Counts are over lines 5 to 152 (142 strings, about 2,900 words, about 170 sentences of running prose). Most frequent first.

1. **Very short sentences: 27 of about 170 have six words or fewer.** One sentence in six. Alone each is plain. Together they make the short-long-short rhythm a reader starts to hear. See `w1.meetings[0].spreads[0].steps[0].notes.3` ("It flatters them.") and `w1.meetings[0].spreads[0].steps[0].after[0]`.

2. **The two-sided turn: 23** (9 on the door page, 7 in each meeting). A sentence, or two set side by side, built as X against Y: does not rot / makes something; soft / hard; one thing / another; near the top / near the bottom; no fire / frozen. Many of the contrasts are real, and Dante supplies some. The density is the mark. I would keep those where the contrast is the point (`door.spreads[0].steps[2].paras[2]`, `w1.meetings[1].spreads[2].steps[0].paras[1]`). The findings above remove or recast the weakest. Worst case: `w1.meetings[1].spreads[1].steps[0].after[1]`, where the second half was written for the balance and does not fit the board. Also `door.spreads[0].steps[0].notes.0`.

3. **A short sentence to close on: 13 of the 59 multi-sentence strings of running prose end on a sentence of nine words or fewer** (22 of 70 if chart rows, questions and blurbs are counted). Ten of the 13 have findings above. Among the 15 gap notes, 8 end on a beat or a turn. See `w1.meetings[0].spreads[1].steps[1].paras[1]` and `w1.meetings[1].spreads[3].steps[1].paras[0]`.

4. **Every reply opens with a verdict on the answer: 11 of the 12 replies and feedback strings, and in 10 the verdict is yes.** Whatever the reader chooses, the first sentence tells him who agrees: Tennyson, Ferdinand, Auden, listeners in Gluck’s day. Four open "That is," a fifth "It is that," and six of the openers are six words or fewer. Nobody is ever told no. That is the teacherly pat in structural form, and week after week it will read as a template. Start some replies with the fact.
