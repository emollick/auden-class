# Joy, read by the skeptic

Reader: the man who wrote the complaint, or one like him. Viewport 1440 by 900, a fresh browser context, every move made with the site's own controls before looking at what it gave back. Screenshots and scripts are in `review4/work/joy-skeptic/` (file names are given where they are the evidence). No console errors or failed requests on any rebuilt page. The server refused connections once for about ten seconds in the middle of the session and then came back; nothing was lost.

## 1. The first screen, as a picture

Evidence: `01-first-screen.png`.

What is there: a menu bar (`ENGLISH 135  The term  By heart  Your book  Sources`), a small-caps line naming the course, the title "A song with six words missing", a white sheet of typewriter text with six ruled gaps, and to its right forty words of instruction over a greyed-out button. The bottom quarter of the screen is empty cream and the top of a two-paragraph footer.

Would I stay? Yes, and only because of two things: the title is about a song and not about a course, and within five seconds I can see something to do that is not signing up or reading a syllabus. That is a real change. The first version made me read about the course before it let me near a poem.

As a picture it is quiet to the point of plain. One white rectangle on cream, no image, no color except the navy of the typewriter face. Nothing on it is ugly and nothing on it is alive but the gaps. Three things on this first screen still say "course web site" before I have touched anything:

- the menu bar. "English 135" and "The term" are the first words my eye meets, top left. Three of the four links lead a new visitor to an empty book, a list of cantos marked "not begun", and a fifteen-row reading list. I clicked "The term" out of habit on a second pass and was looking at a syllabus within one second of arriving (`42-new-the-term.png`).
- the greyed button with "Fill at least one gap first." beside it. A validation message shown before I have done anything is what a form does.
- the sentence "Nothing depends on being right." It is kind, and it tells me that somewhere being right is counted. It is. See finding 4.

What would make this screen a place: no menu until the song is done (the brand alone is enough); the cursor already in the first gap; the sheet narrower and set like a sheet of paper, with the instruction under it instead of in a second column that is two-thirds empty; and a picture or a sound somewhere on the first screen. It is called a song and it is silent (finding 33).

## 2. The opening, step by step

### Step 1. The six gaps

1. Did I want to do it? Yes. It was clear at once. It is a real question even for someone who half knows the song, which is most likely his case. I had four of six and wrote "undergo" and "bell" for the other two.
2. What I got back, in order:
   - a score, in bold: "Your word is the writer’s in 4 of the 6 gaps." This is the first thing the site says to me after my first move.
   - the sheet, marked: my two wrong words struck through in blue italic with the poet's in bold beside them (`03-after-show.png`). This is the best-looking thing on the site and the first small pleasure. It looks like a sheet somebody has gone over with a pen.
   - six notes, one per word. They are well written. The note on *suffer* told me something I could not have said myself: the old sense is only "undergo", and the other sense is still in the word, because the change "is done to him". The note on *knell* ends on a funeral that never ends, so that "nobody has to bury him". I enjoyed both.
   - But the notes are the same whatever I write. I ran the opening four ways (my words; six wrong words; one gap only; odd capitals and a phrase). Only the score and the tag ("your word too" / "you wrote ivory") changed. A reader who writes "ivory" for the first gap gets "0 of the 6" and then a note that never mentions ivory. The notes are written so that the commonest wrong guesses happen to be answered (*undergo*, *bell*, *rot*), which is craft, and it is why my own run felt answered. It was luck.
3. Assignment? Two small marks of one: the disabled button with its hint, and the score.
4. Drag? Not here. Forty words, then the move.
5. Pleasure? Yes: seeing "undergo" struck through beside "suffer", and the note that went with it.

### Step 2. Where the song comes from, and the question

1. Did I want to do it? Very much. Two plain sentences say who sings and to whom, and the second paragraph is one line: "His father is alive, on another part of the same island, and Ariel knows it." That is the surprise of the opening. I had just spent five minutes admiring a consolation, and it turned out to be told to a man whose father is not dead. Then: "What is the song, then?" with three answers (a lie, a comfort, the truth about something else). It is a real question and all three can be defended.
2. I said the third. I got back one sentence: "That is where Auden went with it." Then a dark panel, "What Auden said in class", with one documented sentence of Auden's from a student's notes (“What Shakespeare is dealing with here is transformation.”), and a last sentence that the source note marks as the site's own: the song is false about the king and true about everyone in the play, the king included. That last sentence is the payoff, and it is a good one. I had not put it that way.
   - "If you had said otherwise" opens to show the other two replies, and they are better than mine. To "a lie" the site says it is that, gives the plot, and then pushes back: a lie that takes this much care over bones and eyes is doing more than moving a prince across an island. To "a comfort": Ferdinand takes it so, but the comfort is for a death that has not happened. The reader who picks the answer nearest Auden's gets seven words and a pat. The readers who pick the others get an argument.
   - The panel says Auden "took two lines of this song as the subject of the whole play". It does not say which two, and the sheet is sitting right beside it (finding 11).
3. Assignment? No. This is the step where the complaint is furthest away.
4. Drag? No.
5. Pleasure? "Ariel knows it." And the site's closing sentence on the panel.

### Step 3. "That was one of his classes, in small"

Three paragraphs about the course and two buttons, "Begin with Dante" and "See the fifteen weeks". The first two paragraphs earn their place: no notes to be taken, tested on what you think and not on what he said, and the "verbal contraption" question. The third describes the site to me ("Each week begins as this page did", "a move of your own"). I have just done it, and I do not need it described. "A move of your own" is the builder's word, not a reader's (finding 27).

### Would I go on?

Yes. Honestly yes, which I did not expect. Five minutes, one song, one turn, one thing I had not thought. The opening would not draw the complaint.

Two reservations as I clicked "Begin with Dante". The last thing the opening told me was a count ("thirty-two books and operas in fifteen weeks") and a promise that every week begins as this page did, and both made me brace for fifteen more of the same page. And I had been scored.

## 3. Week 1, first meeting: "A speech in Hell"

"Begin with Dante" goes straight to `#/unit/1/meet/1` and skips the week page. Good.

First impression (`07-week1-top.png`): it is the opening again. Same title block, same white sheet on the left, same "MISSING WORDS", same grey button, same "Fill at least one gap first." The second page I see is the first page with a longer poem. Under the menu there is now a breadcrumb, "The term / Week 1", and a label, "WEEK 1 · FIRST MEETING".

### Step 1. Nine gaps in thirty-seven lines

1. Did I want to do it? Yes, a little less. The italic line above the sheet is short and sets the scene well. Thirty-seven lines of Longfellow is a lot to hold while looking for nine gaps, and "Do it before you open the book." is the first sentence on the site that sounds like a teacher.
2. The mechanics fought me, twice (findings 2 and 3):
   - The sheet is taller than the window and sits in its own scroll box with no scrollbar showing. Scrolling the page with the pointer over the right-hand column stops at the footer with the sheet cut off in mid-passage and no bottom edge. Only the first five of the nine gaps ever come into view that way (`08-page-bottom.png`).
   - When I had typed the ninth word there was no button anywhere on the screen. The right half was blank, the footer was up, and "Show the writer’s words" was a screen and a half above me (`09-m1-typed-last.png`). Enter does nothing. I scrolled back up to find it.
3. What I got back: the score first, "Your word is the writer’s in 3 of the 9 gaps." Then the page jumped to the top while the sheet stayed scrolled to its last lines, so the first note (on the first line) sat beside lines twenty to thirty-seven (`09-m1-after-show.png`).
   - The nine notes are the best prose on the site. On *brutes*: "It flatters them. It also leaves out everyone who stays at home and is not a beast." On *mad* (I wrote "bold"): "This is his own word for the voyage, said in Hell with the voyage long over." On *Another* (I wrote "God"): he has no name for the one who sank him. Three of the nine changed how I read the speech. That is interpretation, and it came before any book was opened.
   - It is also interpretation handed to me. My part was to guess; the reading was done by the site, in nine paragraphs that are the same for every reader (checked with nine other words: the notes do not change, and the score reads "0 of the 9").
4. Drag? Yes: the scroll box, the lost button, and 330 words of notes before I am asked anything again.
5. Pleasure? "Mad" where I had written "bold". That one landed.

### Step 2. "Is this man a hero?"

1. Did I want to do it? Yes. By then the notes had pushed me toward "No", so I said "Yes" to see what the site does with a reader who disagrees with its drift.
2. It did well. "Tennyson read him that way", the last line of his "Ulysses", and the fact that the line was cut into the memorial to Scott's polar party. Then: "Auden grants the likeness. But look at where Dante has put the man who says it." I learned something and was pushed. The reply to "No" is as good: Dante gives him the best lines in the canto and nobody says a word against him when he stops.
   - "Auden grants the likeness" is unclear on the page. Which likeness, and where does Auden grant it (finding 12)?
   - The third answer, "Both, and Dante knows it.", gets: "That is nearest to what Auden said. His reading is on the board below." Second time running that the third option is the one Auden held and gets the shortest reply. By week 3 a reader will click the third box (finding 10).
3. Assignment? No. 4. Drag? No. 5. Pleasure? Scott's memorial.

### Step 3. "A line to find"

"Somewhere in his speech to the crew an older tempter is speaking. Mark the lines where you hear him."

1. Did I want to do it? This is the best move on the site. It is a riddle, I can be wrong about it in an interesting way, and I do it on the poem itself. I guessed the serpent and marked three lines (two of the three the site had in mind, and one of my own).
2. What I got back: "You heard him.", the three lines washed yellow on the sheet with my own marks still showing beside them (`12-m1-after-mark.png`), the verse from Genesis, and then the part I had not seen. Both tempters speak to people who have been shown where the limit is: Eve has been told which tree, and the crew are looking at the pillars. Then a picture (Doré's ditch of flames) and the dark panel: Auden heard the Serpent in this speech too, in *The Enchafèd Flood*.
   - I made the observation and then found out it was his. That is the shape the brief describes, and when it works it works on a reader who came in sour. The panel's last sentence, marked as the site's own, is the best sentence in week 1: "Ulysses went where Dante goes, and went without being sent."
   - With three wrong lines the reply is plain and does not scold ("Look at the three lines beginning *Consider ye*, now marked in the passage."). "Leave this for now" folds the step away and lets me go on, though the panel just below then gives the answer.
3. Assignment? No. The step can be left.
4. Drag? The question is set as a three-line bold headline, heavier than anything else on the page (`11-m1-linefind.png`), and the sheet is half pushed under the menu bar while I work, because the step I am on is always at the very bottom of the page.
5. Pleasure? Yes. The pillars as the forbidden tree.

### Step 4. "For next time"

"Read the *Inferno* and the *Purgatorio*", sixty-seven cantos, and "A canto takes about ten minutes." Nobody does the multiplication for me. It is eleven hours. This is the hard work the complaint is about, and the site hands it over under a homework heading, with one good lure (look at the shore when you reach the first canto of the *Purgatorio*: "You have been there before.") and one good question to carry ("No one in Dante’s Hell asks to be let out. Watch for anyone who does."). The primary button at the foot is "Back to week 1", which takes me to a menu and not to the book (finding 13).

The lure is never picked up. Nothing marks the place in the reading page, and the second meeting does not mention the shore (finding 14).

### The first meeting, whole

About ten minutes. Three moves, each with something given back that I did not have before. If the first minute had not been a fight with a scroll box I would call it good without reserve. It does not feel like an assignment until its last block.

## 5. Week 1, second meeting: "Where Dante puts people"

Taken with the reading as done. Finished, the page is 10,459 pixels tall at this width, about eleven and a half screens, and holds nine separate tasks.

### Step 1. "Before anything else: did you like it?"

1. Did I want to do it? Yes. It is the most human question on the site and it is Auden's own: five verdicts, from "Good, and I like it." to "Trash, and I don’t like it." Doré's Charon stands beside it (`18-m2-top.png`). I chose "Good. I don’t like it yet, but I expect to." and wrote a line saying why.
2. What I got back: "Kept in your book." and two sentences about Auden's regard for Dante. All five verdicts get the same two sentences (I tried all five). To "Trash, and I don’t like it." they read as a mild rebuke. The best question in the meeting has the least reply (finding 15).
3. Assignment? No. 5. Pleasure? Being asked.

### Step 2. "A chart": where in Hell are they?

1. Did I want to do it? No. Seven dropdown menus, each reading "Choose", in a ruled table, under "Do it from memory first." (`19-m2-after-verdict-full.png`). It is a form, and it is a memory test. I placed five of seven.
2. What I got back: "5 of 7 as the book has them.", the table corrected, and then two paragraphs that are good: "The order is an argument about which sins are worst", fraud below violence because only a creature with reason can cheat, the lovers near the top and "the man who made the speech you began with" near the bottom. That last clause pays off "look at where Dante has put the man" from the first meeting.
   - The payoff is about judgment and the move was about recall. I was never asked where I would put anyone (finding 5).
3. Assignment? Yes. This is the step that most looks and feels like a quiz.

### Step 3. "No one in Dante’s Hell asks to be let out. Why not?"

1. Did I want to do it? Yes. The question was planted a meeting ago and it is a real one. I wrote sixty words.
2. The form of it is an essay box: a label that states a rule ("YOUR ANSWER FIRST"), a live word count, "A few sentences will do: at least 10 words.", a button that stays grey until the count is met, and a notice that Auden's answer is shown only when mine is set down. "Leave this for now" skips the step and Auden's answer with it. Ten letter a's separated by spaces open the gate (finding 6).
3. What I got back, after writing: my own answer set out in blue, and a dark panel that begins "No essay by Auden on the *Comedy* itself has been found, but he touches on this question in his introduction to a selection from Søren Kierkegaard". The thing I was waiting for, that Auden agreed with Landor that the people in the *Inferno* do not want to get out, arrives four sentences from the end as "He adds that". It is honest and exact about its sources. It is also the flattest paragraph in the week, at the one place where the week's question is answered (finding 7). The hint sent me to the end of Canto III, and those lines are never shown.
4. Then a second box, "Your reply": "Say where you and Auden differ, and what in the book decides it." Two essay boxes in a row.
5. Pleasure? In writing my own answer, yes. In what came back, no.

### Step 4. "On the blackboard": seven terraces, three ways for love to go wrong

1. Did I want to do it? Yes. The set-up is the best in the meeting: Virgil names four terraces and says Dante must find out the other three for himself. "You have read on, and you can." The poem leaves the exercise and the site passes it on.
2. Mechanics: the cards look as if they should be dragged and cannot be. Choosing a card and then clicking in a column does nothing; only the heading words take it (finding 16). Once I knew that, it was quick.
3. What I got back: "6 of your 7 cards are where the board has them." (a fourth score), the board with a line on each card, and on the one I had wrong "you put this under Too much" and why (they wanted revenge). Then two paragraphs, and the second is a real find: the lovers are in the highest circle of Hell and on the highest terrace of the mountain. "In both places Dante puts too much love closest to the good, and coldness of heart farthest from it." I had never set the two charts side by side.
4. Pleasure? Yes, that.

### Step 5. "How can people be blamed for what they love?"

The same form as step 3: label, box, "at least 10 words", gate. I wrote sixty-five words. The panel opens on an essay about masters and servants and on Caliban, and reaches my question in its last sentence. It contains one delight, Auden's remark that animals cannot fall because the tempter's promise is in the future tense, which animals do not have. That is the promise I heard in Ulysses' speech an hour ago, and the panel does not say so (finding 17).

### Step 6. The record

Corot's Orpheus in sepia on the left, the first warm color on the site, three short paragraphs, and the 1922 disc (`30-m2-opera.png`). "Play it before you go on.", then a button, "I have listened". It played and kept playing as I went on. Then: "What did you hear?" Grief; something too calm for what has just happened; or I could not tell from the tune.

This is the high point of the week. The question is a real one about music, each reply is a fact I was glad to have (Hanslick's remark that the tune would go as well with the opposite words; to "Grief": "Now try to say what in the sound is sad."), and the panel that follows ties the record to the week's question without saying that it is doing so: in opera, Auden says, whatever they suffer “they are doing exactly what they wish.” Then the site's own line: "Orpheus was told not to look, and he looked. Paolo and Francesca, in the second circle of Hell, are still together." I would have ended the week there.

### Step 7. "Who says it?" and the end

It does not end there. Four passages, a text box and a "Check" button under each, the right half of the screen empty (`29-m2-who-before.png`). I named all four. I got back the canto number and "4 of 4 placed." Then "Open the exercise" for a chart of Hell, and the week's last sentence: "Auden had the class learn its last canto by heart."

### The second meeting, whole

Thirty to forty minutes, nine tasks. Three of them are worth the reading that came before (the cards, the record, my own answer to the first question). Two are tests of memory. The two panels that answer the essay questions bury their points. It ends on a quiz score and a line about memorizing. Taken whole it is a problem set with some very good answers in the back.

Below that the page turns into a ledger: seven rows, six reading "not begun" and one "not chosen", five cantos of 136 to 148 lines, a link called "Examination", and a marking rule in small print (`35-heart-full.png`).

**The nine lines (`#/heart/gate`).** I did the Learn tab (text open, first letters, text covered) and the Write out tab, with slips of my own. It works. A slip is struck through in red with the right word after it, a red bar in the margin, and a verdict in bold: "2 words wrong or missing." (`38-learn-covered-slips.png`). It is the only place on the rebuilt site where my words are marked in red. # Findings

Seven must, twenty-seven should, six could. Numbered so the other sections can point to them.

## Must

### 1. `#/` (front page, after both meetings of week 1)
- where: "You have been through the term" / "Every meeting of the fifteen weeks is behind you."
- problem: False. One week is done and fourteen are marked "not yet rebuilt". This is the page he lands on when he comes back after being shown the opening and week 1.
- fix: While any week is unbuilt or unheld, head the page "Week 1 is behind you" and say: "You have held both meetings of week 1. Weeks 2 to 15 are not yet rebuilt. Your book holds what you set down." Keep the present wording for a reader who has in fact held all thirty meetings.
- weight: must

### 2. `#/unit/1/meet/1`, "Missing words", after the last gap
- where: "Show the writer’s words" / "Fill at least one gap first."
- problem: The button stays at the top of the right-hand column. By the fifth gap it has scrolled out of sight, and after the ninth the screen shows the end of the sheet, a blank right half and the footer. Enter in a gap does nothing. The reader has to guess that the way on is back up.
- fix: Put the button under the last line of the sheet as well (or keep the instruction and button fixed in view while the sheet scrolls), and let Enter in the last gap show the words. The same on the opening, where Enter also does nothing.
- weight: must

### 3. `#/unit/1/meet/1`, the typed sheet before the words are shown
- where: the sheet beginning "I and my company were" (37 lines, 9 gaps)
- problem: The sheet is in a scroll box 805 pixels high with no scrollbar showing. Scrolling the page ends at the footer with the sheet sliced off in mid-passage and no bottom edge; gaps 6 to 9 can be reached only by scrolling again with the pointer over the sheet. It looks broken, and a reader told "nine words" can find five.
- fix: Until the words are shown, let the sheet run its full length in the page with no inner scroll. Keep the box only afterward, when the right-hand column is the long one, and then give it a visible bottom edge and a fade where lines continue. After "Show the writer’s words", scroll the sheet back to its first line (it is left at its last lines now, beside a note on line 1).
- weight: must

### 4. The score lines (opening; both meetings)
- where: "Your word is the writer’s in 4 of the 6 gaps." / "5 of 7 as the book has them." / "6 of your 7 cards are where the board has them." / "4 of 4 placed."
- problem: The first thing the site says after each move is a count of right answers, in bold, twice directly after "Nothing depends on being right." A new visitor who does not know the song is told "0 of the 6". A score is what a class web site gives back. The marked sheet, table and board already show every difference.
- fix: Delete all four lines. If a sentence is wanted there, describe the marking and do not count: for the gaps, "The sheet now shows the writer’s words. Where yours was different, it is struck through beside his."; for the chart, "The chart now shows where the book has them."; for the cards, "The board now shows the mountain as Virgil gives it."; for the four passages, nothing.
- weight: must

### 5. `#/unit/1/meet/2`, "A chart"
- where: "Where in Hell are they?" / "Do it from memory first."
- problem: Seven dropdown menus in a ruled table, and a test of recall. It is the plainest form on the rebuilt site, and it asks the one thing the complaint named. The payoff ("The order is an argument about which sins are worst") is about judgment and would land harder on a reader who had just judged.
- fix: Change the move from recall to judgment. Heading: "Which of these seven is the worst?" Instruction: "Put them in order as you would sentence them, the least guilty first. Then see the order Dante gives them." Give back the two orders side by side, with one line for each name that moved ("You put Ulysses second. Dante puts him sixth, below the murderers."), and then the two paragraphs that are there now. Do it on a drawing, not in menus: the pit as a funnel on the dark board, a gate and nine rings, names placed by choosing a name and then a ring. If the from-memory chart is kept at all, it belongs on the week page under "Something to make".
- weight: must

### 6. `#/unit/1/meet/2`, both "Your answer first" steps
- where: "A few sentences will do: at least 10 words." / "What Auden said is shown when your own answer is set down."
- problem: A word minimum, a live word count, a button disabled until the count is met, and the answer withheld from anyone who declines. That is the form of a graded assignment, and it is a formality: ten single letters open it. A reader in a hurry will type junk or press "Leave this for now" and never see the answer to the week's question.
- fix: Keep the order (yours first) and drop the apparatus. Remove the word count. Replace the hint with "A sentence is enough." and enable the button as soon as anything is written. Replace the notice under the buttons with "Yours stays as you wrote it, and you can reply to his." Add a third, quiet control beside "Leave this for now": "See what Auden said without writing". Change the label "Your answer first" to "A question" on both.
- weight: must

### 7. `#/unit/1/meet/2`, first "What Auden made of it"
- where: "No essay by Auden on the *Comedy* itself has been found, but he touches on this question in his introduction"
- problem: This panel answers the question the reader has carried through sixty-seven cantos and has just written about. It opens with a disclaimer about sources and a note on who Kierkegaard was, and gives the point (the damned do not want to get out) near the end as an aside.
- fix: Same facts, point first, the disclaimer in the source note:

  "Auden held that the people in the *Inferno* do not want to get out. The remark is Walter Savage Landor’s, and Auden says that Landor was right. He says it in his introduction to a selection from Søren Kierkegaard (1952), at the end of this argument.

  The relation of each creature to the God of the Bible cannot be broken. People may try to put it out of their minds, Auden says, but they do not get rid of it by doing so; they still feel it, as guilt and despair. On this view the wrath of God does not describe a feeling in God but the way God appears to anyone who distorts or denies that relation. Auden says that this is why Dante could write over the gate that Hell was made by Power, Wisdom and Love.

  Auden does not say how Landor’s remark follows from the rest. As this site reads the passage, the damned would have to stop denying their relation to God before they could ask to be let out.

  Sources: “Søren Kierkegaard,” in *Forewords and Afterwords*, p. 176. No essay by Auden on the *Comedy* itself has been found. Kierkegaard’s *Fear and Trembling* is assigned in week 6."

  Before the panel, show the three lines the hint pointed to (the end of *Inferno* III, where Virgil says what drives the crowd to the boat), on a typed sheet beside the reader's answer. The poem answers first, then Auden.

## Should

### 8. Opening and `#/unit/1/meet/1`, the notes on the missing words
- where: "coral / you wrote ivory / Coral is the one thing the sea grows that looks like bone"
- problem: The brief says the reader's move is "set beside the poet’s". Only the tag is. The fifteen notes are word for word the same whatever is written (tried with four sets of words on the song and two on the speech). A reader who writes "die" for *fade* or "Heaven" for *Another* is told nothing about that word.
- fix: For each gap keep a short list of the guesses most readers will make, with one sentence each, shown above the standing note when the reader's word is on the list. For example, *suffer*, if the reader wrote "undergo": "Your word is the plain sense of his. His has the pain in it as well." *Knell*, if "bell": "He keeps *bell* for the last word of the song." *Mad*, if "bold", "brave" or "last": "That is how the crew heard it. He is telling it afterward, from Hell." *Another*, if "God" or "Heaven": "That is who is meant. Ulysses has no name for him." When the word is not on the list, show the note alone, as now.
- weight: should

### 9. `#/unit/1/meet/1`, the nine notes and the sheet
- where: the notes from "old / your word too" to "Another / you wrote God"
- problem: The notes run down the right for a screen and a half while the sheet scrolls on its own. Nothing ties a note to its line, so the reader is reading about a word that is off the top or bottom of the sheet.
- fix: Choosing a note's word brings its line into view on the sheet and marks it; choosing a marked word on the sheet opens its note. Open the notes on the words the reader missed, and show the ones matched as a single line each that can be opened.
- weight: should

### 10. Opening and `#/unit/1/meet/1`, the three-way questions
- where: "That is where Auden went with it." / "That is nearest to what Auden said. His reading is on the board below."
- problem: Twice running the third option is the one Auden held, and both times it gets the shortest reply, a confirmation. The other two get an argument. By the third week a reader will pick the third box and expect the pat.
- fix: Vary the position from week to week, and give the nearest answer something to do. On the song, after "You said: The truth, but about something else.", say: "Auden went the same way. Now and then, offer a question where no option is Auden's and his view arrives as a fourth.
- weight: should
