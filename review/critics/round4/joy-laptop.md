# Joy, interpretation and payoff: a laptop reader's report

Reader: a curious adult who reads a poem now and then, has never read Dante, has not seen *The Tempest*. Laptop, 1280 by 850, a fresh browser carried from step to step. Scripts, saved state and screenshots are in `review4/work/joy-laptop/` (screenshots in `shots/`). No console errors, page errors or failed requests were seen on any page visited, except where a finding says so.

## Running account

For each step: what I did, then the five questions of the brief in short (want / got back / assignment / drag / pleasure).

### 1. The opening (#/)

**The six gaps.** I wrote shells, pearls, decay, undergo, strange, bell. Two were the poet's.
- Want: yes, at once. One sentence of instruction, a typed sheet, six blanks. A real question: three of my words were wrong in ways worth hearing about.
- Got back: my word struck through beside the poet's on the sheet, and a note on each word. The notes on *suffer* (I had written undergo, and the note says that is what it meant, and what else is still in the word) and on *knell* (the funeral that never ends) told me things I had not seen. The notes are the same whatever you write (checked with a second visitor), but they are written to meet the likely wrong words, and they met mine.
- Assignment: one moment. The first thing back, in bold, is a count: "Your word is the writer's in 2 of the 6 gaps." After "Nothing depends on being right" that reads as a mark.
- Drag: none. The sheet stays in sight while the notes scroll, which is exactly right.
- Pleasure: yes. Then the two sentences that say whose father this is, and that Ariel knows he is alive. I did not know the play and that turned the whole song over.

**"What is the song, then?"** I said a lie, told to lead him somewhere.
- Want: yes. I had just been handed the fact that made it a question.
- Got back: "It is that", the reason, and then a sentence that pushed me past my own answer (a lie that takes this much care is doing more). That depended on what I chose. The other two answers are folded under "If you had said otherwise", which I opened.
- Assignment: no.
- Drag: no.
- Pleasure: the board. Auden's one sentence on transformation, and then the site's own line that the song is false about the king and true about everyone in the play. That is the payoff of the whole page and it landed.

**"That was one of his classes, in small."** Three short paragraphs and two buttons. Nothing to do; it did not drag. "Thirty-two books and operas in fifteen weeks" gave me a small chill, and nothing on the page tells me I may do less. I pressed "Begin with Dante".

One thing on coming back: until I had begun week 1, reloading #/ showed the finished opening from the top, with the two onward buttons 3,000 pixels down. Once I had started the first meeting, #/ became the week's front page (step 6).

### 2. Week 1: the first meeting (#/unit/1/meet/1) and the week's page

"Begin with Dante" goes straight to the first meeting; the week's page comes after it, by "Back to week 1".

**The nine gaps.** I wrote weary, unknown, race, beasts, eager, wings, bold, grief, the gods. Two were Longfellow's.
- Want: yes. The italic line above the sheet says who is speaking and where, and that is all I needed.
- Got back: the best notes on the site. *Unpeopled* (nothing out there but the knowing), *brutes* (it flatters them, and leaves out everyone who stays at home), *mad* (I had written bold; it is his own word, said in Hell). They turned the speech from stirring to suspect while I watched.
- Assignment: the count again ("2 of the 9 gaps"), and two notes that hand me things to remember for books I have not opened ("Keep the phrase in mind for the first canto of the *Purgatorio*").
- Drag: the mechanics, not the words. The sheet sits in a box with its own scroll. With the pointer over the right-hand column, scrolling took me to the footer with the passage apparently ending at the close of the speech and four gaps showing out of nine. When I pressed the button the notes began at line 1 and the sheet was still at line 37. Nine notes in a column, and the sheet does not follow them.
- Pleasure: bold against mad.

**"Is this man a hero?"** I said No.
- Want: yes. The notes had made me take a side.
- Got back: "That is the charge against him", and then the weight on the other side: Dante gives him the best lines and nobody rebukes him. It argued with me, which is what I wanted.
- Assignment: no. But this is the second question in a row whose third choice is the one the site calls nearest to Auden. I now know which to pick next time.
- Drag: none.

**"A line to find."** I marked the three lines from the appeal to their origin down to virtue and knowledge.
- Want: very much. The best move of the two meetings: a riddle to solve by ear.
- Got back: "You heard him", Genesis 3:5, and a thought I had not had: both tempters speak to people who have just been shown the limit. I had guessed the serpent. I had not seen the pillars as the tree.
- Assignment: no. "Leave this for now" is a kindness.
- Drag: none.
- Pleasure: yes, when my three lines turned yellow.

**The picture and the board.** Doré's plate is large and handsome and stays beside the text. The board reports Auden with a page number and marks its last paragraph as the site's. That paragraph (Dante using Ulysses' own word of himself, and looking back at the track from Paradise) is the payoff, and by then I had earned it. It would have been the same whatever I did, and I did not mind.

**"For next time."** Here I would have left. The label is a classroom's, the instruction is "Read the *Inferno* and the *Purgatorio*", and no shorter way is offered. The question to carry in (nobody in Hell asks to be let out) is excellent, and it worked on me in canto III.

**The week's page (#/unit/1).** Short and good-looking, the dark wood on the right. It is the week in order: first meeting ("held on October 2"), the book, the opera with a record that plays (checked: 7 seconds in, of 4:07), the second meeting, three tiles. It says the size plainly, "sixty-seven cantos", and offers nothing smaller. Two of the three tiles ("A chart of Hell", "Passages to place") lead to old pages: "names must be spelled correctly", "your best score is kept".

### 3. Reading: *Inferno* I, III, V, XXVI in Longfellow

"Inferno" on the week's page opens the prose translation; "Longfellow's verse" is one click away in the Versions line. I read the four cantos and pressed "I have read this" under each.
- The page is the text and nothing else, well set, with line numbers. No argument at the head, no gloss, no picture, nothing to look for. In canto I, I was lost from the Greyhound to Camilla; in V at Galeotto.
- The carried question paid off: in canto III, three lines near the end say the dead are eager to cross. I would not have stopped there without it. I kept them.
- I kept four passages by selecting them (I 1 to 3, III 124 to 126, V 138, XXVI 97 to 99). A dark bar asks "Keep these lines in your book?"; one press, "Kept." It is quick and pleasant. Flaws: a dead "Keep them" button stays in the bar after "Kept."; kept lines are not marked in the text; a drag that ends inside a word is stored that way.
- Under every canto the one black button is "I have read this", which becomes "Read on October 2 · Unmark" and puts a tick in a list of cantos with line counts. That is the old ledger.
- Canto XXVI after the meeting was the best reading of the four: I knew the speech, and now saw the son, the father and the wife he names before it.

### 4. By heart: the nine lines (#/heart/gate)

I went by the tile on the week's page (the first meeting also ends with a button for it).
- Read: the nine lines, "Read it aloud.", and three sentences on how the nine are built, which did help me hold them.
- Learn: "Stretch 1 of 1". I wrote it with the text open ("Every word right."), then from first letters, where I gave the old word in line 8 its modern form ("1 word wrong or missing", my word struck out in red, the box emptied, write it all again), then with the text covered (right; it forgave missing quotation marks, capitals and commas). The status went from "not begun" to "being learned".
- Write out: from memory, with four honest slips (pain for the last word of line 2, moved for the verb in line 4, the adjective before Love left out, the modern form again in line 8). Back came "Your memory changed 4 words of 61", three pairs under "Where your memory changed the text", a sentence telling me the writer did something I did not expect at each place and to ask what his word does that mine does not, and then "Your script, marked", in red.
- Want: more than I expected. Nine lines is a fair ask, and the By heart page sells it as an experiment ("You can find out here what it does").
- Got back: the right idea and half a payoff. The frame turns slips into questions about the poem, which is the missing-word sheet again. But it stops at the question. On the sheets every word had a note; here I am told to ask and left alone with two words I do not know. And the one thing in these nine lines that would have astonished me, that Love is named among the makers of the gate, nobody says. The word I dropped stood in that very phrase, and dropped words are not in the list of pairs.
- Assignment: yes, more than anywhere else in the rebuilt part. "A stretch is done when you have written it with the text covered and every word right." "A piece this short counts as known when every word is right." "Your script, marked." "Leave the text alone until…". Status words follow me about: not begun, being learned, written out with slips.
- Drag: nine lines three times under Learn, and all nine again for one slip.
- Pleasure: a small real one, seeing my words and his set out in pairs and finding that my memory had modernized him twice.

**The By heart page (#/heart).** The first three paragraphs are the best pitch for memorizing I have read: the student with the typed pages over her kitchen sink, and "the missing-word sheet turned round, with every word missing". Then: "You have 9 lines by heart", on the day I wrote them out with four slips. My four kept passages have turned into rows stamped "not begun". Below them are five cantos (136 to 148 lines each, "not begun"), an examination, and a rule for what "counts as known". I tried one of my own lines (the one from canto V): same three tabs, same marking, and it worked.

### 5. The second meeting (#/unit/1/meet/2), "Where Dante puts people"

Reached by "Begin" on the week's page. Eight steps and, by the end, a page nearly thirteen screens long.

**"Did you like it?"** Auden's five verdicts. I chose "Good. I don't like it yet, but I expect to", and wrote a line saying why.
- Want: yes, at once. Nobody on a course site has asked me that before, and "Trash, and I like it anyway" made me laugh.
- Got back: "Kept in your book", and two sentences on Auden putting Dante first among his judges. Nothing that depended on my choice; I did not need it to.
- Assignment: no. Drag: no. Pleasure: yes, the five choices themselves.

**"Where in Hell are they?"** Seven people, seven drop-down menus. I placed three from my reading and guessed four.
- Want: half. I wanted to know; I did not want a form. It is the one step that looks like a web form, and "Do it from memory first" is an examiner's sentence.
- Got back: "5 of 7 as the book has them" in bold, each place with its canto and a line, and then two paragraphs that were worth it: the order is an argument, fraud lies below violence because only a creature with reason can cheat, and the bottom is ice.
- Assignment: yes. The button stays grey until all seven menus are filled and nothing says why.
- Pleasure: the ice.

**"No one in Dante's Hell asks to be let out. Why not?"** I wrote 67 words, starting from the lines I had kept in canto III.
- Want: yes. I had carried this question through four cantos and had something to say. This is the best-prepared move on the site.
- Got back: my answer set above a board. The board begins with what has not been found and who Kierkegaard is, then a paragraph of theology in the abstract, and only then the two things I wanted: Auden agreeing with Landor that the people in the *Inferno* do not want to get out, and the gate said to be made by Power, Wisdom and Love. It then says Auden does not explain the connection and gives the site's own. Honest, but the slowest board of the five.
- Assignment: a little. The grey button, "at least 10 words", and a reply prompt worded like an essay question. I wrote a reply anyway.
- Pleasure: the gate turning up again. Nobody pointed out that I had just learned those lines.

**The blackboard: seven cards, three ways for love to go wrong.** I put pride under "Too much" and the other six where Virgil has them.
- Want: yes. The passage is beside the board and the puzzle can be worked from it, which is right for someone who has not read the *Purgatorio*.
- Got back: "6 of your 7 cards", my one error answered in a line (they hoped to rise by another's fall), and then the best payoff in the meeting: in Hell and on the mountain alike the lovers stand nearest the good.
- Assignment: the count, and the grey button again.
- Pleasure: yes, twice: the board itself, and the lovers.

**"How can people be blamed for what they love?"** I wrote 57 words and a reply.
- Want: less. A second writing box so soon, with the same two sentences of instructions word for word, began to feel like sheet two of a worksheet.
- Got back: a board that goes round by Caliban and *The Tempest* ("which you will read in week 5") to say again what the passage had said, then admits Auden does not apply the lines to the question. One sentence was a joy: animals cannot fall because the tempter's promise is in the future tense.
- Drag: here.

**The record.** A Corot, three short paragraphs, a disc that turns. I pressed play (it ran: 25 seconds, of 4:07), then "I have listened", then answered "Grief."
- Want: yes.
- Got back: the best single response on the site. Most people hear grief; now say what in the sound is sad; the key is major; and the old remark that the tune would fit the opposite words. Then Auden on opera as willfulness, and the site's two sentences joining Orpheus to Paolo and Francesca. Every thread of the meeting meets here.
- Assignment: only the button "I have listened", an attestation like "I have read this".
- Pleasure: the strongest of the whole visit.

**"Who says it?"** Four passages, a box and a "Check" button each. I answered three from what the meeting had taught me and wrote "I don't know" for the fourth.
- Want: no. After the record this is a test, and it is introduced as one ("Auden's examination…").
- Got back: my word, the right name, a citation. "3 of 4 placed." Nothing about any passage.
- Assignment: yes, plainly.

**The end.** "Open the exercise" (an old page), "For next time", "On to week 2" (an old page, where "both are required"). The last sentence of the rebuilt week is about learning a canto by heart.
