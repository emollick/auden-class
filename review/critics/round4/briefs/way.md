# Your lens: is this the way Auden taught?

The commissioner asked for a site that would "reconstruct the process of learning poetry and prose in the way that auden intended", and rejected the first version as "a class website" with "no joy or interpretation, just assignments." You are the reader who knows how Auden actually taught and what he said reading and teaching were for, and who judges the rebuilt opening and week 1 against that.

First learn the record. Researchers' notes are under /tmp/claude-0/-home-user/16660bbc-c653-54ad-936c-75a5d2bff792/scratchpad/research/ : `B_pedagogy.md` (how he taught, at Michigan, Swarthmore, the New School and elsewhere, and what he wrote about teaching and reading) and `A_michigan_course.md` (the 1941 course and what its students recorded). `C_auden_on_works.md` has what he wrote about each book on the list; read its sections on Dante and on Gluck's *Orpheus* and opera. The notes are long; read them in pieces and take your own notes. They mark what is documented and what is only recalled.

Then go through the rebuilt parts in the running site as a reader would (the common brief lists the routes), doing every step, and read the prose file for the wording.

Report, as findings in the common form:

1. Anything in the rebuilt parts that the record of his teaching does not support, or that gets his practice or his attitude wrong: a method presented as his that is not documented as his; a tone he would have despised; a way of treating a poem that contradicts what he said about reading; places where the site leads the reader to one right answer when his practice was to send people back to the text to judge for themselves, or the reverse.
2. For each step of the opening and of the two meetings: which documented practice of his it reconstructs (missing-word sheets, learning by heart, questions comparing one book with another, charts on the board, records played in class, set forms, parody, translation with a dictionary, examinations of identifying passages, the commonplace book, the two questions of any poem, the five verdicts, the Eden questionnaire, anything else in the notes), and how faithfully. Say where a step is the site's own invention, and whether the site says so.
3. Documented practices and convictions of his that would bring joy, play and interpretation, and that the rebuilt parts do not yet use. For each: what the record says (with the note's section so it can be found), and a concrete proposal for a step a reader could do on a web page, naming a week or book of the course where it would fit best. The reading list is on the site at #/syllabus and the weeks at #/term. Give your ten best proposals, no more, the strongest first.
4. Where the site is still a "class website": anything that makes the reader a pupil doing set work, where Auden's own account of reading would have made him a person finding out what he thinks.
5. Anything cheesy: role-play, a fake professor's voice, period costume.

Then give a plain verdict in a short section "Does it reconstruct his way?": what the rebuilt parts get right, what they get wrong, and the one change that would matter most.

Auden's writings and the witnesses' memoirs are in copyright. Report them in your own words with the note's section or the source and page; never copy a full sentence of a source into a message, your thinking, a tool call or the report.
