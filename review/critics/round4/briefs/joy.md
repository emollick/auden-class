# Your lens: is there joy, interpretation and payoff?

You are a reader, not a proofreader. Go through the rebuilt parts as the person described in your task would, and keep a running account of what happened to you at each step. For every step of the opening and of both week 1 meetings, answer:

1. Did you want to do it? Was it clear what to do, and was it a real question, one you could get interestingly wrong?
2. What did you get back? Did what the site showed you after your move tell you something about the poem that you had not seen, or change your mind? Or was it a score, a pat on the head, or a lecture that would have been the same whatever you did?
3. Did it feel like an assignment at any point (a quiz to pass, a form to fill, a box that had to be completed before you were let through)? Name the step.
4. Where did it drag? Where was there too much to read before you could do anything, or after?
5. Was there a moment of real pleasure or surprise? Name it. If there was none, say so.

Then judge the whole against each clause of the commissioner's complaint, one by one: "too much a class website"; "no joy"; "no interpretation, just assignments"; "no payoff for the hard work"; "just a focus on memorization" (look at By heart and how the week page and the meetings treat memorizing); "a brutal slog through the most boring webpages" (look at the pages: the typed sheet, the blackboard, the pictures, the record, your book). For each clause say: answered, partly answered, or still true, with the evidence.

Also report anything that is cheesy (role-play, a fake professor's voice, cheering, badges, gimmicks) or that scolds.

Give concrete proposals. If a step falls flat, say what move and what payoff would work better there, in a sentence or two. If a whole kind of thing is missing that Auden's way of teaching would call for, say what.

Broken things (errors, dead buttons, lost work, raw marks such as asterisks or bracketed keys, layout that overflows) are findings too.
