# Your lens: writing that reads as machine-made, and writing that is dead

The words were written by an AI. The commissioner asked for critics to "scour every word" for LLM-speak, and he called the first version boring. So you are looking for two things: the marks of a machine, and prose so dutiful that it kills the interest of the thing it is about.

Read every sentence in your part. For each ask: would a person who knows these poems well, showing one to a friend, have said this sentence? Does it do work for the reader? You are looking for:

1. Stock phrases and signposts. Sentences that announce, frame, sum up or reassure and do not say a thing.
2. Reflex symmetry. "Not X but Y," "less X than Y," "X, and not Y," used for the rhythm when nobody holds X. Sentences built in mirrored pairs. Lists of exactly three by habit. Count the triples.
3. Endings that pose. A note or paragraph that closes on an aphorism, a neat reversal, a moral, or a short sentence added for the beat. The notes beside the gaps and the replies to choices are short and are the likeliest place for this: check the last sentence of every one.
4. Inflated or vague diction. Abstract nouns that could be cut. Verbs such as explore, navigate, underscore, highlight, capture, embody. Words such as rich, deep, powerful, crucial, journey, lens, landscape. "A kind of," "a sense of."
5. Padding. A sentence that repeats the one before in other words. A clause that tells the reader what he will feel or find. The obvious explained.
6. Mannered plainness. A staccato that calls attention to itself, a gnomic or mock-antique tone, coyness, a knowing wink, a quaint word where the ordinary one would do. Fragments posing as sentences.
7. Sameness. Every note opening the same way. One sentence frame used again and again ("He does not say…", "This is…"). The same closing move in note after note. Count them.
8. Hedging, throat-clearing, apology, and the site talking about itself more than the reader needs.
9. Unearned authority. A confident reading of a line with nothing in the line to support it. Evaluative adjectives offered as fact.
10. The teacherly pat. Praise or reassurance that would be the same whatever the reader had done; a reply that tells the reader he is right or wrong and nothing more.
11. Deadness. A sentence so cautious that it has no life: a caption that only labels, a note that tells the reader what the word means and nothing about why it is the right word there, an instruction that reads like a form. Where a livelier plain sentence is possible, write it. No hype, no exclamation, nothing a careful person would be embarrassed to have said.
12. Anything else that made you think a machine wrote it. Trust the reaction and say what set it off.

Do not flag plain statements of fact, instructions that are simply clear, the questions put to the reader, or quotations. Do not flag a verb of saying or a "this site's" merely because the house rules require one there; flag it when the same job is done twice in a paragraph.

For each finding write the replacement in full, and make sure the replacement does not commit a different fault from the list or break a house rule. Where the right fix is to cut, say cut. If the replacement would need a fact you do not have, say what is missing and do not invent it.

Read the whole of your part once before you write anything, so that you can see what recurs. Then go through it string by string. Do not skip the short ones. End the report with a section "Patterns": the faults that recur, most frequent first, each with a count and two labels where it can be seen.
