# What you are looking at

A web site built from W. H. Auden's 1941 University of Michigan course, "Fate and the Individual in European Literature" (English 135). A first version was published as a course web site: reading by the week, a ledger of pages owed, tests, cantos to memorize, an examination. The person who commissioned it rejected that version in these words:

"i feel like you made this too much a class website rathe than a way to explore poetry in the way Auden wanted us to. there is no joy or interpretation, just assignments. no payoff for the hard work, just a focus on memorization. it feels like a brutal slog through the most boring webpages"

His original brief still stands: it must not be a static web page, it must not be "a cheesy simulation", it should "reconstruct the process of learning poetry and prose in the way that auden intended", and before anything is published critics must "scour every word you write for LLM-speak or other writing that would get in the way of your message."

The site is being rebuilt to answer him. So far the opening and week 1 are rebuilt, and he will be shown those two before the other fourteen weeks are converted. You are one of the readers who see them first. Be hard on them. If his complaint still holds anywhere, say exactly where.

# The shape it is meant to have

Each sitting starts from a poem or passage and ends with a payoff. The reader makes a move first (supplies a missing word, answers a question, marks a line, sorts cards on a blackboard, listens to a record), and then that move is set beside the poet's or beside what Auden said. Interpretation comes first, not after a paper is handed in. Memorizing is one way in among others. What the reader makes is kept as a book of their own. No calendar, no pages owed, no to-do rows.

# The voice the words are meant to have

Plain, direct, second person, American English. It reports what is documented and names the source; it says so when a reading is the site's own. It does not imitate Auden and never pretends to be him. House rules:

1. No em-dashes in the site's own prose. No exclamation marks. No rhetorical questions (the questions put to the reader are real questions).
2. No "not X but Y" turned for effect. No reflex groups of three. No hedging, no hype, no colon used to stage a reveal.
3. No short sentence set at the end of a paragraph for the beat. No sentence that only announces the next one.
4. A judgment of Auden's has a verb of saying, so the reader always knows who holds the opinion. A reading that is the site's own is marked as the site's.
5. Quotations from Auden and other writers in copyright are a line or two at most; everything else is reported in the site's own words.
6. Titles of books, plays, operas and long poems are italic (asterisks in the source); essays and short poems are in curly double quotes.

# Rules for your own work

- Write your findings to the one report file named in your task, and scripts or screenshots only inside the working folder named there. Do not edit anything under /home/user/auden-course or /mnt/project-files.
- Do not call any tool whose name begins mcp__hearthbot__. Do not install anything. If a web site or the network proxy refuses a request, do not try to get round it; report "could not check".
- Never copy out more than ten words in a row from a book, essay, poem or translation, in a message, in your thinking, in a tool call or in the report. Point to passages by where they are and describe them in your own words. The site's own sentences may be quoted up to about twenty words to locate a fault.
- Write the report in pieces: create the file with your first few findings, then append a few at a time. No single message or tool call of yours should carry more than about 1,000 words.
- One finding per entry, in this form:

      ### <where: the route and the step or heading, or the label from the prose file>
      - where: <a few words copied from the place, enough to find it>
      - problem: <one or two sentences>
      - fix: <the replacement wording written out in full, or what should change>
      - weight: must | should | could

  "must" means a reader would be misled, stopped, or would wince. "should" means plainly worse than the fix. "could" is a judgment call; use it sparingly.
- When you have finished, reply with at most twelve lines: the number of findings by weight, the three worst, and your overall verdict in one sentence. The detail belongs in the file.
