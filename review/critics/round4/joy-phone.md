# Joy, on a phone

Reader: a new visitor on a 390 x 844 touch screen (Playwright, Chromium, `isMobile`, `hasTouch`, taps only), twenty minutes, likes poems, has not read Dante. Everything below was done by tapping and typing in the site's own controls, in one visitor's state carried from script to script. Two throwaway visitors were used to see the other branches. Scripts and screenshots are in `work/joy-phone/`. No console errors, page errors or failed requests were seen on any rebuilt page.

Things I could not do as a thumb would, and did another way: the seven dropdowns of the chart of Hell were set with `selectOption` (Playwright cannot drive the phone's own picker); the selection in the reader was made by script (see part 3 of the account).

## Running account

### The opening (#/)

**The six gaps.** I wanted to do it at once. The title, two short paragraphs and the first gap are all on the first screen, and the instruction is one sentence. I wrote coral, pearls, rot, undergo, strange, knell. My words appear on the typed sheet in a blue italic hand, which is a small pleasure. Tapping "Show the writer's words" puts the poet's word beside each of mine, with my two misses struck through. What I got back was mixed. The first thing is a tally in bold, "Your word is the writer's in 4 of the 6 gaps", which is a score. Under it are six short notes, and those are the payoff: the note on the fourth gap told me that the poet's verb once meant the very word I had written, and the note on the last one, that the bell is rung every hour so nobody has to bury him, changed how I heard the end of the song. The notes are fixed, though. A second visitor who wrote "die" in the third gap was told the poet's word is "a long way from rot", a word he never wrote. It did not feel like an assignment. It dragged slightly only in that all six notes are full length even for words I had right.

**"What is the song, then?"** The two sentences before it are the best moment of the opening: the father is alive and the singer knows it. I did not expect that, and the question that follows is a real one. I said it was the truth about something else. What came back for that answer is one line, "That is where Auden went with it", which is a pat on the head. The two answers I did not give get the better replies (each grants the answer and then turns it), but they are folded away under "If you had said otherwise".

**The blackboard and the close.** The dark panel with what Auden said in class is the payoff proper, and its last sentence, marked as the site's own, lands: false about the king, true about everyone in the play. After it come three paragraphs about the course and two buttons. That is two and a half screens of reading with nothing to do, but it is the end of the sitting and I did not mind. No moment felt like a class website. Whole opening: about four minutes.

### Week 1, first meeting (#/unit/1/meet/1), reached by "Begin with Dante"

**The nine gaps.** I wanted to: a man in Hell telling how he died is a good hook, and the italic setting is five lines. The sheet is long on a phone (two full screens) and half its lines turn over, some with the gap alone on the turned line. I wrote old, unknown, stock, beasts, eager, wings, last, grief, the gods. What I got back: "3 of the 9 gaps" in bold first, then nine notes. The notes are better than the opening's. The one on the second gap (a world with nobody in it, so the voyage offers only the knowing) and the one on the last gap (a pagan has no name for the one who sank him) each showed me something I had read past. That last note was the best moment of the meeting. The sheet with my words struck through is two screens above by then and I only found it by scrolling back.

**"Is this man a hero?"** A real question and I could be interestingly wrong. I said yes. The reply took my answer seriously: Tennyson read him that way, the line went onto a polar memorial, and then "look at where Dante has put the man who says it". It did not tell me where. A second visitor who chose "Both, and Dante knows it" got only "That is nearest to what Auden said. His reading is on the board below", and there is no board below at that point.

**Marking the lines.** The riddle (an older tempter is speaking) made me want to look again at the speech, which is what a good question does. On a phone this is the step I feared, since the passage is three screens above the question. The site handles it well: "Go up to the passage" takes you there and a dark bar stays at the bottom of the screen with the button. I marked three lines, two of them the right ones. The reply ("You heard him") set the speech beside the Serpent's promise in Genesis and added that both audiences are standing at a marked limit. That second point I had not seen. No score here, and the visitor who marked the wrong lines was not scolded.

**The plate, the board, "For next time".** The Doré plate fills the screen and is a relief after so much type. The board gives Auden's reading and then the site's own third paragraph, which ends on the mountain Ulysses drowned in sight of. That is a real payoff and it made me want the book. Then "For next time: Read the Inferno and the Purgatorio", sixty-seven cantos at ten minutes each, with no word on where a reader with one evening might start. That heading is the first moment the page sounded like a class. The question to carry into the book is good. The whole meeting is nine screens and took about twelve minutes.

### The week 1 page (#/unit/1), reached by "Back to week 1"

A title, two paragraphs, the Doré dark wood, then a card for each meeting with the book and the opera between them. It reads as a table of what is here and leaves the order to me. Nothing is locked: "Begin" on the second meeting works before any reading. There are no dates and no rows to tick. Three things sounded like a class. The finished meeting is stamped "held on October 2". The second meeting's card promises seven of the damned, seven terraces and a record, and says nothing of the two written answers and four passages it also asks for, so on a train I began something twice as long as the card said. The second paragraph under the opera is catalogue matter about versions and acts where one sentence on why an opera sits beside Dante would do more.

### Week 1, second meeting (#/unit/1/meet/2)

I had not read sixty-seven cantos. I went in anyway, as the page allows.

**Your verdict.** "Before anything else: did you like it?", with Auden's five verdicts as the choices. I wanted to answer. It is the one question on the site that asks for my taste and it is disarming. I chose the verdict that calls the book good, not liked yet, expected to be. What came back: "Kept in your book. Add a line saying why, if you like." and a paragraph on Auden's own verdict. Not an assignment, no drag. On the phone the "why" field is a one-line input and my sentence of 74 characters ran out of sight as I typed.

**The chart of Hell.** "Where in Hell are they? Do it from memory first." Seven names, each with a dropdown. This is the step I least wanted to do and the one that most felt like an assignment. It is a form with no picture, it asks what I remember, and it cannot be got interestingly wrong: I know the circle or I do not. Each dropdown opens the phone's own picker, so it is fourteen taps at the least. What came back was "4 of 7 as the book has them", the right circle beside each miss, and then two paragraphs that are the real thing: the order of the circles is an argument about which sins are worst, fraud sits below violence, the traitors are in ice. Those paragraphs would be the same whatever I had chosen. The surprise was the ice.

**"No one in Dante's Hell asks to be let out. Why not?"** The question I had carried from the first meeting, and a real one. A gloss points to the end of Canto III, with no link and without the lines. The box wants at least ten words and the button stays grey until it has them, with no count: "They like it there." is refused without a word. I wrote two sentences. What came back is a board, "What Auden made of it", which opens by saying that no essay of Auden's on the *Comedy* has been found and goes round by his introduction to Kierkegaard. It is three hedged paragraphs. The one thing I will remember, Landor's remark, is in the third, and the board never returns to the crowd on the riverbank that the question began from. Then a "Your reply" box. The ten-word rule felt like an assignment and the board felt like a lecture. This is where the meeting dragged most.

**The mountain passage and the blackboard.** Fifteen lines of Virgil on love, then "Seven terraces, and three ways for love to go wrong. Which is which?" Tap a card, then tap a heading. I wanted to do this. It is a puzzle with an idea behind it, and I could be interestingly wrong: is gluttony too much love, or love of the wrong thing. Under a thumb it is the best-made control on the site. What came back: "6 of your 7 cards are where the board has them", then a line on each card ("First terrace. They hoped to rise by another's fall."), then two paragraphs: every evil is a former good, and the lovers stand nearest the good both in Hell and on the mountain. That was the moment in this meeting when I saw the design of the poem. Two small drags: ten of the passage's fifteen lines turn over at this size, and "You have read on, and you can" assumes a reader who has.

**"How can people be blamed for what they love?"** The same shape as the first written answer: ten words at least, then a board. The question is a real one and the blackboard had sharpened it. What came back is a board whose first paragraph is twenty lines long on the phone and goes through an essay on master and servant and through Caliban before it reaches Virgil's lines. The second paragraph holds the gem: animals cannot fall, and a tempter's promise is in the future tense. The third says plainly that Auden does not apply the lines to this question and that the site does, which is honest. It felt like an assignment because it is the second "write, then read three paragraphs" in a row. It dragged in the first paragraph. The future tense was the pleasure.

**The record and "What did you hear?"** A Corot plate, a paragraph on Gluck, a record card and "Play it before you go on." After six screens of type a voice is a gift, and I wanted it. One tap started the 1922 record (4 minutes 8 seconds) and the disc turned. Two faults. "I have listened" can be tapped before the record has played a second. When I tapped it the page jumped up to the heading of the step, and the new question sat below the bottom of the screen until I scrolled for it. The record went on playing while I answered and while I read the reply: same audio element, time still advancing, not paused. I said I heard something too calm for a man who has lost his wife. The reply began "Listeners in Gluck's own day said the same", gave Hanslick's remark, and then a board on what Auden said about suffering in opera, with a sentence of the site's own that sets Orpheus beside the two lovers of Canto V. This is the high point of both meetings. My ear was taken seriously, the reply answered what I had said, and the tune was still going while I read it. No assignment, no drag.

**The four passages.** "Who says it?", taken from Auden's examination of 1947. Four prose passages, each with a box and a "Check" button. I did not want to do it: I had not read the book and the step calls itself an examination. What comes back for each is a name and a citation, and at the end "3 of 4 placed." Nothing is said about any passage. This is the plainest assignment in the two meetings, and it is the last thing the meeting asks.

**The ending.** After the tally come a link to the full set of fourteen passages, "Something to make: A chart of Hell" with "Open the exercise", "For next time: Next week: Paradise", and "On to week 2". The meeting is eighteen phone screens (15,332px) and nothing at the top says so. By my estimate it is half an hour with the record, longer than my whole train ride. The first meeting ended on a board and a question to carry. This one ends on a score and housekeeping.

### 3. One canto in the reader (#/read/dante-longfellow/inf-05)

The canto is 142 lines and on this screen 121 of them turn over, because the verse is set at 18px beside a 47px gutter for line numbers. Nearly every line is a line and a stub, and the canto runs to eleven screens. I read it with pleasure all the same. Under the title a hint says "To keep a line in your book, select it."

How I made the selection: a long press could not be produced in headless Chromium (touch events sent through the browser's debugging channel gave an empty selection), so I selected part of lines 103 and 104 by script, with a Range and a `selectionchange` event on the document, as the brief allows. I could not check whether the phone's own selection menu would cover the site's bar.

The bar that appears at the foot of the screen is 195px wide on a phone. Its question wraps to five lines and its button to two. Tapping "Keep them" worked and the bar said "Kept." The "Keep them" button stayed on show beside it, though, and "See your book" was squeezed into a column 38px wide, one word to a line. At the foot of the canto "I have read this" turns into "Read on October 2 · Unmark", with links to the next and previous cantos and "Learn this canto". Nothing is given back at the end of a canto: no plate, no question, no word on what I had just read.
