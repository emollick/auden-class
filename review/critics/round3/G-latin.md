### week 2, line 8, "postero"
- problem: The sense given, "In the next day", reads in English as a time (during the next day), and the Latin form taken by itself could be exactly that. Here the word depends on credula and names the thing trusted. A student who takes it as a time gets the close of the ode wrong and cannot tell.
- fix: ['postero', 'posterus: coming after, next. The next day. Goes with credula: it is what she trusts']
- weight: should

### week 2, wordsIntro
- problem: It says each entry gives "the word", but three entries are phrases of two words (line 1, the entry that begins ne; line 4, Seu … seu; line 8, the entry that begins quam). Weeks 10 and 14 say "the word or phrase".

Everything else in the list stands. All 52 headwords are letter for letter what the site prints in that line and come in the line's order; the second quem is the only word left out. The dictionary forms are right. Verbs are given in the first person singular (quaero, do, sum), which is how a Latin dictionary lists them, and tento is the right form for an edition that prints Tentaris. The senses that turn on a form are right: the two commands not to (lines 1 and 3), dederint (line 2), erit (line 3), tribuit with both its tenses (line 4), loquimur and fugerit (line 7). The notes on which words belong together are right (lines 1, 2, 5, 7 and 8). The note at line 5, mare, says more than a dictionary would, but it is needed: without it a student would make the sea wear out the rocks. At line 6, spatio, the sense "within a span" is the reading of Macleane's own note and of Nisbet and Hubbard's commentary (1970, p. 141), who reject the other old reading, that the span is short. Conington, shown afterwards, follows that other reading, so the student will find a difference there that is no error of the list. The list follows the punctuation of the site's text, which runs lines 3 to 6 as one sentence; Nisbet and Hubbard would end the sentence with line 3, and no entry would change if it did. No entry uses a term of grammar or an abbreviation, and none hands over a line.

The sentences of fact are right. The ode is I.11 and has eight lines; they are long lines (greater Asclepiads, sixteen syllables each) and they do not rhyme. The poem itself only forbids astrology to the woman and does not say that she has gone to astrologers, but the commentaries read it as the brief does (Nisbet and Hubbard, p. 135, and Macleane's introduction to the ode). Astrology did come from Babylon, wine was strained before drinking, and the Tyrrhenian is the sea west of Italy. The title page of the source file reads Macleane, revised and edited by Reginald H. Chase, Boston, Allyn and Bacon, 1895. Conington's book was first printed in 1863, and his version of this ode has eight lines in four rhymed couplets. One thing to know, not counted as a fault: the space after line 4 is the site's, by its rule for every ode; Macleane prints the eight lines unbroken, and the sentence runs across the space.
