Read with both lenses (machine-made prose, and the student who has to use the page). Nine entries have a fault worth acting on, and each of the nine came in with the last revision. The other marked sentences are sound.

## Week 5

### week5.intro.1
- where: Auden gave Iago the first of the two
- problem: The sentence now opens with "his" while Pascal is the only person the paragraph has named, so until Auden's name arrives the lecture on *Othello* is Pascal's. The student must then count back to find what "the first of the two" is, and guess what "gave" means.
- fix: Auden said in his lecture on *Othello* (week 4) that Iago has the mathematical mind.
- weight: should

### week5.intro.2
- where: asked in his lecture on the play
- problem: Neither opening paragraph names a play. The last work named is the opera, the last lecture named is the one on *Othello*, and the week has two plays, so "the play" points at the wrong thing or at nothing.
- fix: Change the last two words to the title, so that the sentence ends: in his lecture on *The Tempest*. (The same words in the hint under the first question can stay, because the question above them names Prospero.)
- weight: should

### week5.reading.4.auden.paras.3
- where: Auden later made an English version
- problem: The paragraphs above date the essay 1962, so "later" reads as later than 1962, and the next sentence gives 1956. The word was added in the revision.
- fix: Cut "later", so that the sentence begins: Auden made an English version.
- weight: should

### week5.questions.2.auden.paras.1
- where: fought over by vices and virtues
- problem: The participle that completes "shows one figure" arrives thirteen words later, after a relative clause in two parts, and the sentence has to be read twice. Before the revision this was a clause with a verb of its own.
- fix: (In a morality play, vices and virtues fight over one figure who stands for all mankind. In the best-known example the figure is called Everyman.)
- weight: should

## Week 6

### week6.reading.7.paras.1
- where: It stops where the statue of the Commandant
- problem: Two sentences earlier "It" means the opera, so the student first reads that the opera stops there, which the next sentence denies. The two "he"s have the Commandant between them, so the second can be taken for the man who was killed, and the split has also left three short sentences in a row.
- fix: Make the two sentences one again, and name the subject: In the English printed here the hero is called Don Juan, and the text stops where he is dragged down to hell, after the statue of the Commandant, whom he killed in the first scene, has come to supper.
- weight: should

### week6.reading.7.auden.paras.1
- where: He calls the Don’s pleasure
- problem: The sentence before ends with Leporello speaking of his master, so "He" reads as Leporello and the quoted word as Leporello's. The next sentence then names Auden afresh, which confirms the wrong reading.
- fix: Begin the sentence: Auden calls the Don’s pleasure [quotation kept]. Begin the next one: He therefore wants the Don to look. Leave the rest of both sentences as it stands.
- weight: must

### week6.questions.1.auden.paras.3
- where: His example is God’s command to Abraham
- problem: "His" follows a sentence about an apostle and God, and with its capital at the head of the sentence it looks like God's. Two sentences on, "in his words" stands beside "An apostle" and reads as the apostle's.
- fix: Begin the fourth sentence: Auden’s example is God’s command to Abraham. In the fifth, write "the essay says" for "Auden says", so that the name does not come three times running. Begin the last: An apostle, in Auden’s words, has to say, [quotation kept]
- weight: should

### week6.questions.3.auden.paras.3
- where: in the same book about the Elizabethan stage
- problem: In this order the words say that the book is about the Elizabethan stage. In the fourth sentence "get round" is British, and the site is meant to be in American English.
- fix: In the first sentence write: an essay about the Elizabethan stage in the same book. In the fourth write: try to get around the difficulty.
- weight: should

### week6.questions.4.auden.paras.3
- where: and it credits Blake with having seen this
- problem: The subject of the sentence is "Dualism" and the nearest "it" means the creation, so "it credits" has no owner that could credit anyone. The note, which is meant, stands only in the aside.
- fix: End the sentence: and Auden credits Blake with having seen this.
- weight: should
