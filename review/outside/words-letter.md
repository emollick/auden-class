## Reasons
The drafts keep the house rules: no dash inside a sentence, no question, no "we", no stock word ("journey" is
meant plainly). Three things read as habit and not as help: "shorten the task" (a second name for one thing),
"and it is Auden’s own" (a name held back), and "differ in two more things" (a sentence that only announces).

### 3.1
- The join holds. "He did not telephone." closes Pearce’s telling, a second account follows, and the first has
  to be named. The page already says "In Miller’s account". Two dates are reported and nothing is argued. The
  paragraph on the third canto still follows: it names Pearce at once, and the order of time holds.
- "had only to learn" is clear. "he wrote" gives the "only" to Auden, and the shock of the class confirms it.
- "ask for less" is clear and is the site’s idiom ("the work he asked for", "Miller has six asked for"). It is
  better than "shorten the task". Two names for one promise, two sentences apart, read as variation for its
  own sake, and a reader may count two promises. The class page uses "task" nowhere.
- The three "where" clauses are easy to follow, in the manner of the caveat on Friday evenings ("Miller’s book
  has beer brought by the students, and his memoir coffee only"). "he" is Pearce by the parallel, but every
  "he" before it in the paragraph is Auden, and the train and the drive are his. The break cures that.
- "seven cut to five". This sentence sets the letter’s six against Pearce’s seven, 3.7 sets it against his
  five, and the caveat has "seven assigned in December and cut to five". The added words are the page’s own.
- The last sentence is needed and plain. Without it a reader may take six for what was asked at the
  examination. The page says elsewhere what a source leaves out ("He records no word of explanation").
- Length. The scene’s paragraphs have 102, 69, 41, 80, 59 and 67 words. The new one has 149, and only one in
  the scenes is longer (177, on Kierkegaard). Broken after the page reference it gives 100 and 52. The report
  of the letter ends there. Of the 19 paragraphs in the scenes that cite a page, 16 end on the citation.
- Two small things, here and in 3.6. "would try on the train" can be read for a moment as "try on", and "and
  on the train from Chicago to Los Angeles he would try" avoids it. The caveat on dates has the departure as
  a plan ("says that he was leaving at noon"). "the day he was to leave" would match it. For the critic of facts.

### 3.3
- The caveat on dates, five above this one, cites another letter of Auden’s from the same morning, to the
  Sterns. "Auden’s letter of December 19, 1941" can be taken for that one. 3.2, 3.4 and the next caveat name her.
- "and fewer if he could not learn them himself" leaves the reader to supply "would be asked", where Miller’s
  sentence beside it has "asked for" and "allowed". It also says "fewer" where 3.1, 3.6 and 3.7 say "less".
  "fewer" claims more. `facts-letter.md` (item 7, so far) finds no amount given in the letter, so "less" is safer.
- In the last sentence "says set" puts two verbs side by side and trips the eye.
- The new caveat is plain. Its first and third sentences are in the words of the caveat on Pearce’s pages,
  which is right, and they tell a reader who has never searched a scanned book what was read and what was
  not. If the phrase is ever made plainer, both caveats should change together.

### 3.5
It matches "The order of the books", "The books", "The records" and "The type", which take the article. No
other heading has a colon, and a count of accounts has to be kept up. Without the article it is a catalog label.

### 3.6 and 3.7
- The same account in the same words is right. Either page may be read alone, the site already tells Pearce’s
  account on both, and for a letter known only from extracts one wording is safer than two. The about page
  differs where its job differs: it says where the letter is printed and how it was read. Do not shorten it.
- "One account was written at the time, and it is Auden’s own" holds the name back for effect, and it counts
  the accounts as the old heading did. The paragraphs beside it open with the witness.
- "two scanned copies returns" looks like a slip of grammar. The verb belongs to "a search", five words back.
- 3.7, first sentence. "Auden’s letter, and those of two people who were in the class, Pearce and Chapman" can
  be read for a moment as a list of four.
- "The letter and Pearce differ in two more things" only announces, which the house style rules out, so the
  end reads as a list under a label. "in two more things" is not natural. The site says "differ on" (the
  table, the note under the cantos) and "disagree on" (the caveat). The sentence proposed names the two points.
- "will go" and "will drive" put a future tense into a statement about two texts, and "In Pearce he will
  drive" is hard at first reading, since "he" is Auden. "In Miller’s account" is the class page’s form.
- "by Auden in December" can stay. It marks the figure as one given before the examination.

### 3.2, 3.4, 3.8 and 3.9
- 3.2 and 3.8 keep the form of their lines. Each is the one item in its line without pages, which is sound,
  since the text beside each gives the book and the pages.
- 3.4 has the form of Pearce’s entry: the piece, "in", the title in asterisks, "edited by", the year, the pages.
- 3.9. No entry in "Works cited" begins with an editor. The list runs in three parts: witnesses and scholars
  by surname, then Auden’s own writings (the Stern letters, then his books by date), then three works of
  reference. The one other letter of Auden’s stands under "W. H. Auden, letters to", with its pages last. Not
  wording, but it bears on the entry: `build/shelf.mjs` (lines 73 to 76) reports any entry with no row in
  `citedBy`, so a row is needed, such as `['W. H. Auden, letter to Ursula Niebuhr', ['Remembering Reinhold Niebuhr']]`.
- In passing, not wording: the item on the cantos under "Papers and exercises" (`content/class.mjs`, line 572)
  cites only Pearce for "Written out at the examination". The letter now says so too.

### "his friend Ursula Niebuhr"
Enough, and not a word too many. People on these pages get a short tag at first mention ("who shared Auden’s
house that fall"). "his friend" tells the reader what kind of letter this is. A longer tag would add a fact
that is not in the draft. The bare name is right in the lines of sources.
