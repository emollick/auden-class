- Headword: valor, valour, a noun.
- Etymology, with the dictionary's signs read by its own key. The English word is from Middle English valour, from Old French valour or valur, later valeur. French valeur is set down as cognate with Spanish and Portuguese valor and with Italian valore. All are derived from Medieval Latin valor (strength, valor; in Late Latin value, worth), and that from Latin valere, to be strong, to be worth. So the entry does set the English word beside Italian valore, and it takes both from one Latin word.

- Sense 2: "Value; worth." The number carries a dagger. Four examples (the *Romaunt of the Rose*, Torkington, Peele, Ben Jonson), none dated.
- Sense 3: a man of courage, marked rare. One example.
- The key. The same part has it on the inside of its back cover (leaf n290): the dagger is to be read as obsolete, the sign of equality as cognate with, the open angle as derived from. ### 2d. The note, the new ground, the "stands" sentence and the sources lines

- "the English word of the same root": holds. Both dictionaries bring English valour and Italian valore from the one Latin noun valor. To be exact, the English word came through Old French, which is the sister of the Italian. The note does not say otherwise.
- "which now means courage": holds. It is the one sense of general use left unmarked in both dictionaries (Century 1; NED 1c). For the present day, the Merriam-Webster page the researchers kept gives only personal bravery.
- "and once also meant worth": holds. Century 2; NED 1a, 1b and 2, all marked obsolete. The NED's dates show worth as the earlier sense (from about 1330) and courage as the later (from 1581), and the two together for about sixty years.
- Does the note claim more than the dictionaries support? No.
- Does it say or imply what Longfellow meant? It does not say. It no longer hints that he kept the look and changed the sense. Set after "worth or power", the words "once also meant worth" will lead some readers to think the old sense is the one in play. No source settles that, and the note does not assert it. One thing "once" leaves unsaid is when: by these two dictionaries the sense of worth was dead long before 1867 (last examples 1586 to 1642). The ground says "marked obsolete", which covers it.
- The new ground. "From the same Latin word as valore": holds (both etymologies). "Its living sense is courage": holds; the NED also leaves unmarked a name or title (1d) and a rare sense of quantity (4), neither of which is a rival meaning. "An older sense, worth or value, is marked obsolete": holds, and "older" is right by the NED's dates. The senses cited are the right ones: Century 1 and 2; NED 1a, 1c and 2. "With no source": true, the tab's six grounds cite nothing on the English word. "An outside reader pointed out the older sense": I know this only from the researchers' report of the review.
- "*Valour* in English: the *Century Dictionary* and the *New English Dictionary*": right.

- Is it true to pp. 19 and 21, as a short statement? Yes. On p. 21 (scan lines 1032 to 1033) Auden names the figure as the sentence names him, calls hybris his first sin, and says it is to believe oneself godlike. On p. 19 hybris is strictly the self-confidence, and the belief is what comes of it. The sentence follows the form of p. 21, where hybris is the believing.
