## 1. Hubris (Horace, second passage, the tab "Word")

**The review.** The caption called hubris "a Greek word that first meant
presumption toward the gods". The review called that too narrow, citing
Aristotle's *Rhetoric* and a modern study, and offered a replacement that
ends by saying Auden applies the word to presumptuous defiance of limits
set by the gods. It also asked for the ground under the sentence to be
changed.

**At the sources.** The review is right about the history. The caption
had followed the Online Etymology Dictionary. Liddell and Scott's Greek
lexicon has no such sense, and in Homer and Hesiod the word is used of
outrage done to other people. The end of the review's replacement does
not hold: where Auden uses the word of this stanza (*The Enchafèd Flood*,
p. 9) he names no god and no limit, and he does not explain the word
there. He explains it in "The Greeks and Us" (1948; *Forewords and
Afterwords*, pp. 19 and 21), where he spells it hybris: in Greek tragedy
it is the hero's confidence that he is a god, out of reach of suffering,
and only the unusually fortunate are tempted to it.

**Changed.** The history is cut and Auden's own account stands in its
place. The site's rule is a point of view, ideally Auden's, without a
layer of critics, so Aristotle and the modern study were not brought in.

- Was: "Auden, quoting the stanza, calls the attempt to cross the sea
  rash and close to hubris, a Greek word that first meant presumption
  toward the gods."
- Now: "Auden, quoting the stanza, calls the attempt to cross the sea
  rash and close to hubris. Elsewhere he explains hubris as the Greek
  tragic hero’s belief that he is a god."

Ground 6 now reports the essay and says why the old gloss went. The
"stands" line and the list of sources name *Forewords and Afterwords*,
pp. 19 and 21. The Online Etymology Dictionary is no longer cited for
hubris.

## 2. Longfellow's *valour* (Purgatorio, first passage, the tab "English")

**The review.** The note on *valore* said that Longfellow "keeps the look
of the word, *valour*, which in English means courage". The review
answered that the Century Dictionary also gives the sense of value or
worth, and offered a replacement saying that the word "can also carry"
that older sense, though modern readers are more likely to take it for
courage.

**At the sources.** The review is right that courage alone was too
little. The old note also spoke of the look of the word, when *valour*
and *valore* are from the same Latin word. The Century Dictionary (1889–91)
gives bravery as sense 1 and value or worth as sense 2, with the dagger
that its key reads as obsolete. *A New English Dictionary* (vol. X, part
2, 1928) marks the senses of worth obsolete and courage as the living
one. So "can also carry" does not hold: both dictionaries had marked the
older sense obsolete. The remark about modern readers has no source, so
the note speaks of the word and not of readers. The note does not say
what Longfellow meant, and his own notes do not comment on the word.

**Changed.**

- Was: "*Valore* is worth or power, and here a name for God. Okey writes
  *goodness*. Longfellow keeps the look of the word, *valour*, which in
  English means courage."
- Now: "*Valore* is worth or power, and here a name for God. Okey writes
  *goodness*. Longfellow keeps the English word from the same Latin root,
  *valour*, which now means courage and once meant worth."

A new ground cites the two dictionaries. It also records that the Boston
edition of 1867 spells Longfellow's word *valor*, and that the London
edition of the same year and the hosted text have *valour*. The "stands"
line and the list of sources name both dictionaries.

## 3. Auden's letter of December 19, 1941 (the class page and the about page)

**The review.** Auden wrote of the Dante assignment at the time, in a
letter to Ursula Niebuhr, which the review knew through Arthur Kirsch's
*Auden and Christianity*: six cantos, and a possible reduction if he
could not do the assignment himself on a train from Chicago to Los
Angeles. The review asked for that account on the class page's scene
"The examination" and in the about page's section on the accounts, and
suggested a new heading for the section.

**At the sources.** The review holds. The letter is printed in
*Remembering Reinhold Niebuhr* (1991), pp. 281–82, and the class passage
is on p. 282. It could be read only in the short extracts that a search
of the scanned book returns. Two scanned copies were searched and their
texts agree word for word. The pages themselves were not seen, and both
pages of the site now say so. What the letter says is set out in
`facts-letter.md`, in the critic's own words.

**Changed on the class page** (`content/class.mjs`).

- The scene "The examination" has two new paragraphs after Pearce's
  account. The first gives Auden's own account from the letter. The
  second sets it beside Pearce's: six cantos against seven cut to five, a
  train against a drive, a promise to ask for less against a promise to
  cancel the assignment. It ends by saying that the letter, written
  before the journey, does not say what was asked in the end.
- The scene's line of sources names the letter. So does the line of
  sources of "Papers and exercises", where the cantos now cite the letter
  for what was set and Pearce for what was written out.
- "Where this page may be wrong": the caveat on the cantos now gives the
  letter's number first, and a new caveat says how the letter was read.
- The list of sources has an entry for the letter.

**Changed on the about page** (`content/about.mjs`).

- The section is headed "The accounts of the examination" (was "The
  examination: five accounts"). The review suggested the heading without
  its article. The article is kept because the page's other headings have
  one.
- A new first paragraph gives the letter's account and says how it was
  read.
- The summing-up now says that the letter tells what was set, and that
  the two people who were in the class tell what was done. It gives the
  letter's number with the others, and says how the letter and Pearce
  differ on the journey and on the promise.
- The table's row on the cantos and the works cited name the letter.

**Found in the checking, and changed with it.** Two sentences that
already stood on the pages said that Auden promised to call the
examination off (class page) or to cancel the examination (about page).
Pearce's word for what would be cancelled is the assignment. Both
sentences now say "cancel the assignment", since the new paragraphs
compare that promise with the letter's.

**Not taken.**

- Kirsch's book: the site cites the letter as printed.
- Miller's report that Auden had first proposed driving. It would be a
  step toward reconciling the two accounts, which the site does not
  attempt.
- Any statement of which account is right.
- The three rows of dots in the printed letter. It could not be settled
  that they are cuts, so a sentence that said so was taken out before the
  correction went live.

## How the corrections were checked

- Each claim of the review was looked up at its source before a word was
  drafted (`hubris.md`, `valour.md`, `letter.md`).
- The drafts (`drafts.md`) went to a critic of words and a critic of
  facts for each correction. The wording that came of their reports is
  `final.md`. A second critic of words read all of it
  (`words-final.md`), and the critics of facts read what was new in it.
- Closeness to the books in copyright: the site's sweeps for shared runs
  of words were run again on the built pages, and
  `review/outside/tools/close.py` compared the letter's paragraphs with
  the extracts of the letter and with Pearce's memoir. No run of five or
  more words is shared at the places reported. A few common phrases of
  five words turn up elsewhere in the scans and stand.
- The checker passes both readings: Horace with no warnings, the
  Purgatorio with the three it had before.
- The site's tests pass. A walk of every page reported nothing, and only
  the lines of the changed pages differ from the walk before. With every
  tab pressed at four phone widths, no page is wider than its window. The
  changed passages were looked at in pictures at 1280 and 390 px.

## Waiting for the owner's word

- On the same tab as the note on *valore*, the note on *lucido* still
  says "Longfellow keeps the look-alike word, *lucid*." *Lucid* is from
  the same Latin root, and shining or bright is its first sense in the
  dictionaries, so "look-alike" can be read as alike in looks only. The
  mend that claims nothing new: "*Bright* for *lucido*, where Longfellow
  keeps *lucid*." The review did not ask for it, so it was put to the
  owner and left as it is.
