## Reasons

### The Horace sentence (question 1)

- All three read at one pass. After the first sentence each gives the word, then "as", then whose belief it is, then what the belief is. With none of them does a reader have to go back.
- Best in its place: (a). (c) is the easiest by a little, since every word in it is common and no figure has to be known, but it does not say where the word belongs. I am told it was set aside for a reason of fact, which is not mine to weigh.
- (b) is the weakest. "The tragic hero" with nothing before it sends an English reader to Shakespeare as soon as to the Greeks, and of that wider figure the sentence is a large claim. Nothing else prepares it: the words "tragic" and "tragedy" occur nowhere else in the reading (I searched and printed only the places).
- (a) costs one word more than (b), and the word answers the question (b) leaves open: which hero. It also lets a reader gather that hubris is a Greek word. It does not say so, as the old caption did. It says the belief is a Greek hero's, and the rest is an easy guess.
- The phrase before the noun is not too heavy. It is the heaviest possessive in the caption: four words, where the only other is a pair of names. But "Greek tragic hero" is one familiar name for one figure, its words stand in the usual order, and each narrows the one before, so a reader holds nothing open. "Belief" follows at once. After a caption full of glosses "the Greek" may make a reader expect "word" for a moment, and "tragic hero’s" settles that within two words.
- The lighter forms cost words the caption does not have. "The belief of the hero of Greek tragedy that he is a god" makes the sentence three words longer and has "of" twice. "A Greek tragic hero’s" weighs the same and turns the type into one man. The plural ("the belief of Greek tragic heroes that they are gods", which also gives 15 words) is no lighter and loses "he is a god".
- (a) is the one whose words can all be found in its ground: Greek, tragedy, hero, a god. That is the test the earlier critic used when "a god" replaced "godlike".
- Nothing in (a) is unclear. "He" is first Auden and then the hero, but the second stands right after "hero’s", and it is so in all three. Nothing is stiff beyond the possessive. Nothing sounds machine-made: there is no stock word and no figure of speech, and the checker's word patterns find nothing in the caption with any of the three.
- The caption's longest sentence was the old last one, at 26 words. With the two new ones no sentence has more than 19.
- One caution. With (a) the caption stands at exactly 120 words, and the checker warns above 120. Any later addition must be paid for with a cut. (b) would leave one word to spare, which is not reason enough to prefer it.

### Ground 6 (questions 2 and 3)

- "Unusually" is plain, and before "fortunate" it can only be a word of degree. It is stronger than "very" and no stiffer. "The unusually fortunate" is formed as "the very rich" is.
- The caption as it is now to be has no "fortunate" (the earlier critic's wording had it), so the last clause of the claim stands under nothing that is printed. It does no harm in a working note, and it keeps the rest of the account for whoever opens the caption next.
- The source is word for word what the earlier critic proposed. A later worker learns from it what the caption used to say, on whose word, why that would not do, and what was done. It also tells that worker why the claim spells the word "hybris" where the caption has "hubris". The citation has the form of ground 4 of the "English" tab, which cites the same essay, and the note opens as the earlier critic asked: who made the change, then the date in figures.

### The Purgatorio ground's source (question 3)

- Up to "as well" it is the earlier critic's wording, and it keeps the form of its neighbors: hyphens in the ranges of years, headwords in lower case, "the scans at archive.org" as ground 5 has "the scan at archive.org", no period at the end.
- The new clause is clear as a statement, and "the hosted text" is the neighbors' own term (ground 4). What it does not say is what follows from it. The first two parts of the parenthesis tell what the note said and what it now gives. The third reports two spellings and stops. A later worker cannot tell whether the matter is still open or was seen and left. "Which the note follows" tells them. It is the standard the earlier critic set for this same note of change, that it should say what was done.
- Two lesser points, for the lead to weigh. The clause does not say where the first edition was read, as ground 5 and the words just before the parenthesis do for their books. The report of the critic of facts names a scan at archive.org; if that is wanted here, "p. 94, in the scan at archive.org," fits. And the clause sits under the dictionaries, though it is about Longfellow's words, which are ground 4. A worker checking Longfellow will look there first. It can stay where it is if ground 4 is not to be opened.

### The two phrases (question 5)

- The page does not print the five notes in the order of the file. It lists them in the order their words stand in the Italian (site/js/kit/lens-compare.js, the function inPlace; I read the code and did not open the page). By the row numbers of the ties the order is: *lucido* first, *carità* second, *valore* third, then the two notes on verbs. So "the look-alike word" is read first, the note on *charity* stands between, and "the English word from the same Latin root" comes third.
- The answer in three sentences. Read in that order, the three notes say "keeps" three times, and most readers will take them for one habit of Longfellow's told three ways, the third most fully. A careful reader may look back from the third note and suppose that *lucid* only looks like *lucido* and is no kin to it, which by the earlier critic's report is not so, though neither note says anything untrue. That is a small fault, it was the *lucido* note's before this correction, and it can wait for the owner's word: it should not hold the correction back.
- The order on the page makes the fault smaller than the earlier critic took it to be, since that report read the *valore* note as standing just before the other. When the *lucido* note is opened, the form of the note on *charity* would mend it with no new claim: "*Bright* for *lucido*, where Longfellow keeps *lucid*."

### Reasons

- (a) It reads as plainly as "the day he left" and claims only a plan. It matches the class page's caveat on dates, where the letter to the Sterns "says that he was leaving at noon". "Was to leave" can hint at a journey not made, and "written before the journey", which follows on both pages, takes that doubt away.
- (b) "Memorize" is plain and American, and both pages have the word already: "the memorizing" twice in the scene, and "memorize" in the about page's sentence on Nolan. The sentence is two words shorter and reads at one pass. "Write them out" is the scene's own phrase, "cantos of Dante" is the about page's ("cantos of Dante learned by heart"), and "only" is still given to Auden by "he wrote".
- (b) Two sentences on, his own attempt is still "learn it all", as Pearce's bargain just above has "learn the five himself". A reader takes "memorize" and "learn" for one act there. Within the lead's two limits I found no better form: the others are longer or stiffer.
- (c) The new order cannot be read as "try on". It ends the account on "he would try", and "try" has its object earlier in the same sentence.
- (d) Class page. The scene has spoken of nothing but the cantos, "the five" stands in the same sentence, and "assignment" is already the page's word for them: the scene on the first class has "the first assignment was given that afternoon too: six cantos". The second use repeats the first and is set against "ask for less".
- (d) About page. It is as clear there, and by 3.7 the phrase has been met once. One small thing: the sentence before the one in 3.6b has "the task" for the same thing. A reader will take the two for one. If one name is wanted, "called the assignment impossible" gives it, and that is for the critic of facts to pass.
- (d) The phrase does not say whether some other examination would have followed. By facts-letter.md that is as far as the witness goes, so the openness is right.
- (e) Three things slow a reader. After "as Auden’s own letter says", the words "and two people who were in the class" read at first as a second authority for what was set, until "say that" shows that a new statement has begun. "They" follows "Pearce and Chapman, say that", and a reader tries the two people first. And the sentence has 35 words, the most in the paragraph, where the one on the page now has 22.
- (e) There is also a change of voice. "As … says" gives the first half as the site's own finding, where every other account in the section is reported, and the list of things left out on purpose has "any statement of which account is right". In the wording above both halves report: the letter says what was set, and the two students say what was done. The second sentence is the one on the page now, without its "So". "Set for" is already the about page's phrase ("questions set for the next meeting"). If the site does mean to state the first half as settled, the same repair serves: a period after "says", and "the cantos" for "they".
- (f) The caveat loses nothing with its fourth sentence. Its first and last sentences are the opening pair of the caveat on Pearce's pages, with the two copies between them, and it now ends on the limit. In 3.6 "The letter is printed in …" becomes a short sentence, and the "It" that opens the next can only be the letter. Nothing printed in the section still speaks of words left out.
- 3.2b. Three statements, each in the list's form: what, a colon, who and where, with periods between and none at the end. The letter has no page, as in 3.2 and 3.8, and the item links to the scene, which gives the book and the pages. "Set for the final examination" is the phrase of 3.7, so the two pages say it one way.
- 3.2b, "there". Each statement is a claim and then its source, so "there" has to reach back over a source to the claim before it. The nearest thing it can point to is the letter, and "written out there" right after a letter reads for a moment as written out in the letter. The words on the page now, "Written out at the examination", stand alone as every other statement in the list does. The cost is "examination" twice and two words more (32).
- The rest. Compared by script: the caveat on the cantos and 3.9 are word for word the earlier critic's; 3.2, 3.4, 3.5 and 3.8 are as drafted and passed; 3.1, 3.6 and 3.7 differ from that critic's wording only in the changes listed.
