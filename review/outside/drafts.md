## 1. Horace, second passage ("The crossing"), tab "Word"

File: `remake/works/horace/reading.json`, `plates[1].lenses[1]`.

### The caption's last sentence

Now:

> Auden, quoting the stanza, calls the attempt to cross the sea rash and close to hubris, a Greek word that first meant presumption toward the gods.

Proposed:

> Auden, quoting the stanza, calls the attempt to cross the sea rash and close to hubris: in his account of Greek tragedy, the fortunate hero’s belief that he is godlike.

The rest of the caption stays as it is. With the new sentence the caption has 120 words; the checker warns above 120.

A shorter form, if the critics prefer it:

> Auden, quoting the stanza, calls the attempt to cross the sea rash and close to hubris, which he explains elsewhere as a fortunate man’s belief that he is godlike.

### Ground 6 (kind "source" now; kind "auden" proposed)

Now: claim "hubris is from the Greek for wanton violence, insolence, outrage, originally presumption toward the gods"; source "Online Etymology Dictionary under hubris, read on 2026-10-03".

Proposed claim:

> in Greek tragedy the sin the gods hold against the hero is hybris: a confidence so great that he takes himself for a god, out of reach of suffering; only the very fortunate are tempted to it

Proposed source:

> “The Greeks and Us” (1948), in Forewords and Afterwords (1973), pp. 19 and 21; Auden spells the word hybris there. ## 3. Auden's letter of December 19, 1941, on the Dante examination

The research: `review/outside/letter.md` (its findings 1 to 3 are finished; 4 and 5 are notes).

### 3.1 The class page, the scene "The examination": a new paragraph

It goes third, after the paragraph that ends "He did not telephone." and before the one that begins
"The third canto of the *Inferno* took Pearce".

> That is Pearce’s account, written more than forty years later. Auden gave his own on the morning of December 19, 1941, the day he left for California, in a letter to his friend Ursula Niebuhr. For the final examination, he wrote, his class had only to learn six cantos of Dante by heart and write them out. The class was shocked and thought it could not be done. He had promised to ask for less if he could not learn it all himself, and he would try on the train from Chicago to Los Angeles (*Remembering Reinhold Niebuhr*, pp. 281–82). The letter has six cantos where Pearce has seven, a train where he has a drive, and a promise to shorten the task where he has a promise to call the examination off. It was written before the journey and does not say what was asked in the end.

### 3.3 The class page, "Where this page may be wrong": the caveat on the cantos

Now:

> The accounts of the cantos disagree on their number and on when they were assigned. Miller has six asked for at the first class, as an exercise, and four allowed to anyone who could not manage six (pp. 26–27). Farnan, who was not in the class, also has six, assigned on the first day (pp. 73 and 76). Nolan has each student choose six, in place of an examination (p. 53). Proposed (one sentence added second, and the last sentence changed):

Auden’s letter of December 19, 1941, has six, for the final examination, and fewer if he could not learn them himself (*Remembering Reinhold Niebuhr*, pp. 281–82). Miller has six asked for at the first class, as an exercise, and four allowed to anyone who could not manage six (pp. 26–27). Farnan, who was not in the class, also has six, assigned on the first day (pp. 73 and 76). Nolan has each student choose six, in place of an examination (p. 53). Pearce has seven assigned in December and cut to five (pp. 153–54). Chapman’s classmates spoke of eight (p. 509). The account of the examination on this page is Pearce’s, with what Auden’s letter says set beside it.

### 3.6 The about page: a new first paragraph of that section

> One account was written at the time, and it is Auden’s own. On the morning of December 19, 1941, the day he left Ann Arbor for the Christmas vacation, he wrote to his friend Ursula Niebuhr. Proposed:
