# The gloss on *hubris* (Horace piece, second passage, tab "Word")

Checked on 2026-10-05 for the outside reader's review. Status: complete. Two researchers: the first wrote findings 1 to 3(c), the second the rest.

## Summary

1. Holds: the reviewer is right about the clause. "A Greek word that first meant presumption toward the gods" is wrong as a history of the Greek word. In Homer and Hesiod, in the standard lexicon and in the *Oxford Classical Dictionary*, hybris is insolent or violent conduct that dishonors other people, and the dictionary says the word is not at root religious.
2. Holds: the reviewer's account of Aristotle and of Fisher. The Online Etymology Dictionary does say "originally", but it names no authority and the lexicon contradicts it; the caption also dropped the three violent senses that the site's own ground repeats.
3. Does not hold: the last clause of the reviewer's replacement. Auden's sentence (*The Enchafèd Flood*, p. 9) names no god and no limit and says only that the rashness comes near hubris. The limit set by a god is in the Horace stanza he quotes.
4. What the site can say: Auden's own sense, which he printed in 1948 ("The Greeks and Us", *Forewords and Afterwords*, pp. 19 and 21): the fortunate hero's belief that he is like a god and cannot suffer. Or the caption can end at "rash and close to hubris." Three wordings are in finding 6.
5. Could not be checked: Fisher's 1992 book and MacDowell's 1976 article were not opened (known through the publishers' extracts and notes, Fisher's own summaries, and Cairns's article, which was read whole). The *Oxford Classical Dictionary* entry was read only in search lines of a lending scan. The Oxford English Dictionary sits behind a login, so where the etymology site got "originally" is not known.

## 2. The Online Etymology Dictionary does say it

Holds. I fetched https://www.etymonline.com/word/hubris on 2026-10-05 (the page answered, and I searched its source for the phrase). The entry dates the English word to 1884, calls it a back-formation from *hubristic* or else a borrowing of Greek *hybris*, glosses the Greek word with the three terms the site's ground repeats (wanton violence, insolence, outrage), and then adds: "originally 'presumption toward the gods'" (quotation 1 of 3). It goes on to a guess at the word's parts (a first element meaning up or out; the second debated) and notes the spelling *hybris* in English translations of Nietzsche about 1911. It names no lexicon or scholar for the "originally" clause.

So the ground reports its source correctly. The caption then goes one step past the source: the dictionary puts the three violent senses first and the clause about the gods second, while the caption keeps only the second and calls it what the Greek word "first meant".

## 3. The Greek word

(a) Liddell, Scott and Jones, *A Greek-English Lexicon* (9th edition, 1940), under ὕβρις. Perseus was down when I tried (three addresses, error 503 each time), so I read the same entry through Logeion at the University of Chicago (https://logeion.uchicago.edu/ὕβρις, data read on 2026-10-05). The senses in order: A.I "wanton violence, arising from the pride of strength or from passion, insolence" (quotation 2 of 3), said to be frequent in the *Odyssey*, mostly of the suitors; I.2 lust; I.3 violence in animals; II an outrage, a concrete act (the two *Iliad* places are cited here), with II.2 an outrage on the person, especially rape, and II.3 the term of Athenian law for the more serious injuries to the person; III a loss at sea (Pindar, Acts 27.21); B a violent, overbearing man (Hesiod). The entry has no sense "presumption toward the gods". The words *god*, *divine* and *presumption* do not occur in it (counted by script). The same page of Logeion shows Cunliffe's *Lexicon of the Homeric Dialect* (1924), whose whole definition is about disregard of decency and of the rights or feelings of others, and an overbearing or violent spirit.

(b) The earliest uses. I listed every form of the word family in the Perseus Digital Library's own text files (PerseusDL/canonical-greekLit on GitHub, files tlg0012.tlg001, tlg0012.tlg002, tlg0020.tlg001 and tlg0020.tlg002, read 2026-10-05) and read the Greek around each.

- *Iliad*: the noun twice, 1.203 and 1.214, both of Agamemnon's treatment of Achilles; Athena is the one who comes to see it and promises amends. The verb at 9.368 (Agamemnon again) and 11.695 (the Epeians against Nestor's people); the agent noun at 13.633 (Menelaus, of the Trojans who wronged their host).
- *Odyssey*: the noun fifteen times. Eleven are of the suitors (1.368, 4.321, 4.627, 15.329, 16.86, 16.410, 16.418, 17.169, 17.565, 23.64, 24.352) and one of overbearing men, who are again the suitors (17.581); two are of raiders who plunder Egyptian farms (14.262 and 17.431). At 17.485 to 487 gods go about disguised as strangers and watch for it among men. At 23.63 to 66 a god is thought to have punished it, and what it consisted in is stated: the suitors honored no human being who came to them.
- Hesiod, *Works and Days*: 134 to 136, the silver race could not keep it from one another, and their neglect of the gods is listed after it as a separate fault; 146 (the bronze race); 191; 213 to 218, where it is the opposite of justice and Perses is told to shun it; 238 to 239, where Zeus punishes it. *Theogony* 307, 514 and 996 use the agent noun of Typhaon, Menoetius and Pelias.

In these texts the conduct is always directed at other people (a comrade, a host, a household, strangers, neighbors, one another). The gods are the witnesses and punishers of it, not its target.

(c) Aristotle, *Rhetoric* 2.2.5 to 6 (1378b23 to 30), read in Greek and in J. H. Freese's Loeb translation (1926), both in the Perseus repository (tlg0086.tlg038). Hybris is one of three kinds of slighting. It consists in doing or saying things that bring shame on the person who suffers them, for no gain beyond the doing, for pleasure. The pleasure comes from the thought that by ill-treating others one shows oneself their superior, which is why the young and the rich are given to it. Dishonor belongs to it, and his example is Achilles on Agamemnon. Hitting back is not hybris but revenge. No god is mentioned. The reviewer's summary of this passage is accurate.

(A second researcher took over at this point on 2026-10-05. Findings 1 to 3(c) above are the first researcher's and are left as written. The last sentence of 3(a) was not cut short: I checked it against Logeion's data for the entry, and Cunliffe's definition is as described.)

(d) The *Oxford Classical Dictionary*, entry "hubris". The publisher's own pages (oxfordre.com and oxfordreference.com) refused my requests with error 403, and I did not try those hosts again. I read the entry's main statements in the Internet Archive's lending scan of the third edition revised (Oxford University Press, 2003; item oxfordclassicald0000unse_w0u7), p. 732 by the page number that shows in one search line, through the archive's text search limited to that one book. The search shows a line of print around each phrase asked for. What follows is what those lines say (about twenty of them). It is not a reading of the whole entry, and I did not see the initials that sign it; its closing note sends the reader to N. R. E. Fisher's *Hybris* (1992) for the views it sums up.

- It defines hubris as conduct meant to dishonor someone, and says the word carried strong moral blame in Greece and named a serious crime at Athens.
- It says that English writers commonly use the word for pride, for too much confidence, or for whatever might anger the gods; that scholars now mostly agree this comes from misreading the Greek sources; and that an over-simple picture of Greek religion has gone with it and propped up doubtful readings, of tragedy above all.
- It ranks Aristotle's account in the *Rhetoric* (1378b23 to 30) above every other ancient treatment, and says that what the word names most often is violence done as an insult.
- It says: "Hubris is not essentially a religious term" (quotation 3 of 3). Gods come into it as punishers: sometimes because the insult was to themselves, more commonly because they were thought to stand behind the ordinary rules of Greek life.
- It warns against taking Greek tragedy to be mainly about heroes whom the gods strike down for hubris.

Fisher's book, *Hybris: A Study in the Values of Honour and Shame in Ancient Greece* (Aris and Phillips, Warminster, 1992). I could not open the book itself: the Internet Archive's catalog has no copy and Google Books offers no preview of its pages. I read five reports of it, two of them Fisher's own.

- The publisher's description on the Google Books record (books.google.com/books?vid=ISBN085668144X, read 2026-10-05): the book follows the word through literature and law from Homer down to Aristotle. Its finding is that hybris at its core means shaming and dishonoring someone on purpose, and that the usual notion of it, a pride peculiar to tragic heroes that angers the gods, is mistaken.
- Fisher's summary of his newest chapter, "Hybris in Ancient Greece", in D. Cairns, N. Bouras and E. Sadler-Smith (eds.), *Hubris, Ancient and Modern* (Cambridge University Press, December 2025), pp. 33 to 56, as printed on the publisher's page (cambridge.org/core, DOI 10.1017/9781009461405.003, read 2026-10-05). Today's hubris, the pride or excess of confidence that goes before a fall, descends by his account from what classicists used to teach: that hybris was an arrogant frame of mind which angered the gods by overstepping the bounds set for mortals. That teaching left out something plain: many Greek cities had a charge of hybris in their law, brought as a rule for violent or sexual assault. His 1992 book moved the concept under the heading of honor. Most scholars, he says, now accept that hybris took in both arrogance and the dishonoring of other people, and argue only about how to weigh the two. He now partly revises his own account, which gave the state of mind too little weight.
- Fisher's summary at the head of "Hybris and Dishonour: II", *Greece and Rome* 26 (1979), 32 to 47 (the extract on the publisher's page, DOI 10.1017/S0017383500026681): following Aristotle, he puts the heart of the concept in conduct whose whole aim is to shame and dishonor someone else.
- Cairns 1996 (below), p. 1, gives the book's definition with its page (Fisher, p. 148): insults meant as insults, acts done on purpose to shame another. On p. 17 he states the view that Fisher attacks and calls traditional (Fisher, pp. 2 to 4, 32, 142 to 148, as Cairns cites them): hybris as the overreaching of mortals, answered by punishment from heaven, with tragedy as its chief stage.
- Andrew Wolpert's review of the 2025 volume, *Bryn Mawr Classical Review* 2026.06.33 (open access, bmcr.brynmawr.edu, read 2026-10-05), treats the traditional view (pride punished by heaven) as disproved by Fisher's 1992 study, and reports general agreement that hybris belongs to the Greek vocabulary of honor.

So the reviewer's sentence about Fisher holds, and is close to Fisher's own account of his work.

(e) MacDowell and Cairns.

D. M. MacDowell, "Hybris in Athens", *Greece and Rome* 23 (1976), 14 to 31. The article is behind the publisher's wall, and the Internet Archive's scan of the issue is not searchable. I read the record, the opening extract and the article's notes, all shown openly on the publisher's page (cambridge.org/core, DOI 10.1017/S0017383500018210), and two accounts of its argument by the scholars who answer it. The opening is about the law: in Demosthenes 54 a man is knocked down and crowed over, and yet he prosecutes for assault and not for hybris. Fisher's reply, "Hybris and Dishonour: I", *Greece and Rome* 23 (1976), 177 to 193 (the extract on the publisher's page, DOI 10.1017/S0017383500019203), reports MacDowell's definition, by which hybris is having strength or high spirits and spending them on one's own indulgence, and five further points. By the fourth, religion is seldom in question. By the fifth, there is often someone wronged, and the case is graver when there is. MacDowell's notes 14 and 15 cite passages in which the offense touches gods (among them *Persians* 807 to 822 and *Hippolytus* 474 to 475); I could not see the sentences they belong to. Where he differs from Fisher, by Cairns's account (pp. 1, 22 to 23, 32): for MacDowell there can be hybris with nobody wronged, so dishonor is not of its essence; what is of its essence is surplus energy enjoyed for its own sake, and he takes the hybris that Greeks ascribed to animals and plants as real evidence. For Fisher there is always, or nearly always, someone wronged and a will to dishonor him.

Douglas L. Cairns, "Hybris, Dishonour, and Thinking Big", *Journal of Hellenic Studies* 116 (1996), 1 to 32. I read all of it as page images of the publisher's version, which the University of Edinburgh's research repository holds open (pure.ed.ac.uk, file 11874467, read 2026-10-05).

- He sides with Fisher against the old view, and credits him with having disposed of the notion that the concept is religious at bottom (p. 17).
- He differs on what is central. For Fisher the act comes first and is aimed at a victim. For Cairns the state of mind comes first: hybris is an inflated opinion of oneself (the thinking big of his title), an over-valuing of one's own honor that slights the claims of others even when no one in particular is aimed at (pp. 1 to 2, 10 to 11, 17, 32). He shows that Aristotle ties it to the rich, the young and the lucky, who think their good fortune puts them beyond misfortune (pp. 7 to 8).
- On the gods: they stand at the top of the scale of honor, so presumption toward them is an ordinary case of hybris and not a separate religious meaning (pp. 14, 16 to 19, 31). His cases are Xerxes in Herodotus, book 7, the ghost's speech in Aeschylus' *Persians*, and the scene of the crimson cloth in the *Agamemnon*, which Fisher allows to be at most mildly hybristic (p. 19, citing Fisher, pp. 287 to 289). These are the two examples Auden gives (finding 4).

(f) Greek uses that bear on Auden's sense. Read in Greek in the Perseus repository's texts (files tlg0085.tlg002, tlg0006.tlg005, tlg0016.tlg001 and tlg0086.tlg038, 2026-10-05), with a script that lists every form of the word.

- Aeschylus, *Persians*: the word occurs twice, both in the ghost's speech. At 808 it is paired with godless thoughts, and the acts named in 809 to 812 are the plunder of images and the burning of temples. Lines 820 to 822 warn mortals against thinking above their station and liken hybris to a plant whose harvest is ruin. The bridging of the Hellespont is condemned earlier, at 744 to 750, as a mortal's hope to master the gods; the word is not used there.
- Euripides, *Hippolytus* 474 to 475: the Nurse tells Phaedra that wanting to be stronger than the gods is plain hybris.
- Herodotus 7.16α: Artabanus says that of two counsels put to the Persians one fed hybris. Where Xerxes has the Hellespont whipped (7.35) the word does not occur.
- Aristotle, *Rhetoric* 2.5.14 (1383a1 to 3), 2.8.3 and 2.8.6 (1385b19 to 31) and 2.16.1 (1390b32 to 34): people in great good fortune think no harm can come to them, and that is why they are hybristic; the rich behave as if they had every good thing.

So fifth-century Greeks did use the word of presumption toward gods, as one use among many, and Aristotle knew the state of mind that Auden describes. Neither is the word's first meaning.

## 4. What Auden means by the word

(a) *The Enchafèd Flood*. I searched the site's kept scan (.cache/research/auden/ef.txt, the first Vintage Books edition, September 1967) for both spellings: three hits. I read only the few lines around each and worked out the pages from the page numbers at the foot of each page of the scan.

- p. 9 (scan lines 458 to 460). After the Old English sea poems, whose mariner he finds pitiable, Auden writes one sentence of his own. Its three points: the sea is a place to keep away from; trying to cross it is rash to the edge of hubris; a man's friends have reason to worry. He then prints Odes 1.3, lines 21 to 24, with a prose version, and goes on to Dante's Ulysses. He does not explain the word here, and his own sentence names no god and no limit. The god who parted the lands, and the word for impious, are in the stanza he quotes as his evidence. He also stops short of calling the attempt hubris: the rashness comes near it. The caption's "rash and close to hubris" reports this fairly.
- p. 73 (scan line 3096): the hawk that carries off Ahab's hat in *Moby-Dick* is read as Zeus's bird, sent to warn an Ahab cast as Prometheus against his hybris.
- p. 136 (scan line 5619): Ahab's fall is tragic in the Greek way. A Greek tale would have made it the gods' penalty for hybris and placed it last; in the novel the fall has happened before the story opens. The lines that follow bring in his earlier pride.

So in this book the word is the fault of the exceptional man which the gods punish. Nothing in it speaks of the word's history.

(b) Elsewhere. Searched by script in the kept scans of *Forewords and Afterwords* (Faber and Faber, 1973; seven hits) and *The Dyer's Hand* (1962; five hits), with a few lines read around each.

- "The Greeks and Us" (the editor's introduction to *The Portable Greek Reader*, Viking, 1948), in *Forewords and Afterwords*, p. 19. The hero of Greek tragedy is placed where every course open to him is wrong, and Auden reads that as proof of an earlier fault, the one the gods really charge him with: hybris. He defines it there as a self-confidence so swollen that the hero, proud of his excellence, takes himself for a god and thinks suffering cannot reach him. It is sometimes shown in an act, and his two examples are Agamemnon stepping onto the purple cloth and a Persian king's attempt to throw a bridge across the Hellespont. So one of Auden's own two examples of the act is a crossing of the sea. (The scan names the king as Darius. In Aeschylus the bridge is his son's, Xerxes', and the ghost of Darius condemns it: *Persians* 744 to 750, read in Greek. The site should not repeat the name unexamined.) On p. 21 he puts it more briefly: the first sin of the Greek hero is to think himself godlike, a temptation open only to those of unusual good fortune. He sets against it the pride of the modern tragic hero, who knows his own limits and weak points and will not own them.
- "Søren Kierkegaard" (the introduction to *The Living Thoughts of Kierkegaard*, 1952), same book, p. 173: what Oedipus did to his father and mother counts as the penalty for his hubris, not as the sin itself.
- *The Dyer's Hand* (Random House, 1962), "Balaam and His Ass", pp. 141 to 142: Phileas Fogg has hubris. He is certain that no event can take him by surprise, and that certainty draws him into his bet. "The Guilty Vicarage", p. 153, has two looser uses (of intellect, of innocence).
- Recorded in the site's research note C (remake/research/C_auden_on_works.md, sections H4 and H11), and confirmed by me with one search line each in the Internet Archive's lending scans: "The Christian Tragic Hero" (1945; the note read it as reprinted in R. W. Corrigan, ed., *Tragedy: Vision and Form*, 1965, pp. 143 to 147, and marks its first place of printing as recalled), where the Greek hero's flaw is a strong man's false belief that his strength is unshakable; and *Secondary Worlds* (1968), pp. 18 to 19 by the note, where Creon's fall is the penalty for hubris directed at the gods and what he does to Antigone merely shows it.

Auden's sense, in my words: hybris is the fault of the strong and fortunate man who trusts himself so far that he thinks he is like a god and out of reach of misfortune, and the gods punish him for it. He states this in print, with a definition, so the site can truthfully give Auden's own sense of the word in place of a word history. It also fits the Horace stanza: his own example of the act is the bridging of a strait. It is, though, the reading of tragedy that the classical dictionary now warns against (3d), so the site should give it as Auden's and not as the meaning of the Greek word.

## 5. Weighing it

The clause "a Greek word that first meant presumption toward the gods".

- As a history of the Greek word it is wrong. The earliest uses are of wrongs done to other people, with gods as witnesses and punishers (3b). The lexicon has no such sense (3a). The classical dictionary says the word is not at root religious and that the sense of a pride that angers heaven comes from misreading (3d). MacDowell, Fisher and Cairns disagree with one another about much, and all three reject a religious core (3d, 3e).
- It goes past its own source. The Online Etymology Dictionary puts three violent senses first, adds the clause about gods after them, and names no authority for "originally" (2).
- What can be said for it. Greeks of the fifth century did call presumption toward gods hybris, as one use among many (3f). One modern scholar, M. W. Dickie (1984), defines hybris as an over-confidence that forgets human limits, by Cairns's report (p. 1 and note 4), and Cairns himself gives the state of mind first place; neither says that this is what the word first meant. And the clause is close to what Auden meant by the word (4).

So the clause is a fair rough gloss of Auden's use and a false statement about Greek. As worded it is wrong, and not only narrow.

The reviewer's five points all hold: the clause gives a religious sense as the original one (1, 2); the Greek word chiefly concerns insolent, violent or dishonoring conduct toward other people (3a, 3b, 3d); the account of Aristotle is accurate (3c); Fisher did overturn the older account, by his own summary and by Cairns's and Wolpert's (3d, 3e); the etymology site is real and is a poor authority here, since it cites nothing and the lexicon and the classical dictionary contradict it (2, 3a, 3d). One caution: scholars still argue how much the state of mind counts as against the act, so "chiefly conduct toward others" is safe and "only conduct" would not be.

The reviewer's replacement, clause by clause.

1. "Auden describes the crossing as rash and close to hubris." True (*The Enchafèd Flood*, p. 9). It drops "quoting the stanza", which is also true and ties the sentence to the plate.
2. "The Greek term covers arrogant or outrageous conduct, often involving the dishonouring of others;" True and supported: the lexicon's senses I and II, the classical dictionary, Fisher's 2025 summary. "Often" is cautious, since for Fisher dishonor is the essence. The site's spelling would be dishonoring.
3. "here Auden applies it to presumptuous defiance of divinely established limits." Not supported as worded. Auden's sentence has no god and no limit in it, and it does not quite apply the word: the rashness comes near hubris. The limit set by a god is in the Horace stanza he prints next, and the caption already reports that from the Latin (the god, the ships without reverence, the waters not to be touched). Where Auden does define the word he makes it the fortunate man's belief that he is godlike, and he gives the refusal of known limits to the modern hero's pride, as its contrast (*Forewords and Afterwords*, p. 21). The clause is a reasonable reading of the page. It is still the reviewer's reading laid on Auden, and under the site's rule it cannot stand as a report of what Auden says.

## 6. What the caption can truthfully say

In each case the sentence before it stays ("The edition printed here finds in the ode a lament for the presumption of mankind."), and "hubris" comes out of `sources[15]`. The notes that repeat the old gloss (notes.md lines 376 to 379 and 557, critic.md line 317, exam.md line 148) would follow.

**A. No gloss.** "Auden, quoting the stanza, calls the attempt to cross the sea rash and close to hubris."
Stands on *The Enchafèd Flood*, p. 9, which is ground 7 already. Delete ground 6, and delete "*Hubris*: the Online Etymology Dictionary." from the "stands" line. A reader loses three things: help with the word, the notice that it is Greek and not Horace's, and the link between Auden's word and the god of the stanza. The loss is small. The sentence before gives "the presumption of mankind", and the caption has already glossed *impiae* as without reverence for gods.

**B. Auden's own sense (my recommendation).** "Auden, quoting the stanza, calls the attempt to cross the sea rash and close to hubris. In 1948 he had explained the Greek word as the sin of the tragic hero, a fortunate man so sure of himself that he thinks he is like a god and safe from harm."
Stands on "The Greeks and Us" (1948), in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), pp. 19 and 21, read in the kept scan (4b). Ground 6 becomes a ground of kind "auden": the claim, that the gods charge the hero of Greek tragedy with hybris as his real sin, a confidence in which he takes himself for a god beyond suffering, and that only the very fortunate are tempted to it; the source as above. The "stands" line: "*Hubris* as Auden explains it: 'The Greeks and Us,' in *Forewords and Afterwords*, pp. 19 and 21." Add those pages to `sources[6]`. Three cautions. Auden spells the word hybris in that essay. The second sentence should be run through the site's closeness test against p. 19. And the caption must keep "he had explained": this is Auden's account, which classical scholars since Fisher reject as the meaning of the Greek word (3d), so it cannot be turned into a statement about Greek.

**C. The Greek word, from the lexicon.** "Auden, quoting the stanza, calls the attempt to cross the sea rash and close to hubris. The word is Greek and meant insolence or outrage, most often violence that shames another person; the tragic poets also used it of mortals who set themselves above the gods."
Stands on Liddell, Scott and Jones under ὕβρις, senses I and II (3a, read whole), with the classical dictionary in support on what the word most often names (3d, read only in lines), and for the last clause on Aeschylus, *Persians* 808 and 820 to 822, and Euripides, *Hippolytus* 474 to 475 (3f, read in Greek). Ground 6: the same claim, with the source "Liddell, Scott and Jones, *A Greek-English Lexicon* (9th edition, 1940), under ὕβρις, read at Logeion (logeion.uchicago.edu) on 2026-10-05; Aeschylus, *Persians* 808 and 820 to 822, and Euripides, *Hippolytus* 474 to 475, in the Greek texts of the Perseus Digital Library". The "stands" line: "*Hubris*: Liddell, Scott and Jones, *Greek-English Lexicon*; Aeschylus, *Persians*; Euripides, *Hippolytus*." Add the lexicon to the sources list. The *Oxford Classical Dictionary* should be named only after someone has read the entry whole. In C the site speaks about Greek in its own voice and says nothing of what Auden meant. The reviewer's second clause, with the site's spelling, could serve in place of C's second sentence, and stands on the same sources.
