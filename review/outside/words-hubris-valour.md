## Reasons

### The Horace sentence

- The colon does not carry the reader. A colon and then a phrase with no verb is the site’s own way of saying what a term is ("This is the Sapphic stanza, named for Sappho of Lesbos: three lines of eleven syllables and one of five."). What stops the reader here is the opening of the phrase. "In his account of Greek tragedy," makes a reader wait for a clause. The sentence ends at "godlike" with no verb, and the reader goes back to see that all of it defines "hubris". The opening cannot simply be cut, because it is what marks the account as Auden’s.
- For a moment "in his account of Greek tragedy" also reads as the place where Auden calls the crossing rash. By the draft’s own "stands" line that place is *The Enchafèd Flood*.
- "The fortunate hero’s belief" reads like the name of a known figure, as "the tragic hero" is, and the reader looks for who that is. It packs two things into one label: he is the hero of a tragedy, and he is fortunate. "A fortunate man’s" asks for no such knowledge, and there "fortunate" gives the reason for the belief in one word.
- "Godlike" is not quite right there. In English it is mostly a word of praise, and for the heroes of Greek story a standing compliment, so the fault does not show. The proposed claim says he "takes himself for a god". "A god" is plainer, and the caption then says what its ground says. If "godlike" is kept as Auden’s own word, the claim should carry the word too.
- The shorter form reads at one pass: "which" follows "hubris" directly. It is 29 words, and no other sentence of the caption has more than 19. As two sentences (16 and 14 words) each reports one source. The "Word" caption of the second Purgatorio passage does the same: one sentence says where Auden wrote, the next what he wrote.
- Keep "elsewhere" whatever else changes. It tells the reader in one word that the explanation is not on the page where Auden quotes the stanza, as the piece’s second caveat does for his remarks on Greek.
- Once "a Greek word" goes, only the form with the colon still says where the word comes from. The loss is small: the reader has "hubris" as an English word, and the list of sources names “The Greeks and Us”.
- Nothing in either form sounds machine-made. Both pass the checker’s word patterns, and so does the wording above.

### The Purgatorio note

- "The English word of the same root" is clear in itself, and "root" is already the site’s word: the Inferno tab has "from the same Latin root" and "from the same root", the Paradiso tab "keeps one root". The site’s phrase has "from", and "Latin" at its first use. With both, this note speaks as those notes do, for one word more, and the new ground covers "Latin".
- Beside the next note it jars a little. The notes are printed as one numbered list, so the phrase is read just before "the look-alike word". A reader will take *lucid* for a look-alike only and *valour* for true kin. By the research (valour.md, finding 6) the two are the same case. That is a fault of the *lucid* note, and this correction makes it easier to see. The mend is to give the *lucid* note the same phrase when it is next opened. If it will not be opened, the simpler course here is to leave "keeps the look of the word" as it is and add only the new clause (30 words). Beside "where Longfellow keeps *charity*" there is no trouble.
- "Now means courage and once also meant worth" reads well but for "also". "Now" and "once" set the two times apart plainly. Ending on "worth" answers the "worth" of the first sentence, so the reader sees why the old sense matters without being told.
- "Also" is not needed. It makes the reader stop to ask as well as what, and when. "Once meant worth" is covered by the new ground ("an older sense, worth or value"). If the point is wanted that worth was one sense among others, "could once mean worth" makes it without the stop (34 words).

### Grounds, "stands" and sources

- Horace ground 6, the citation. The two grounds of this reading that cite the essay have “The Greeks and Us,” in Forewords and Afterwords (1973), with the comma inside the quotation mark and no year after the essay’s name. The year 1948 fits in the remark that follows.
- The two notes of change are clear: each says what the page used to say and why that would not do. Three small things would help. The other notes of change open with who made the change and give the date in figures ("Finisher, 2026-10-03:", "(examiner: …)"). The Horace note opens with a date in words and no name, and the Purgatorio note with neither. "From the Online Etymology Dictionary" hangs on "the gods"; "following" ties it to the caption. The Purgatorio note says "the note" in a tab that has five, and does not say what was done.
- The Horace note runs to two sentences inside one pair of parentheses. Another ground of the same tab does that work in one sentence, with a semicolon. "Greek" before "lexicon" tells a later reader what Liddell and Scott is.
- Purgatorio ground, the source. No ground in this reading or the Horace one has an en dash: ranges are typed with a hyphen (1955-57), and the en dash belongs to the printed list. Headwords in grounds are in lower case ("under prudens"; in the Inferno reading "under dolent and dole"). "Under valor, valour, senses 1 and 2 and the etymology" runs the double headword into the list after it; a colon shows where the headword ends.
- Horace "stands". The two proposed sentences are in the form of their neighbors: two other tabs of the piece cite the essay the same way. The fault is only the length. Counted as the checker counts, the line has 69 words now and 82 as proposed. No "stands" line in the Horace reading or the three Dante readings is above 70. The version above saves two words in the third sentence by giving it the colon the second sentence has. It names both books after one "Auden:", as the "stands" line of the next tab does, in the order of the caption’s two sentences. The essay’s name stays in the list of sources. If the third sentence must not be touched, "Auden: *The Enchafèd Flood*, p. 9; *Forewords and Afterwords*, pp. 19, 21." gives 70 words, at the cost of "19, 21" where the files write "19 and 21".
- Purgatorio "stands". "In English" is odd of an English word, and is left over from the old note ("which in English means courage"). The sentence before it has the form "*Intendere* in the *Enciclopedia Dantesca*"; the same form fits here and saves a word.
- sources[11]. The list writes headwords in italics and lower case (*simile*, *caritas*, and the new entry’s own *valor*, *valour*). "Under Valour" beside them looks like a slip. "Plutonian" keeps its capital as a word made from a name.
- The rest keeps the form of its neighbors. Page lists end in "and", as the list’s entry for Mendelson’s book does. Titles carry asterisks in the printed lines and none in the grounds. No ground ends in a period. The new entry for the *Century Dictionary* gives title, place, publisher and years in the order the other entries do.
