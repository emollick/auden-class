# The corrections as they are to be made: final wording

The lead, 5 October 2026. This is the wording to be applied after the critics' reports
(`words-hubris-valour.md`, `facts-hubris-valour.md`, `words-letter.md`, `facts-letter.md`).
Where it differs from what a critic proposed, the difference is marked "new" and has not
yet been read by a critic.

## 1. Horace, second passage ("The crossing"), tab "Word"

File: `remake/works/horace/reading.json`, `plates[1].lenses[1]`.

### The caption's last sentence becomes two

Now (26 words):

> Auden, quoting the stanza, calls the attempt to cross the sea rash and close to hubris, a Greek word that first meant presumption toward the gods.

To be (16 and 15 words; the caption then has 120 by the checker's count, and it warns above 120):

> Auden, quoting the stanza, calls the attempt to cross the sea rash and close to hubris. Elsewhere he explains hubris as the Greek tragic hero’s belief that he is a god.

New: "the Greek tragic hero’s" where the critic of words proposed "a fortunate man’s". Reasons:
the critic of facts found "a fortunate man" further from Auden than the hero; "the tragic
hero" is a figure a reader knows, which was the critic of words' objection to "the fortunate
hero"; and "Greek" says where the word comes from, which was lost with "a Greek word".

### Ground 6

Kind: "auden" (was "source").

Claim (new: "unusually" where the drafts had "very", since the critic of facts found "very" a shade weak):

> in Greek tragedy the sin the gods hold against the hero is hybris: a confidence so great that he takes himself for a god, out of reach of suffering; only the unusually fortunate are tempted to it

Source:

> “The Greeks and Us,” in Forewords and Afterwords (1973), pp. 19 and 21; the essay is of 1948, and Auden spells the word hybris there (outside review, 2026-10-05: the caption used to call hubris a Greek word that first meant presumption toward the gods, following the Online Etymology Dictionary; Liddell and Scott’s Greek lexicon has no such sense, and in Homer and Hesiod the word is used of outrage done to other people, so the history was cut and Auden’s own account put in its place)

## 3. Auden's letter of December 19, 1941, on the Dante examination

Files: `content/class.mjs`, `content/about.mjs`. This is the word critic's wording (`words-letter.md`)
with five changes the critic of facts asked for (`facts-letter.md`). Each change is marked "new".

- New (a): "the day he was to leave", since the leaving is only his plan as stated that morning.
- New (b): the sentence on the task is recast ("memorize … and write them out"), because the earlier form shared runs of five and of six words with the letter.
- New (c): "and on the train from Chicago to Los Angeles he would try", so that nobody reads "try on".
- New (d): Pearce has Auden promise to cancel the assignment, which is Pearce's word. The pages as they stand say "the examination" in two sentences; those two sentences change with the new ones.
- New (e): the letter says what was set, and Pearce and Chapman say what was done, so the summing-up on the about page no longer counts the letter among the accounts of what was written at the examination.
- New (f): the sentence about three places where the book leaves words out is gone. It could not be settled that the dots are the editor's cuts.

### 3.1 The class page, the scene "The examination"

The second paragraph's last two sentences now end "… he would telephone and call the examination off. He did not telephone."

To be (new (d)):

> … he would telephone and cancel the assignment. He did not telephone.

Two new paragraphs after it:

> That is Pearce’s account, written more than forty years later. Auden gave his own on the morning of December 19, 1941, the day he was to leave for California, in a letter to his friend Ursula Niebuhr. For the final examination, he wrote, his class had only to memorize six cantos of Dante and write them out. The class was shocked and thought it could not be done. He had promised to ask for less if he could not learn it all himself, and on the train from Chicago to Los Angeles he would try (*Remembering Reinhold Niebuhr*, pp. 281–82).

> The letter has six cantos where Pearce has seven cut to five, a train where he has a drive, and a promise to ask for less where he has a promise to cancel the assignment. Written before the journey, it does not say what was asked in the end.

### 3.3 The class page, "Where this page may be wrong": the caveat on the cantos

> The accounts of the cantos disagree on their number and on when they were assigned. Auden’s letter to Ursula Niebuhr of December 19, 1941, has six, for the final examination, and a promise to ask for less if he could not learn them himself (*Remembering Reinhold Niebuhr*, pp. 281–82). Miller has six asked for at the first class, as an exercise, and four allowed to anyone who could not manage six (pp. 26–27). Farnan, who was not in the class, also has six, assigned on the first day (pp. 73 and 76). Nolan has each student choose six, in place of an examination (p. 53). Pearce has seven assigned in December and cut to five (pp. 153–54). Chapman’s classmates spoke of eight (p. 509). The account of the examination on this page is Pearce’s, with Auden’s letter set beside it.

A new caveat right after it (new (f): its fourth sentence is gone):

> Auden’s letter to Ursula Niebuhr could be read for this site only in the short extracts a search of the scanned book returns. Two scanned copies were searched, and they agree. The pages themselves were not seen.

### 3.6 The about page: a new first paragraph of that section

> Auden’s own account was written at the time. On the morning of December 19, 1941, the day he was to leave Ann Arbor for the Christmas vacation, he wrote to his friend Ursula Niebuhr. For the final examination, he told her, his class had only to memorize six cantos of Dante and write them out. The class was shocked and thought it could not be done. He had promised to ask for less if he could not learn it all himself, and on the train from Chicago to Los Angeles he would try. The letter is printed in *Remembering Reinhold Niebuhr* (pp. 281–82). It was read for this site in the short extracts that searches of two scanned copies return, and the pages themselves were not seen.

### 3.7 The about page: the last paragraph of that section (its first two sentences are new (e), in the form the critic of words gave on the second reading)

> So Auden’s own letter says that the cantos were set for the final examination. Two people who were in the class, Pearce and Chapman, say that the cantos were written out at a timed examination. The number of cantos is given as six by Auden in December, five by Pearce, six by Farnan and Nolan, six or four by Miller, and eight by some of Chapman’s classmates. The letter and Pearce also differ on the journey and on the promise. In the letter Auden is going by train, and promises to ask for less if he cannot learn the cantos himself. In Pearce’s account he is driving, and promises to cancel the assignment. The letter was written before the journey, so it does not say what was asked in the end.
