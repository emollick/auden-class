## The lead's five questions

1. Items 9 and 10: hold. The lowering is put as a promise already made to the class. The condition is that he himself fails to get the whole of it by heart on the train from Chicago to Los Angeles. Then he will make it smaller. The letter says nothing of calling the examination or the assignment off, and nothing of a telephone.
2. Item 16: in Pearce's report of Auden's words, the thing to be cancelled is the assignment, by a call to the students' representative. Pearce then adds, in his own voice, that no call came and so the Dante examination went ahead. So "the examination" is Pearce's gloss at most, and it says more than he gives Auden. He does not say there would have been no final examination. The truthful wording is "cancel the assignment". This touches the draft's two sentences and also the pages as they stand (`content/class.mjs` line 515, `content/about.mjs` line 112).
3. Item 14: not settled. The dots are there. The editor's note on the text (read in scan 1) says she usually corrected spelling and punctuation, but left some letters as written, and names the Auden letters among those. It does not say how cuts are shown, in the part I could read. The third row reads like the writer's own pause. The sentence should come out of 3.3 and 3.6.
4. Items 17 to 19 on the word critic's wording: as in the summary. Item 20: lengths and places below. Item 21: below.
5. "The day he left": the departure is only his plan at 8 that morning (vacations begin that day, he leaves at noon). The site's caveat on dates already treats the Stern letter so. "The day he was to leave" is the truthful form, on both pages. "Six cantos of Dante": not false, since the poem is Dante's, but the letter names the poem. If the poem's name is put into the sentence as it stands, the two shared runs join into one of fourteen words. To name the poem, recast the sentence.

### What the letter says, in my words

- Heading: Ann Arbor, MI, then December 19, 1941, then a salutation to Mrs. Niebuhr. The state's two letters are the book's form.
- First half: thanks, a joke about Reinhold in Purgatory, the war, a newspaper, a broadcast, news from Malaya, his Christmas oratorio. No word about teaching.
- Then: vacations begin that day; he leaves at noon for California; a parenthesis says it is 8 in the morning. A few of his students are doing well. People there hold differing views of him, and he tells the joke.
- His class is much shocked just now, because he has told it what the examination closing the semester will be: six cantos from Dante's poem, which he calls by its title, to be got by heart and then set down on paper. The class believes the thing impossible. So he has given it his word: should he fail to get the whole of it by heart during the train ride from Chicago to Los Angeles, he will make the assignment smaller.
- Then the question about criticism, a remark on their own undergraduate days, Waley's translation, packing, the icebox, love to her, to Reinhold and the family, a Christmas wish, his first name.
