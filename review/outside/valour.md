# The note on *valour* (Purgatorio piece, first passage, tab "English")

One point of the outside reader's review, checked on 5 Oct 2026 by two researchers in turn. The first was cut off: findings 1, 4a and 5 and the first half of 2 are that researcher's, kept after spot checks. The rest is the second researcher's. Dante's lines are named by canto and line and never written out.

## Summary

1. **Holds.** The Italian word here names God (Treccani, *Enciclopedia Dantesca*), and none of 14 commentaries read takes it as courage. Okey writes *goodness*. Longfellow writes *valour*, here and in six other places, each time for *valor* or *valore*.
2. **Holds in part.** "Which in English means courage" gives the word's living sense, in the 1860s as now, in every dictionary read. But the site cites no source for it, it leaves out that the word once also meant worth, and as placed it suggests that Longfellow kept the look and changed the sense. No source says that, and at Paradiso 5.3 he uses the word of the eyes, where courage is impossible. The note is misleading and unsupported, not false.
3. **Does not hold as worded.** The reviewer's "can also carry the older sense": the Century, Webster 1913 and the Oxford dictionary of 1916 all mark the sense of worth obsolete (last examples 1586 to 1655), as Worcester (1860) and Webster (1864) already did. The Century's own mark is missing from Wordnik's reprint. "Modern readers are more likely" is a claim about readers with no source. The clause needs the past tense and should speak of the word.
4. **Also found.** Longfellow never comments on the word in his notes. His stated rule was to report what the author says and leave explaining to the commentator, and Koch (1896) says he seeks words of Romance origin. Of the other ties in the three Dante pieces only "the look-alike word, *lucid*" runs a like risk. The Inferno ties on *dolent* and *dole* are sound and show the pattern. Recommended wording: candidate A in finding 8.
5. **Could not be checked.** The full Oxford English Dictionary entry online (subscription: only its summary was read), Cherchi's survey of 2003, the Harvard exhibition by Irmscher (rate limit), and what Longfellow himself meant by the word, on which no source was found.

## 2. The Italian word at Purgatorio 15.72

- Treccani, *Vocabolario on line*, valore (read 5 Oct 2026 at https://www.treccani.it/vocabolario/valore/, page fetched and read by me). Sense 1 is said of persons. 1a is the possession of high gifts of mind and character; the entry then says that in the *Comedy* the word also stands for God's omnipotence, the sum of his powers (it cites Purgatorio 11.4), or for God himself, and it cites Purgatorio 15.72, Paradiso 1.107, 29.143 and 10.3. Sense 1b is courage shown against enemies in battle (the sense of the modern military medals). Sense 1c, marked old, is strength of body or mind, and the strength of one faculty such as sight (Paradiso 5.3). Sense 2 is value in the economic sense. The site's citation "sense 1a, which cites Purgatorio 15.72" is exact.
- *Enciclopedia Dantesca* (1970), valore, signed Alessandro Niccoli (read the same day at https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/valore_(Enciclopedia-Dantesca)/). It calls the word one of the key terms of Dante's poetics, with a very wide range, first of all the possession of high gifts of mind and character. It says that the sense "courage in combat" is found only once, in the *Fiore* (128.2), and rejects a reading of Inferno 16.67 as military virtue. In the *Paradiso*, it says, the word most often serves the theme of God's omnipotence, his sovereign perfection and his work as creator; it lists Purgatorio 15.72 with Paradiso 1.107 and 29.143 as places where God is called by this phrase. It adds that the schoolmen assigned creative power to the Father in particular.
- Commentaries on the line at the Dartmouth Dante Project (https://dante.dartmouth.edu, search form set to Purgatorio, canto 15, line 72; it returned 49 notes; I read 14 of them on 5 Oct 2026, each on its own page of the site). None takes the word as courage. Serravalle (1416-17) glosses it in Latin as eternal goodness, or the grace of God. Tommaseo (1837, edition of 1865) says the word is used of God in the sense of good, as often in the *Paradiso*. Scartazzini (second edition, 1900) calls it the divine splendor, the infinite good of line 67. Tozer (1901) explains it as the might of God's love. Grandgent (1909-13) turns it by *goodness*, Okey's word, and says that God's blessing answers the measure of the soul's love and is added to it; Singleton (1970-75) only repeats Grandgent. Porena (1946-48), Sapegno (1955-57), Bosco and Reggio (1979), Pasquini and Quaglio (1982) and Chiavacci Leonardi (1991-97) all paraphrase it as the divine or eternal good that pours into the soul. Three read it as light or power: Lombardi (1791-92) the lasting brightness of the light of glory, Andreoli (1856) the eternal power that makes blessed, Fosca (2003-15) the divine power.
- So the two reference works say that the phrase names God, and most commentators say more exactly that it is God's good, given to a soul in the measure of its love. Okey's *goodness* is the commentators' own word. The note's "worth or power, and here a name for God" keeps to the two reference works and is not contradicted by the commentaries.

## 3. The English word in the dictionaries

Every entry below was read by me on the scan or page named, on 5 Oct 2026. None comes from a search summary. Senses are given in each dictionary's own order.

- Johnson, *A Dictionary of the English Language* (1755), VALOUR (scan: https://archive.org/details/johnsons_dictionary_1755, leaf n2148). One sense, unnumbered: "Personal bravery; strength; prowess; puissance; stoutness." No sense of worth or value. Two of its five defining words are words for power.
- Webster, *An American Dictionary of the English Language* (1828), vol. 2, VALOR (scan: https://archive.org/details/americandictiona02websrich, leaf n868; the reprint at https://webstersdictionary1828.com/Dictionary/valor agrees). One sense: firmness of mind in danger, personal bravery, courage. Being worth appears only in the etymology, as a meaning of the Latin verb. No sense of worth.
- Worcester, *A Dictionary of the English Language* (Boston, 1860), p. 1614, VALOR (scan: https://archive.org/details/cu31924027443393, leaf n1689). Sense 1 is value, with a dagger and one example, from Thomas More. Sense 2 is bravery, courage. The key on p. lxviii (leaf n75) says the dagger marks words or meanings that are obsolete or antiquated.
- Webster, *An American Dictionary*, the Goodrich and Porter revision of 1864 in its 1865 printing, p. 1460, Valor (scan: https://archive.org/details/americandictiona00websuoft, leaf n1537). Sense 1 is value, marked obsolete and rare, with no example. Sense 2 is courage. This and Worcester were the large American dictionaries on sale when Longfellow's version came out in 1867.
- *The Century Dictionary* (1889-91), part 23, p. 6690, valor, valour (scan: https://archive.org/details/centurydictipt2300whituoft, leaf n181). Sense 1 is bravery, above all courage and skill in fighting. Sense 2 is "Value; worth." and its number carries a dagger. Sense 3 is a brave man, marked rare. The key of signs (vol. 1, p. xviii, https://archive.org/details/centurydict01whit, leaf n25) says the dagger is to be read as obsolete. The four examples under sense 2 are from the *Romaunt of the Rose*, Torkington's travel diary, Peele and Ben Jonson; the Century gives no dates for them. So the reviewer is right that the Century gives the sense, and the Century itself marks it obsolete.
- Wordnik (https://www.wordnik.com/words/valor) reprints the Century entry without the dagger and without the mark for rare. There the sense of worth looks like a living one. A reader who knows the Century only through Wordnik cannot see that it is marked obsolete. The same page shows the entry of the American Heritage Dictionary (fifth edition), which has one sense only: courage, as shown in battle.
- *Webster's Revised Unabridged Dictionary* (1913), VALOR (Project Gutenberg no. 29765, https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/29765). Sense 1 is the same two words, value and worth, marked obsolete, with the one example from More. Sense 2 is courage. Sense 3 is a brave man, marked rare.
- *A New English Dictionary on Historical Principles* (the first Oxford English Dictionary), vol. 10, part 2, p. 27, Valour (scan: https://archive.org/details/newenglishdictio102murruoft, leaf n48). Sense 1a is "Worth or importance due to personal qualities or to rank", marked obsolete, last example 1586. Sense 1b, worth in manly qualities, is obsolete, last example 1508. Sense 1c is courage or bravery, the living sense, with examples from 1581 to 1878. Sense 1d is the word used as a name or title, or of a brave person. Sense 2, value or worth in material or other respects, is obsolete, last examples 1642 and 1616. Sense 3, the sum a thing is worth, is obsolete, last literary example 1609, followed by one line from a glossary of East Anglian dialect (before 1825). Sense 4, an amount or quantity, is marked rare. The separate entry Valor (p. 26, leaf n47) follows the same pattern: intrinsic worth or merit is obsolete, last example 1655; the force or import of a word or symbol is not marked obsolete, last example 1808; courage is marked as now chiefly American.
- The Oxford English Dictionary online, valour | valor, n. (https://www.oed.com/dictionary/valour_n, only the part shown without a subscription). It says the entry was first published in 1916 and is not fully revised, and that it lists 11 meanings, eight of them labelled obsolete. That count fits the 1916 page: the eight are 1a, 1b, the two parts of 2 and the four parts of 3. The definitions online are behind the subscription and I did not read them there.

Was the sense of worth living English in 1867? By these dictionaries, no. The two oldest (Johnson, Webster 1828) do not record it at all. The two American dictionaries of the 1860s record it only as value, with an obsolete mark and at most one example, from Thomas More. The three later ones mark it obsolete, and the Oxford dictionary's examples of it stop at 1655, apart from the dialect glossary. What the dictionaries cannot settle is whether a translator who had the Italian word before him meant to revive the old sense.

## 4a. Longfellow's use of the word, counted

Counted with a script on the hosted texts (site/data/texts/dante-longfellow and dante-italian, both from Project Gutenberg, nos. 1004 and 1012), printing canto, line and single words only.

- He writes *valour* seven times and *valor* once. The eighth is Purgatorio 26.145, in the lines he leaves in Provençal, so it is not his English.
- The seven: Inferno 16.67; Purgatorio 7.114, 7.117, 15.72, 16.116; Paradiso 5.3, 19.126. In all seven the Italian word in the same line is *valor* or *valore*.
- The Italian text has *valor* or *valore* 28 times (27 outside the Provençal lines). He turns it by *valour* 7 times and by another word 20 times: *power* 9 (Paradiso 1.14, 1.107, 9.105, 10.3, 10.29, 13.45, 19.43, 21.15, 29.143), *worth* 2 (Paradiso 5.26, 14.42), and once each *value* (Paradiso 5.62), *virtue* (Inferno 26.99), *worthiness* (Inferno 4.44), *excellence* (Purgatorio 16.47), *desert* (Purgatorio 14.90), *goodness* (Paradiso 26.42), *beneficence* (Purgatorio 10.74), *omnipotence* (Purgatorio 11.4), *Glory* (Paradiso 33.81).
- His one *courage* (Inferno 2.131) answers *ardire*, not *valore*; *courageous* (Inferno 10.37) answers *animose*.
- Rechecked by a second researcher after the first was cut off: a fresh script over the same two hosted texts (all 100 cantos, line counts equal in every canto) gave the same eight places, the same 28 Italian places and the same English word in each. Nothing in 4a had to be changed.

## 4b. Longfellow's own notes

- His notes of 1867 stand in the Dartmouth Dante Project as a commentary of their own (https://dante.dartmouth.edu, search form, commentary "Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, 1867"; read 5 Oct 2026). He has twelve notes on Purgatorio 15, at lines 1, 20, 38, 39, 55, 67, 70, 89, 97, 101, 106 and 117. None is on line 72. I read those at 55, 67 and 70: they give parallels (Milton, a passage of the *Convito*, Paradiso 14.40) and say nothing of the word.
- A search of all his notes for *valour* finds nothing. A search for *valor* finds eleven notes (Inferno 20.34, 28.31, 28.134; Purgatorio 16.46, 16.117, 18.119; Paradiso 6.25, 9.94, 16.110, 18.47, 18.48). I read the sentence round the word in each. Every time it stands in matter he quotes or translates from another writer, never in a remark on the word. So he does not comment on the word.
- He wrote no preface on his method. Koch (see 4c), p. 42, observes that apart from the sonnets the three volumes have no word of introduction to tell the translator's aim.
- His own statements were printed after his death in Samuel Longfellow's *Life of Henry Wadsworth Longfellow*, vol. 3 (scan: https://archive.org/details/lifeofhenrywadsw03longuoft). To Robert Ferguson, 8 May 1867 (p. 88, leaf n103), he says the one merit of his book is that it is exactly what Dante says, and that he tried to make it as literal as a prose translation. His table talk (p. 411, leaf n438) has: "The business of a translator is to report what his author says, not to explain what he means"; explaining, he adds, is the commentator's work.

## 4c. What has been said of his keeping the Italian word

- Theodore W. Koch, *Dante in America* (Boston: Ginn, for the Dante Society, 1896; scan: https://archive.org/details/danteinamericahi00kochrich). On p. 43 (leaf n46) he says that where the original allows two readings Longfellow keeps both, choosing wherever he can a wording with as many senses as Dante's own and leaving the reader to interpret. On p. 53 (leaf n56) he says Longfellow seeks out words of Romance origin where the translator Parsons likes plain Saxon ones, and that the Italian shows through his version all the time.
- George Ticknor to Longfellow, 1 June 1867 (*Life*, vol. 3, pp. 90-91, leaves n105 and n106). He admires the strictness and wonders whether such conditions leave room for the free movement an English reader wants. The editor's footnote says the version follows the original line for line and almost word for word.
- The unsigned review in *The Atlantic Monthly*, August 1867 (Project Gutenberg no. 19779, https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/19779). It says the English answers the Italian line for line and word for word, and that on Longfellow's theory an author's words must be given by their exact equivalents wherever the idioms of the two languages allow.
- Joseph Luzzi, "How to Read Dante in the 21st Century," *The American Scholar*, 22 March 2016 (https://theamericanscholar.org/how-to-read-dante-in-the-21st-century/). He calls the version close and at times awkwardly literal, and its English Italianate.
- Paola Loreto, "«Englishing» Dante: Three Recent American Poets' Translations of the Inferno," *ACME* 2/2021, pp. 181-196 (open access, https://riviste.unimi.it/index.php/ACME/article/view/18667), p. 182. Reporting a survey by Paolo Cherchi of 2003, which I did not read, she says Longfellow followed Dante line by line and kept close to his syntax, on a principle of literalism.
- None of these names *valour*. I found no scholar who discusses this word in his version. An online exhibition on Longfellow by Christoph Irmscher at Harvard could not be read: its archived copy answered twice with a rate-limit page.

## 7. Weighing it

The present note, sentence by sentence.

- "*Valore* is worth or power, and here a name for God." Stands (finding 2).
- "Okey writes *goodness*." Stands (finding 5).
- "Longfellow keeps the look of the word, *valour*". Stands. It is also the same word by descent: the Century and the *New English Dictionary* both set the English word beside Italian *valore* in their etymologies.
- "which in English means courage." Not false, and not sound as it stands. Three things are wrong with it.
  1. It has no source on the site (finding 1), and the site's rule asks for one.
  2. It tells half of what the dictionaries tell. Courage is the living sense in every dictionary read, in the 1860s as now. But the Century, Webster 1913 and the Oxford dictionary also record the sense of worth and mark it obsolete, and Johnson defines the word by strength and puissance as well as bravery.
  3. Set after "keeps the look of the word", it leads a reader to think that Longfellow kept the look and changed the sense. No source says that, and his own practice tells against it. All seven of his *valour* answer *valor* or *valore* (4a). At Paradiso 5.3 he says it of the eyes, where the Italian means strength of sight (Treccani, sense 1c, which cites that passage; I read the entry again to check) and courage is impossible. Here he says it of God. His rule was to report what the author says and leave explaining to the commentator (4b), and Koch describes a translator who picks wording with as many senses as Dante's and seeks words of Romance origin (4c). He also keeps Dante's two words apart in this passage: *goodness* for *bene* at line 67 and *valour* for *valore* at line 72, where Okey has *Good* and *goodness* (checked by script, single words).
- So the note is misleading and unsupported, not wrong. The reviewer's main point holds.

Where the reviewer goes further than the sources.

- That the Century also gives the sense of value or worth, and that other historical dictionaries record it as obsolete. True, but the Century itself marks the sense obsolete, with its dagger. The dagger is missing from Wordnik's reprint (finding 3).
- That the older sense is the more plausible reading of his wording. A fair inference from 4a, and only an inference. The sense of worth was not living English in 1867: Worcester (1860) and Webster (1864) mark it obsolete. What 4a shows is that Longfellow let the English word stand for the Italian one. He did not always do so. Nine times he writes *power*, twice where the Italian has the same two words as in this line (Paradiso 1.107 and 29.143, checked by script). Why he chose *valour* here no source says. The reviewer's own caution, that his intention cannot be known, is right.

The reviewer's replacement, clause by clause.

- "Valore means worth or power and here refers to God." True, on the same two reference works. It changes nothing of substance.
- "Okey writes goodness." True.
- "Longfellow uses valour," True (hosted text, Purgatorio 15.72).
- "an English word that can also carry the older sense 'worth,'" Half true. The sense is recorded (Century, sense 2; Webster 1913, sense 1; the Oxford dictionary of 1916, senses 1a and 2), and each of the three marks it obsolete. The Oxford dictionary's last examples run from 1586 to 1655, and its online summary still counts eight of eleven meanings as obsolete. So the word once could carry that sense. In present English it cannot. The clause needs the past tense.
- "although modern readers are more likely to understand it as courage." This is a claim about readers, and no source read measures readers. What the sources do support is a claim about the word: courage is its only living sense (the Oxford dictionary, sense 1c; the American Heritage Dictionary as shown on the Wordnik page gives that one sense). The clause should be said of the word.
- So the replacement is nearer the sources than the present note, and two of its clauses still say more than the sources do.

## 8. What the note can truthfully say

Three candidates, each of three sentences, in American spelling but for Longfellow's own word. None says what Longfellow meant, and none should.

- A, the smallest change (recommended). "*Valore* is worth or power, and here a name for God. Okey writes *goodness*. Longfellow keeps the look of the word, *valour*, which in English now means courage and once also meant worth." The new clause stands on three dictionaries. Courage is the living sense in the Century (sense 1), Webster 1913 (sense 2) and the Oxford dictionary of 1916 (sense 1c). Worth is the Century's sense 2, Webster's sense 1 and the Oxford dictionary's senses 1a and 2, each marked obsolete.
- B, with no claim about meaning. "*Valore* is worth or power, and here a name for God. Okey writes *goodness*. Longfellow keeps the English word from the same Latin root, *valour*." It stands on the hosted text and on the etymologies in the Century and the Oxford dictionary. It is the safest, and it tells the reader least.
- C, with his practice. "*Valore* is worth or power, and here a name for God. Okey writes *goodness*, and Longfellow keeps *valour*, as he does in six other places where Dante has this word. In English it now means courage, and its old sense of worth was out of use by his day." The six other places stand on the count in 4a. The last clause stands on Worcester (1860) and Webster (1864), which mark the sense obsolete, and on the dates of the Oxford dictionary's examples.

What the tab's grounds and "stands" line should then cite.

- One new ground, in the form the tab already uses. Claim: "valour comes from the same Latin word as valore; its living sense is courage; an older sense, worth or value, is marked obsolete". Source: "The Century Dictionary (1889-91), valor, valour, senses 1 and 2; A New English Dictionary on Historical Principles, vol. 10, part 2 (Oxford, 1928; the entry first published 1916), Valour, senses 1a, 1c and 2; both read in the scans at archive.org".
- For C, two more. Claim: "Longfellow writes valour seven times, each time where Dante has valor or valore". Source: "the hosted Longfellow and Italian texts: Inferno 16.67; Purgatorio 7.114, 7.117, 15.72, 16.116; Paradiso 5.3, 19.126". Claim: "the sense of value was marked obsolete in the American dictionaries of the 1860s". Source: "Worcester, A Dictionary of the English Language (1860), p. 1614; Webster, An American Dictionary of the English Language (1864, printing of 1865), p. 1460; both read in the scans at archive.org".
- The "stands" line gains one sentence before its last: "*Valour* in English: the *Century Dictionary* and the *New English Dictionary*, under *valor* and *valour*." The Inferno tab already does this for *dolent* and *dole*.
- Tie 3 can be made safe in the same way: "*Bright* for *lucido*. Longfellow keeps the English word from the same Latin root, *lucid*, which in verse could still mean shining." Its ground would be the *New English Dictionary*, vol. 6, part 1 (1908), Lucid, sense 1, read in the scan at archive.org.
- The addresses and leaf numbers of every scan are in findings 3 and 6.
