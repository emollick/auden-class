## Should fix

1. **facts, "To read · Thirty-three works, about {pages} pages"; and first,
   the note "By this site's count the list comes to…".** The list names
   thirty-three works. It did not ask for thirty-three to be read. One of the
   two Greek plays was a choice, as the page says two lines later, which is
   why Chapman (p. 508), Carpenter (p. 318) and Bruccoli (p. 10) all count
   thirty-two. Two more are marked "Omit" on the pictured copy, in a hand
   nobody has identified (notes A, 1.6). The 4,800 pages are a count of the
   copies printed on this site (4,829 in `site/data/shelf.json`). Both Greek
   plays and both "Omit" books are in it, *The Family Reunion* is not, and
   *Fear and Trembling* counts for the third that is printed, 34 pages. The
   about page says all this. Here "To read" and "the list comes to" claim
   more. Proposed: the label "On the list", the value "Thirty-three works,
   nine of them operas", and in the note "By this site's count the copies
   printed here come to about {pages} pages."

2. **facts, "In the room · Fourteen or fifteen students, by Pearce's count".**
   That is one Thursday late in October (Pearce, p. 129). The other figures
   are larger: limited to twenty-five (Farnan, p. 73), twenty-five at the
   first class (Miller, in the *Ann Arbor Observer*), twenty-two enrolled
   (Chapman, known by search summary only, as the about page says). Nolan
   (p. 53) says the list drove off many who had registered, and
   Miller (p. 29) has one student drop out. Proposed value: "Twenty-five at
   the start, by Miller's count. Fourteen or fifteen one Thursday in October,
   by Pearce's."

3. **door, "The mimeographed list that announced it opened it to…".** The
   heading of the reading list says who might take the course. Nothing read
   says the list announced the course: Miller (p. 27) has it handed out at
   the first class, and Pearce learned of the course from a card on the
   office door. The sheet also names the professor. Proposed: "The heading of
   the mimeographed reading list opened it to juniors, seniors and graduate
   students who had permission in writing from Professor Arno Bader of the
   department." (The three kinds of student are named as the sheet names
   them. A list of that sort cannot be loosened.)

## Left out

What these sources have on what the class was asked to do, or on what it was
like to be there, that the page does not use. Each is given in this
examiner's words, with the page.

**What the class was asked to do**

1. Ahab, for the fold in `ego`. In the review of February 1941 Auden has a
   footnote that sets *Moby Dick* apart from every other quest: no saving is
   in view, and the whale is both the thing sought and its hostile keeper
   (*Prose* II, p. 111, quoted in notes C under *Moby Dick*). It answers the
   third question set that afternoon, and with it the fold could say what all
   three are after. Proposed: "In a footnote to the same review he set *Moby
   Dick* apart from every other quest: no saving is in view, and the whale is
   both the thing sought and its hostile keeper (p. 111)."
2. The opening speech (Miller, pp. 26–27, rebuilt by Miller forty years on).
   The page uses its first sentence, the rule against notes. The rest: Auden
   named some of the books (the *Comedy*, the *Confessions*, four plays of
   Shakespeare, *The Brothers Karamazov*), said there were some twenty with
   fifteen more recommended, asked for certain American books as central to
   the theme, and asked for six cantos of the *Comedy* by heart, "as an
   exercise". A graduate student objected that she had never learned a page
   by heart. He answered that any grown person could, and then allowed four
   to anyone who could not manage six. This is the first assignment on
   record, and it was given on the afternoon the scene `first` describes.
3. The translation exercise (Chapman, p. 509; Carpenter, p. 319). Early in a
   semester each student was to put a poem into English out of a language he
   or she had never studied (Latin, French or German), using a dictionary
   and nothing more. Each chose the language. Chapman did a poem of Rilke's.
   Which course it was, he leaves unsaid. The about page has this and the
   class page does not.
4. Reading added in class. Farnan (p. 77) says Auden recommended Groddeck's
   *Book of the It*, which, she says, the library would lend to graduate
   students and to nobody else. Miller (p. 28) says he did not keep to the
   list.
5. Stevens's notebook (Farnan, p. 73) lists more than the papers: the
   *Comedy* used as a textbook, and poems written to a set scansion. The
   second sounds like the spring course (notes A, 3.2).
6. Nolan (p. 53) says the cantos stood in place of a final examination and
   that each student chose which six. It disagrees with Pearce on both
   counts and with Chapman on the first. It belongs with the accounts in
   "Should fix", item 12, if they are given.
7. A second set of notes. Kreyling (p. 40, and his notes) used Kenneth
   Millar's own notes on the class, lent by Pearce, and says they are fullest
   on *Fear and Trembling* and *The Castle*. The Kierkegaard piece says so.
   The first caveat here ("…comes from one student") could add that a second
   student's notes exist and have not been printed.

**In the room**

8. Miller, p. 28. Auden talked and did not lecture. His books and papers
   stayed on the desk at his back, unopened, and he paced between the
   windows and the door. He wore a jersey, jeans, and sneakers that were
   torn and seldom laced. (The memoir in the *Observer* adds a brown tweed
   jacket.) He dwelt on the ideas of the books and hardly ever on their
   style, and Pascal's *Pensées* kept coming back in the early meetings.
   Over half the class were graduate students, an instructor of the
   department and two graduate assistants sat in, and Miller calls it a
   seminar more than a course. At the foot of p. 27 he says it was not an
   easy one.
9. Miller, as notes B have him (3.B1, pages not seen there): Auden frowned
   and made faces while talking and rarely laughed, a joke could start with
   Othello and end with the Marx Brothers, and some thought him an awkward
   professor whose talk went over their heads.
10. Miller, pp. 28–29: Auden said of Melville that he was European in his
    sense of evil. It is one more thing said in class about a book on the
    list.
11. Chapman, p. 508: the trouble at first was his accent and what Chapman
    took for a speech impediment. The class strained to hear, and the
    strangeness wore off. Pp. 508–09: the rule against notes frightened the
    class at first.
12. Nolan, p. 53: Auden did not look at the students and looked out of the
    window as he lectured, which agrees with Pearce (p. 129) and Chapman. He
    read Shakespeare's characters in a Jungian way. The length of the list
    drove away many who had registered.
13. Chapman, pp. 512–13: Auden once quoted Touchstone's line about poetry
    and feigning to the class. Three classmates thought him unhappy or
    troubled that year (p. 512).
14. A visitor. Caroline Newton was a guest at the house for about the first
    ten days of December 1941 and came to one class, where she sat at the
    front (Nicholas Jenkins's note in *Auden Studies* 3, p. 70, citing
    Miller, pp. 59–65).
15. Miller, pp. 77–78: Angelyn Stevens told him she was often late for the
    class, with a house to run and no car, and that she loved being in it.
16. The date of the drive. Auden's own letter, postmarked Ann Arbor on the
    morning of December 19, 1941, says he was leaving at noon for Hollywood
    (*Auden Studies* 3, pp. 65–72). So the last class before Christmas was
    on or before Thursday, December 18 (notes A, 2.3).

**At the house**

17. Miller, p. 56: one of the five who came on Thanksgiving Day was a
    Japanese American student, whom Miller calls lonely. Nolan, p. 54:
    Millar went to the Friday evenings. Chapman, as Bardallis quotes him
    (*Ann Arbor Beer*, about pp. 98–101): at home Auden was expansive and
    glad of company. Notes B (3.B1): Miller says the first evening went well
    and that Auden made a point of talking with every student.

**Other**

18. The one printed notice of the course found from 1941: *The Michigan
    Alumnus*, October 18, 1941, p. 48, which gives the title with "Culture"
    for "Literature" (notes A, 5.2).
19. Farnan, pp. 76–77, who was not in the class: students up late learning
    their cantos, and a delegation of senior faculty that failed to move
    him. It is hearsay and would have to be marked as that.
