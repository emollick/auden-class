Errors of fact come first (1 to 4), then sentences that run too close to Pearce (5 to 11).

1. **`ego`, first paragraph: "Auden began with Augustine, …"** He did not begin there.
   Pearce says these notes catch Auden as he was winding up, just before the short break,
   and the first words he prints start in the middle of a thought (p. 134). The `when`
   line already said "the first hour", which is right, and the sentence under it said the
   opposite.
   Proposed: "These are the first notes Pearce kept in order. They pick up late in the
   hour, as Auden was winding up before the break. Augustine he called “the subtlest
   introspectionist between Aristotle and Freud,” and Augustine’s account of the conflict
   between self and ego the first classic of self-analysis."

2. **Lede, second paragraph: "… in the order Pearce gives them …", and second caveat:
   "The order of the afternoons is Pearce’s."** Not so for the first three scenes. Pearce
   tells the Thursday in late October first (pp. 129-31), then the first meeting
   (pp. 131-32), then the September interview (pp. 132-34). The page has them by date.
   He does not say whether that Thursday fell before or after the lecture on Augustine,
   and he calls the Augustine notes the earliest he has in any order (p. 134).
   Proposed, lede: "What follows are the afternoons they recorded: what Auden said, what
   he put on the blackboard, the records he played, and what he asked for by the next
   meeting." Proposed, caveat: "From the lecture on Augustine on, the order of the
   afternoons is Pearce’s. He tells the first three scenes the other way about, the
   October Thursday first, and they are set here by date. He does not say whether that
   Thursday came before or after the lecture on Augustine."

3. **`tempest`, note after the board: "From the board he turned to Gonzalo, the easiest
   person in the play to overlook: …"** The order is wrong, and it cuts the board off from
   its reason. In Pearce these remarks come before the board and lead to it. Gonzalo is
   easy to overlook, he is Polonius handled again, Shakespeare now finds something in the
   type to respect, and at that point Goethe's three reverences are brought in (p. 151).
   After the board Auden went on with Gonzalo: sometimes a blunderer and a bore, and now
   seen as good and to be trusted (p. 152).
   Proposed, before the board: "Gonzalo, he said, is the easiest person in the play to
   overlook. He is Polonius done again, and this time Shakespeare finds something in him
   to respect. Goethe, he went on, tells apart three kinds of reverence in *Wilhelm
   Meister*, and they fit the outlook of this late play. He wrote them on the blackboard
   and left them without a word of comment." Proposed, after it: "He came back from the
   board and went on with Gonzalo: a blunderer at times, and a bore, and now seen as good
   at heart and to be trusted."

4. **`chart`: "… and copied out, from a sheet he had prepared, a chart in two rows …"**
   More than Pearce says. He says only that Auden copied out a chart that he had made for
   the class (p. 153). There is no sheet in the essay and nothing in Auden's hand.
   Proposed: "When he had finished with *The Tempest* he went to the left edge of the
   blackboard and copied out a chart he had made for the class. It was in two rows and
   ran the whole width of the room."

Too close. The house rule is that a sentence of the page may not share five words in a row
with its source. The seven below do. All are on pages that the kept scan lacks (129-31 and
137-45), so the site's own sweep cannot see them. Each was found in the search text of the
book. The runs are named here by their first and last words and are not written out.

5. **`love`, on the phonograph: "Phrase after phrase he keeps the two voices …"** The
   first three words are Pearce's, and so are nine more in a row, from "from meeting in
   unison" to "of the duet" (p. 138). Only the middle of the sentence is the page's.
   Proposed: "All through the duet he holds the two voices apart, and lets them join only
   as it closes. So it would be with the lovers’ lives."

6. **`othello`, for next time: "For next day, think about the psychology …"** Thirteen
   words in a row are Pearce's, from "think about the psychology" to "villain or criminal",
   and "List the points" after them (p. 143).
   Proposed: "For next day: how does the mind of a saint work, and the mind of a villain
   or a criminal? Write down where the two are alike."

7. **`othello`, first paragraph: "The next few meetings were on Shakespeare: …"** Seven
   words in a row, and then the three titles in his order (p. 139).
   Proposed: "After that came Shakespeare, for several meetings: *Othello*, *Henry IV*,
   *The Tempest*."

8. **`othello`, after the assignments: "He went out into the early night loaded with
    …"** Two runs of five, "out into the early night" and "records and the record player",
    and "loaded with books, papers" besides (p. 144).
    Proposed: "He left in the dark with his arms full: books, papers, the records and the
    phonograph."

9. **`othello`, Desdemona followed across the schemes: "Naive by nature: a schoolgirl
    …" and "Her love is a kind of mother love."** Two runs of five: "of the world of
    action" and "a kind of mother love" (p. 142).
    Proposed: "Naive by nature: a schoolgirl who knows nothing of a soldier’s world, and
    who at once sees everything through Othello’s eyes. She loves him as a mother would."

10. **`three`, first paragraph: "The classroom was small, on the street side …",
    "Fourteen or fifteen students sat in scattered seats, …", and, new at 07:32, "… he
    might have been speaking to the autumn sky …"** The first has seven of Pearce's words

    Proposed: "The classroom was small and looked out on the street, and the sun was low
    in the west windows. There were fourteen or fifteen students, sitting well apart, and
    nobody was writing." And: "He stood half turned to the windows and talked slowly, with
    long pauses and no break. To Pearce he seemed to be talking to the sky outside, or to
    the great dead."

11. **`three`, third paragraph, new at 07:32: "He tried the terms on a case, a man who
    wants to kiss …", and the column head of the board table, "A man wants to kiss another

    Proposed: "He tried the terms on a case, a man set on kissing a married woman, and
    gave the three answers. Then he chalked three lines on the blackboard, and tried them
    on a second case, a millionaire who steals." Proposed head: "A man wants to kiss a
    married woman".

1. **`tempest`, note after the board: "The close answered the question he had set on the
   *Othello* afternoon. The brilliant talkers …"** Two things. First, what stands where.
   The hangers-on, the end of Shakespeare's admiration for cleverness, and the gentle and
   honest were said straight after the board, while the subject was still Gonzalo. Then
   Auden talked about Valéry, and of that Pearce kept nothing. Only the small-time villain
   and the last question belong to the closing remarks (pp. 152-53). Second, "answered
   the question he had set" is the page's own link. Pearce does not make it. The content
   bears it out, so "came back to" is safe and "answered" is not.
   Proposed, to follow the sentence about Gonzalo in Must fix 3: "The brilliant talkers
   of the earlier plays have shrunk in this one to hangers-on. Shakespeare no longer
   admires cleverness, and for ordinary life he prefers the gentle and the honest. Then
   Auden talked briefly about Valéry, and of that Pearce kept nothing. The close came
   back to the question set on the *Othello* afternoon. The clever man is now a small-time
   villain, ready to stab a man in his sleep. Auden ended on a question of his own: …"

2. **`othello`, note: "Then, as the class broke up, he called out the work."; the `src`
   of the next part, "Called out as the class broke up"; and the fourth ask, "… he shouted
   over the noise, as you study *The Tempest*."** In Pearce the first three are the close
   of the lecture. Only the fairy stories were shouted, as the class began to break up
   (pp. 143-44). The tail of the fourth ask is Auden's speech in Pearce too. On the page it
   stands outside the quotation marks, where it reads as the site speaking to its reader.
   Proposed: "Then he gave the work." For the `src`: "Pearce, pp. 143–44. The last was
   shouted as the class broke up." For the ask: "“Read lots of fairy stories over the
   weekend!” he shouted over the noise. They were to go with the study of *The Tempest*."

3. **`tempest`, second paragraph: "He began with a principle."** Pearce's notes open
   there, with Auden already speaking, and he says they cover the part of the lecture that
   was on the play (p. 148). Proposed: "The notes open on a principle."

4. **`falstaff`, fifth paragraph: "In a world like that, Auden ended, …"** It is the notes
   that end there. The next paragraph of the page says the lecture went on (p. 148).
   Proposed: "In a world like that, he said, Falstaff cannot last, …"

5. **`first`, the figure's `taught` list, and the second caveat, "for eleven".** `pascal`
   is now one of the eleven. What Pearce records is two remarks in the lecture on
   *Othello*, where Auden used Pascal's two kinds of mind for Othello and for Iago
   (pp. 141-42). That is a use of the book in a class. It is not "a class, a question or
   the examination" on it. Either widen the key by a few words or count ten.

6. **`othello`, note: "The play, he said as he pointed, …"** No pointing is described.
   In Pearce, Auden's own words have a short phrase telling the class that they can see
   it for themselves (p. 141). Desdemona also comes last of the six as he named them.
   Proposed: "The play, he said, is first of all about a society in layers. The Duke,
   Brabantio, Roderigo, Cassio, Lodovico and Desdemona are the upper crust, as the board
   showed."

7. **`love`, on the phonograph: "He played the love duet from the second act of
   *Tristan*."; and the door under it, "The words of the duet he played, …"** Pearce names
   the love duet and the opera and no act (p. 138). Which stretch of the duet was on the
   record is not recorded.
   Proposed: "He played the love duet from *Tristan*." For the door: "Words from the love
   duet, where each takes the other’s name."

8. **`falstaff`, third paragraph: "The play’s real schemer …" and "… he is Falstaff’s
   best pupil."** Both say a little more than Pearce. He has the Prince as a real schemer
   under the surface, who studies Falstaff as a devoted pupil would and learns well
   (p. 145). Nobody is ranked.
   Proposed: "The Prince, he went on, is a schemer under the charm, one of the most
   hateful people in Shakespeare: …" and "… and he studies Falstaff as a good pupil
   studies his master."

9. **`first`, note: "… the categories, definitions and diagrams were his charms against
   it"** Pearce is speaking of the ones in Auden's published work, the essays and the
   short pieces most of all (p. 132). As the sentence stands, a reader takes it to be
   about the blackboards.
   Proposed: "… and the categories, definitions and diagrams of his published prose were
   charms against it."

10. **`after`, second paragraph: "… and is sure he has them almost word for word."**
    Pearce says he is sure he has given them verbatim (p. 157). The "almost" is the
    page's. Proposed: "… and is sure he has them word for word."

11. **`since`, second paragraph: "(pp. 156–57)", and the scene's `src`, "pp. 132 and
    156–57".** The remark about talent, Pearce's own judgment and the borrowed words are
    all on p. 157. Page 156 is the reading of Wordsworth. Also "he still catches himself":
    Pearce says there have been times.
    Proposed: "(p. 157)", and "… and he says he has caught himself using Auden’s words in
    his own classes".

12. **`since`, the first voice: `of: 'to Pearce, in the next seat'`.** Pearce says only
    that the teaching assistant on his right called Auden that (p. 132). He does not say
    it was said to him. He also gives the man's pen name and not the name Millar, so the
    name on the line is the site's, from other sources.
    Proposed `of`: "the teaching assistant in the next seat, as Pearce tells it".

13. **`door`, second paragraph: "Donald Pearce was a graduate student of twenty-three."**
    "Graduate student" is not in the essay, and the words give no hit in the search text
    of the book. Pearce says he was 23 (p. 132) and that he had just been told he would
    assist in a big American literature class (p. 133). If the fact comes from another
    source, name it in the `src`. If not, proposed: "Donald Pearce was twenty-three, and
    had just been taken on to help teach a big course in American literature."

14. **`exam`, second paragraph: "A committee of students called it impossible, and then
    inhumane."** In Pearce the two words come together, in one breath (p. 153). The "then"
    came from a loose phrase in an earlier state of this report.
    Proposed: "A committee of students called it impossible and inhumane."

15. **`tempest`, third paragraph: "What Ariel’s song says is the governing idea of this
    play"** Pearce has it as the central theme of both sea scenes, the one in *Pericles*
    and the one in *The Tempest* (p. 149).
    Proposed: "What Ariel’s song says is the theme of both sea scenes: …"

16. **`othello`, Othello followed across the schemes: "He is naive so as not to know how
    Venice looks down on him."** Pearce's word is "inferiority", and he does not say in
    whose eyes (pp. 141-42). The page's reading may be right. It is a reading.
    Proposed: "He is naive so as not to know his own “inferiority.”"

17. **Fourth caveat: "Pearce’s pages for the lectures on Kierkegaard, on love and on
    *Othello* could be read for this site only in the short extracts …"** The same is true
    of the first two pages of the lecture on *Henry IV* (pp. 144-45), from its opening
    down to the feudal lords. Proposed: add "and the opening of the lecture on *Henry
    IV*".

Three more, found in the 07:49:42 version and mended in the next. All three are a single
word.

18. **`tempest`, note after the board: "Then Auden talked for a while about Valéry, …"**
    Pearce's word is "briefly" (p. 152). "For a while" was this report's own wording, in
    the first state of Should fix 1, and was wrong. Proposed: "Then Auden talked briefly about Valéry, and of

19. **`love`, first paragraph: "He also urged *The Allegory of Love*, …"** In Pearce it

20. **`falstaff`, third paragraph: "… because as king he will need it"** In Pearce the
    Prince studies such conduct because a king has to understand it (p. 145). He is not
    said to need it for his own use. Proposed: "… because a king must understand it".

2. **The recess and the phonograph** (p. 137). Often, Pearce says, the recess went on the

### Scenes and remarks

4. **A second witness for the slippers** (p. 135). In the lecture on Augustine and
   Descartes, Pearce has Auden cross the room in what he thinks were bedroom slippers and
   touch, without looking, some handwritten pages on the table. The page has the slippers
   from Chapman only, at the first class.
5. **Groddeck in the classroom** (p. 147). When Auden called the belly a mock pregnancy
   he named Georg Groddeck as the man who had studied such cases. The `papers` scene now
   has Farnan saying that Auden recommended Groddeck's book. Pearce is the witness that
   the name was used in a lecture.
6. **Falstaff's type** (p. 144). Auden called him a feeling-intuitive type, in Jung's
   terms, which is where the reading of him starts. Also left out: Falstaff as an
   opportunist who has moved to London so as to be on the winning side (p. 146), the King
   as a hollow man who pleads with his son for feeling (pp. 147-48), and the Prince's
   need of his own opposite (p. 148).
7. **Pearce's own summing-up of the Shakespeare lectures** (p. 139). For Auden, he says,
   each play was a case history in psychology, and Shakespeare a "psycho-historian". The
   page gives the first half of this thought, that Auden kept to minds and society.
8. **What Pearce answered about the reaper** (p. 155). Asked what the girl was a symbol
   of, he offered the peasantry, or a rural England that Wordsworth feared was passing.
   The page goes from the question straight to Auden's answer.
9. **More of Auden's answer** (pp. 155-57). Reaching one's daemon is an act of faith, like
   trying to recall a friend's telephone number without looking it up. The girl is also
   what Jung calls the anima. The poet's boast in the second stanza is true of every poet
   at some time. And the irony at the end: in 1805 Wordsworth's lyric gift was about to
   fail.
10. **The Tempest, small things** (pp. 149-53). Shakespeare as the mind at its most whole,
    a remark Pearce found thrilling (p. 149). What Prospero brings home is the
    philosopher's stone of the fairy tale (pp. 150-51). *Antony and Cleopatra* as the
    second contrast to the young lovers (p. 151). A line of Blake's for Caliban (p. 151).
    A living author cannot be judged for good, and Shakespeare can be seen whole, the
    last four plays as one unit (p. 152). And the name Auden gave to the temptation in
    his last question: for Shakespeare it would have been Baconianism (pp. 152-53).
11. **Othello, small things** (pp. 141-43). Othello as a self-made man, so trusting as
    to be gullible. Cassio as no fraternity boy. Iago's one line of Scripture, which
    Auden quoted, and his way of lecturing on virtue like a theologian.
12. **Left out, and rightly.** Pearce's guess that love came up so often because Auden was
    at work on *The Sea and the Mirror* (p. 137), which research note E marks as
    hindsight. And Auden's remark that Lewis had been his teacher at Oxford (p. 137),
    which research note A marks as doubtful.
