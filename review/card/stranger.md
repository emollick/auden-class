# What a stranger gets from the link card

I looked only at the two pictures and the text I was given. Nothing was refused. I do not write out the two lines of verse; I call them the first line and the second line.

**In short.** The card tells me this is a site about a literature course W. H. Auden taught at Michigan in 1941, and that it shows verse with its beat marked. It does not tell me who made it, how much there is, or what the red marks mean, and at chat size it does not tell me the verse is Shakespeare's. I would open it. The one thing I would change is the description, above all its last clause.

## 1. What I think the link leads to

Somebody's own website (the netlify.app address says a personal project, not a university or a publisher) that rebuilds a course called "Fate and the Individual", which the poet W. H. Auden taught at the University of Michigan in the autumn of 1941. I expect a quiet, bookish site in the same cream paper and serif type: an account of the class meetings drawn from what his students wrote down, then his reading list, and for each work a few passages set large with the stresses and rhymes marked, as the Shakespeare lines are on the card. I half expect to be able to switch the markings on and off.

The card does not say who made it, what sources it rests on, or how long it is. I guess that "english-135" in the address is the course number.

## 2. What I can and cannot read

**Timeline width (506)**

- Easy: the heading, the two lines of verse, and which letters are heavy.
- Readable with a little attention: the italic line naming Auden, Michigan and fall 1941.
- Readable only if I look for it: the small "SHAKESPEARE The Tempest" label, the smallest type on the card.
- The marks are sharp. I can see that the first mark under the first line is a solid red dot and the first mark under the second line is a red ring.
- The dark title box sits just under the red ring, close but touching nothing. It looks too short to hold the full title at that height, so I expect the real label to run wider or to be cut short; I cannot tell which from this picture. Either way it repeats the picture: on X I read the course name twice, and Auden and 1941 twice.

**Chat preview (300)**

- Easy: the heading and the two lines of verse. The heavy and light letters still show.
- At the limit: the italic line. I can pick out Auden, Michigan and 1941, but I had already read it at the larger size, so I may be reading from memory.
- Not readable: "SHAKESPEARE The Tempest". Nothing in the title or description names Shakespeare either, so a reader who does not know the lines could take them for Auden's own.
- The dots show as specks and the red ones as red specks. I cannot tell the ring from the solid dot.

## 3. The marks and the heavy letters

The heavy letters I take to be the stressed syllables, where the voice comes down when the line is spoken. I get that within a couple of seconds, because the weight changes inside words, at the syllable break. For an instant I thought of "bionic reading", the trick of bolding the front of every word; that passes once I see short words left wholly light.

The rule under each line, with a dot hung beneath each heavy syllable, I read as the beat laid out in time, like a ruler or a drum pattern. That comes across without a key.

The red marks I cannot read at a glance. Both stand at the very start of a line; the solid one is under a heavy syllable and the ring under a light one. My first guess was a play-head, as on an audio player, and the word "recorded" in the description encouraged it. After counting the dots, my second guess is that red marks a place where the beat and the spoken stress disagree. I am not confident of it. It did not put me off: it is the only colour on the card and it made me look twice.

## 4. Would I open it?

Yes. I like poetry, and for a reader like me the card works: Auden's name, an exact place and season, and a picture that shows the thing itself (a famous line with its beat marked) where many cards show a logo or a stock photograph. It is calm and well set, and it looks unlike what surrounds it in a timeline.

What puts me off: there is no name behind it and the address is a free hosting one, so I cannot tell a scholar's work from a weekend project; nothing says how much there is; the description took me two readings; and nothing says why this course, of all courses, is worth a site. A reader with no interest in Auden or in verse is given no reason to stop.

## 5. Words I stumble on

- "the afternoons his students recorded": "recorded" first made me think of sound recordings. In 1941 it probably means notes or diaries, but I had to stop and reason that out. "Afternoons" for class meetings also took a moment, though I like it.
- "each opened at a few passages": a second's pause, then I see a book opened at a page. A little mannered, and clear once I have it.
- "the figures and the words drawn on the lines": the phrase I stumble on hardest. "Figures" beside "drawn" reads first as little drawings and only then as figures of speech. And I cannot work out what it means to draw "the words" on lines that are already made of words.
- "Auden's course of 1941", in the title: coming after "Fate", "course" can be taken for a moment as the path of his life that year. The italic line on the picture puts it right.
- The description opens by saying again what the title has just said, adding only the university. The words that explain the picture come last, where a preview that cuts long descriptions would lose them.

Nothing sounds like an advertisement: there is no "explore" or "discover", and if anything the text undersells, since it never says what kind of thing is on offer. Nothing sounds machine-written. It reads like one careful, bookish person who has packed too much into a single sentence.

## 6. One thing I would change

The description, and in it above all the last clause. Open with what the title has not said (the university can stay; "Auden's course" and "1941" need not come twice), say plainly how the students recorded the classes, and end with a clause I can follow, for example "with the beat, the rhymes and the figures of speech marked on the lines". Either say what "the words" means or drop it.
