## The head of the page

[title]
The catalogue and the duet

[where]
*Don Giovanni*, Act 1

[about] (under “The passage”)
Mozart’s opera to Lorenzo Da Ponte’s Italian words was first given in Prague in 1787. In Act 1 Don Giovanni slips away from Donna Elvira, a lady he has deserted, and leaves his servant Leporello to tell her everything. Leporello reads to her from the list he keeps of his master’s women. Later the Don comes upon a peasant wedding, sends the others off, tells the bride, Zerlina, that he will marry her at once in a small country house of his, and begins the duet.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden reads the Don as a man with one end, chosen at will: to seduce all the women there are. He sets him against the Duke in Verdi’s *Rigoletto*, a libertine of the senses, who goes after any pretty girl and turns his eyes from a plain, older woman. The Don’s looks should tell the audience nothing, and stamps would have done as well as women. He can be understood only together with Leporello.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
not sensual but arithmetical; his satisfaction lies in adding one more name to his list which is kept for him by Leporello

[view.source]
“Balaam and His Ass,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 119–20

### Tab “Count” (beat)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Count

[plates[0].lenses[0].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
ten syllables by the Italian count, with the accent fixed on the third, the sixth and the ninth

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Each line has ten syllables by the Italian count, with the accent on the third, the sixth and the ninth. Two lines stop on the ninth. Italian reference books call this line catchy, its regular cadence monotonous but effective, and its marked rhythm fit for verse to be sung. Da Ponte gives that cadence to a count. In the four lines of totals the first accent always falls on a country and the last on the end of a number. There, only <b>già</b>, already, takes an accent and is neither country nor figure. No woman is named anywhere in the song. Auden calls the Don’s pleasure arithmetical.

[plates[0].lenses[0].points[0].say] (a numbered remark)
The last vowel of the first word and the vowel of the second count as one syllable of the measure. Italian verse counts a vowel that ends one word and a vowel that begins the next in this way, even across a comma, and both vowels are still said. It happens thirteen times in these eight lines.

[plates[0].lenses[0].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)
The verb, *amò*, is stressed on its last syllable, the sixth of the line. The article after it, *il*, begins with a vowel and is counted with that syllable.

[plates[0].lenses[0].points[2].say] (a numbered remark)
The line stops on its last accent, the ninth syllable. Italian names a line by the place of its last accent, so this is still a line of ten. The last line of the count stops the same way, and the two rhyme.

[plates[0].lenses[0].points[3].say] (a numbered remark)
The first accent falls on the country, the second on the hundreds, the third on the last part of the number. The next line is built the same way.

[plates[0].lenses[0].points[4].say] (a numbered remark)
Two countries share this line, and each takes an accent. The hundred that begins the line falls outside the three accents. The last two vowels of the second country are counted as one syllable here.

[plates[0].lenses[0].points[5].say] (a numbered remark)
The second accent falls on the word for already. The thousand follows at once with a stress of its own, off the measure.

[plates[0].lenses[0].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage, and the whole song for the absence of names. The line of ten syllables and its cadence: Treccani’s *Enciclopedia on line* and the *Enciclopedia Italiana* (1931), under *Decasillabo*. How Italian verse counts syllables and names a line, and this line’s fitness for music: the *Enciclopedia dell’Italiano* (2011), under *Versificazione* and *Sinalefe*. Auden on the Don’s arithmetic: *The Dyer’s Hand*, p. 119.

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Each stanza leaves its first line unrhymed, rhymes its two middle lines and stops short on its last. The two short lines rhyme across the gap: <b>me</b> with <b>tre</b>, me with three. In the first stanza <b>mio</b>, my, ends the line about the master’s loves, and <b>fatt’io</b>, made by me, ends the line about the catalogue. Auden writes that the servant keeps the list and that the Don is never seen to look at it. In the second stanza two totals rhyme because both end in the word for one, and the last total ends on the rhyme for <b>me</b>. The figures appear to be chosen for the verse.

[plates[0].lenses[1].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage, and the lines themselves. Auden on the keeper of the list and on who enjoys it: *The Dyer’s Hand*, p. 120. That a line may stop on its accented syllable: the *Enciclopedia dell’Italiano* (2011), under *Versificazione*.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
A <b>catalogo</b>, the dictionary says, is an ordered list of objects of one kind, such as books, works of art or goods. Auden says that stamps would have served the Don as well as women. Leporello calls the women <b>belle</b>, beauties, in the plural. Just before the song he shows Elvira the list and says it is full of their names. He then reads out countries and totals, and not one name. The word <b>già</b>, already, gives the last total as the count so far. Auden writes of the Don Juan of legend that any fondness would make a name of a number on the list.

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[0].note] (a note on the word)

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[1].gloss] (set under the word)

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[1].note] (a note on the word)

### Tab “English” (compare)

[plates[0].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
English

[plates[0].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
The English beside the Italian appears to be made for singing. Its full lines keep three beats and rhyme on two syllables where they can, and it changes the count. Italy has seven hundred, Germany eight, and France loses its hundred and shares Turkey’s ninety-one. The thousand and three for Spain is kept, with its rhyme. Auden and Chester Kallman, who translated this opera themselves, write that a translation must fit the music as sung, and that such rhymes, mostly comic in English, can help in this song. The Italian figures do not agree either: they come to 2,065, and earlier in the scene Leporello says eighteen hundred.

[plates[0].lenses[3].ties[0].say] (a numbered remark)

[plates[0].lenses[3].ties[1].say] (a numbered remark)

[plates[0].lenses[3].ties[2].say] (a numbered remark)
Six hundred and forty in the Italian. Seven hundred in the English.

[plates[0].lenses[3].ties[3].say] (a numbered remark)
Two hundred and thirty-one in the Italian. The English says eight, or eight hundred if the hundred of the line before is carried over.

[plates[0].lenses[3].ties[4].say] (a numbered remark)

[plates[0].lenses[3].ties[5].say] (a numbered remark)

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The noun <b>conquista</b>, conquest, is a word of war, used also of love. The first two lines give the Don’s reason for winning old women: the <b>piacere</b>, the pleasure, of putting them on the <b>lista</b>, the list. Auden says as much: the Don is satisfied when the list is one name longer. The next two lines name one <b>passion</b>, the young <b>principiante</b>, the beginner. Auden writes that the Don Juan of legend means to be the first man each woman has known. The last lines leave one condition, the <b>gonnella</b>, a skirt, which Italian puts for the woman herself.

[plates[1].lenses[0].notes[1].gloss] (set under the word)

[plates[1].lenses[0].notes[1].note] (a note on the word)
The dictionary gives the noun first for conquering by arms and then, in familiar use, for a success in love and for the person won, and it quotes this line and the next.

[plates[1].lenses[0].notes[2].note] (a note on the word)

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[1].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)
anaphora

[plates[1].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)
the same words at the head of clause after clause

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Three clauses in a row begin with the same two words, <b>se sia</b>, whether she is. The figure is anaphora. A different quality follows each time: rich, ugly, beautiful. All three hang on one verb, which says he is not particular, and so make one list of things that do not count. Then <b>Purchè</b>, provided that, brings the one condition, a skirt. Auden quotes these three lines after naming the end the Don has chosen, and says no man so indifferent to looks is met in life. Of the Don Juan of legend he writes that what counts about a woman is only that she belongs to the class of women.

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The first four lines are two couplets, pairs of rhymed lines, and a rhyme that comes back at once makes its two lines one unit. The first couplet rhymes <b>conquista</b> with <b>lista</b>, conquest with list. Leporello has made this rhyme before, asking his master about a new conquest so as to put her on the list. Auden writes that Leporello keeps the list and the Don only tells him the newest name. In the last four lines <b>picca</b>, insists, and <b>ricca</b>, rich, rhyme inside one line, then <b>bella</b>, beautiful, rhymes with <b>gonnella</b>, skirt. The last line stops short and finds its rhyme in an earlier stanza.

The marks on the passage, and the rest of the song for the last rhyme. The same two rhyme words come in Leporello’s talk with his master, in English prose at [Act 1, Scene 4](#/read/don-giovanni/act-1:152). The couplet as one unit: Baum’s *Principles of English Versification*, chapter 4; rhyme inside a line: the *Enciclopedia dell’Italiano* (2011), under *Rima*. Auden on the keeper of the list: *The Dyer’s Hand*, p. 120.

English

[plates[1].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
The English keeps ugly, has <b>pretty</b> for beautiful, and makes the skirt a <b>petticoat</b>. ### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The Don’s four lines rhyme by turns, and so do Zerlina’s, on new sounds. Then come four single lines, and each of hers rhymes with his line before it: <b>Masetto</b> answers <b>diletto</b>, beloved, and <b>forte</b>, strong, answers <b>sorte</b>, lot. The last three lines are for both, and their last word, <b>amor</b>, love, takes the rhyme of her <b>cor</b>, heart, and her <b>ancor</b>, yet. Her part goes from a stanza of her own to the answering half of his rhyme to a shared line. Auden, writing of the legend, says the seduction is to be a triumph of the seducer’s will.

[plates[2].lenses[0].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage, and the singers’ names in the Italian. What counts as a rhyme in Italian: the *Enciclopedia dell’Italiano* (2011), under *Rima*. Auden on the seducer’s will: “The Greeks and Us,” in *Forewords and Afterwords*, p. 24, where he speaks of the legend of Don Juan.

### Tab “Voices” (sentence)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Voices

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Six of the Don’s seven verbs are futures, orders or urgings: <b>dirai</b>, you will say, <b>Vieni</b>, come. A future, the reference book says, can carry an order, so all six tell her what is to happen. Three of Zerlina’s eight verbs are conditionals, the mood of a softened wish: <b>Vorrei</b>, I would like. Her other five are presents: her heart trembles, he may fool her, she is no longer strong. This printing puts her stanza in brackets, its mark for words said aside, and in it she speaks of him, not to him. Then both sing his kind of verb, <b>Andiamo</b>, let us go. Auden counts Don Giovanni among opera’s great roles, each a state of passion and will.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Just before the duet the Don tells Zerlina he will marry her this instant, in a small country house of his. Each line of his stanza has a word of place: <b>Là</b>, there, twice, then <b>non è lontano</b>, it is not far, then <b>di qui</b>, from here. Giving the hand seals a pact and the yes is a bride’s word, so his first two lines describe a wedding. Zerlina is already a bride. Earlier in the act Donna Elvira says he called her his wife and left her after three days. Auden writes of this Don that one time with a woman is enough for him.

### Tab “English” (compare)

[plates[2].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
English

[plates[2].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
The English keeps the hand, the yes and Zerlina’s doubt. In the Italian Masetto moves her to pity; in the English he <b>claims my duty</b>. In the Italian she says quick, or soon, and that she is no longer strong; in the English she yields to <b>fate</b>. The Italian word nearest to fate is <b>sorte</b>, and it is his: the lot he says he will change. Each new word answers a rhyme of two syllables: <b>beauty</b> then duty, <b>state, love</b> then fate, love. Auden and Kallman write that such rhymes are mostly comic in English, and that a tender scene is better left unrhymed than rhymed absurdly.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
When Leporello, in his first song, says he wants to play the gentleman, the audience laughs, Auden notes, because he has nothing of the master about him. By the end, he thinks, the joke turns round, and the servant may have been the master from the start. The list is in Leporello’s keeping, and without it the Don would have no reason for being. The Don is never shown looking at the list or enjoying it. His part is to bring the newest name. Auden also calls him a figure of myth: there are promiscuous men, but none in life to whom the women’s bodies make no difference at all.

[auden[0].source]
“Balaam and His Ass,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 118 and 120–21

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Writing of the legend in general, Auden pairs Don Juan with Tristan and Isolde as two modern myths of love that the Greeks could not have had. What matters to Don Juan about a woman is only that she is one. The list is what makes the myth foreign to the Greeks. He means to be the first man every woman has known, and each is the next number in an endless series. Time is his enemy because it repeats. Auden says both myths are sicknesses that only a Christian imagination could have bred.

[auden[1].source]
“The Greeks and Us,” in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), pp. 23–25

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden, writing on Byron’s poem, says it is his will, and no bent of his nature, that makes the Don Juan of legend promiscuous. He has made seduction his calling. Any fondness would make a name of a number on the list, so the calling demands that he give up love. The legend therefore needs him to be a defiant unbeliever, the demonic reverse of the saint who denies himself and prefers no neighbor to another, so that he can be charitable to every one. Byron’s hero has five women in two years, which is little beside the thousand and three in Spain that Leporello reads out.

[auden[2].source]
“Don Juan,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 392

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In *The Enchafèd Flood* Auden puts the Don of Da Ponte and Mozart among the heroes who leave everyone else behind. A seducer looks like a man absorbed in women, and Auden says this one is not. One time is enough for him, and he does not want them to remember him. Some do, and that brings him down. What holds his interest is the tally itself, how many names his private book has reached.

[auden[3].source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), p. 107

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden quotes Shaw, who sided with Beethoven against Mozart for giving what is glad, tender and noble in him to a libertine in his cups, a foolish peasant girl and a fine lady of the usual kind. Auden answers that Mozart was right, since such feelings are everybody’s. Opera, he says, copies human willfulness: people insist on having their feelings, whatever it costs them. Each great role, Don Giovanni first in his list, is one state of passion and will. In song, he believes, the listener hears syllables and not the words of a poem. For that reason he is in general against performing operas in translation.

[auden[4].source]
“Notes on Music and Opera,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 469–70 and 473

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
With Chester Kallman, Auden put the words of this opera into English. They write that a translation has to match the rhythm of the music as sung, which is not the rhythm of the verse as spoken. Italian rhymes mostly on two syllables. English seldom does, and then the effect is usually comic, which in Leporello’s catalogue song can help. A librettist’s own verses, they say, have only to inspire the composer, and before Wagner and the Verdi of the middle years they followed conventions that any able writer of verse could learn.

[auden[5].source]
W. H. Auden and Chester Kallman, “Translating Opera Libretti,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 484–85, 488 and 493

[auden[6].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In June 1941, a few months before the course began, Auden reviewed Denis de Rougemont’s *Love in the Western World*. He says there that the legend of Tristan calls up its reverse, the legend that reaches its height in Mozart’s *Don Giovanni*, where the present moment is everything. For Tristan time is an evil to be endured and for Don Juan an evil to be destroyed, so that the one is like a man who kills himself and the other like a man who kills.

[auden[6].source]
“Eros and Agape,” *The Nation*, 28 June 1941; reprinted in his *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 137–40

[auden[7].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Donald Pearce, who took the course, recalls that at the interview for admission Auden listed some of the authors and added that there would be operas too, in score and libretto: *Carmen*, *Don Giovanni* and *Tristan and Isolde*.

[auden[7].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), p. 133

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
The Italian is the Florence printing of 1867 as Italian Wikisource gives it, with its slips, such as *Zar.* for Zerlina. In the second passage one line begins with *but* where the Italian printed with the English has *his*, and the last line is not the one usually sung.

[caveats[1]]
The stanza breaks were made for this page and follow the rhymes. The stresses marked are those of the verse read aloud. In the opera words and lines are repeated, and Auden and Kallman say that the rhythm of verse as sung is not its rhythm as spoken.

[caveats[2]]
Two places in the act go against Auden’s account. Just before the second passage Leporello says his master wants the plump in winter and the thin in summer. Later the Don tells Leporello to add ten names by morning to what he calls his list.

[caveats[3]]
Auden’s sentences on the first lover, the class of women and giving up love are about the legend, not this opera. He held that opera verse before Wagner was a convention, heard as syllables, so the close reading is this page’s own. His 1941 review was read only in extracts.

[caveats[4]]
The English beside the Italian is an unsigned version printed for an opera house. It is free and appears to be made to fit the music. From the wedding on it numbers the scenes one behind the Italian. The first word of Zerlina’s last line can mean quick or soon.
