## The head of the page

[title]
Racine’s line

[where]
*Phèdre*, Act 1, Scenes 1 and 3, and Act 2, Scene 5

[about] (under “The passage”)
Thésée, king of Athens, has been gone over six months, and his wife Phèdre is dying of a sickness she will not name. In the first passage his son Hippolyte, leaving to seek him, is asked by his tutor Théramène what drives him away; he answers in three lines, and Théramène begins to reply. In the second Phèdre has let her nurse Œnone name Hippolyte, her stepson, and tells how her love began. In the third Thésée is reported dead; Hippolyte says he may live, and she answers.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
For Auden French classical tragedy belongs with opera. Its verse is a different language from prose, kept far from ordinary speech, and he thought nobody could know *Phèdre* without hearing a great performance of it. The noble poetry Racine gives his sinner he called a conjuring trick: told by a journalist, her story would be a pathological case. He also admitted that his English ear heard the French line wrongly.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
I must confess that French classical tragedy strikes me as being opera for the unmusical.

[view.source]
“Writing” and “The Globe,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 25 and 176; “Un Homme d’Esprit,” in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), pp. 358–59

### Tab “Count” (beat)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Count

[plates[0].lenses[0].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
twelve counted syllables, cut after the sixth; an accent on the sixth and on the twelfth

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
French verse is measured by count. Each line here has twelve syllables, and the voice rests after the sixth, at the cut. A final <i>e</i> counts before a consonant, as in <b>fille</b>. It is dropped before a vowel, as in <b>Phèdre ici</b>, and left out at the end of a line. A history of French verse says only the sixth and twelfth syllables must carry an accent. Auden found verse measured by count hard for an English ear, but in Racine, he wrote, at least every line has twelve syllables. In the third line the cut falls between two names: Phèdre’s father ends the first half, her mother the second.

[plates[0].lenses[0].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)

[plates[0].lenses[0].points[2].say] (a numbered remark)
The cut falls after her father’s name. Her mother’s ends the line.

[plates[0].lenses[0].points[3].say] (a numbered remark)
Théramène answers from here. His first word means I understand.

[plates[0].lenses[0].points[4].say] (a numbered remark)

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[0].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)
periphrasis

[plates[0].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)
the long way round: a description put in the place of a name

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Asked what drives him away, Hippolyte answers without the name <i>Phèdre</i>. He says whose daughter she is, and the description fills the third line: rhetoric calls this periphrasis. The play’s list of persons describes her in the same words. Théramène, in the last two lines, supplies the name. Later the play says what each parent was. In Act 1, Scene 3 Phèdre recalls the <i>égarements</i>, strayings, into which love threw her mother; in Act 4 she says her father Minos judges the dead. The verb before the names is <b>ont envoyé</b>: the gods sent her. Racine’s preface says her destiny and the anger of the gods draw her into her passion.

### Tab “English” (compare)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
English

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Boswell writes the unrhymed five-beat line of Elizabethan drama, and it is shorter than Racine’s. Hippolyte’s three lines have thirty-six counted syllables; Boswell’s version of them has nineteen. The line of two names becomes one word, <b>Phaedra</b>, and where Théramène says <i>Phèdre</i> Boswell writes <b>the queen</b>. Auden wrote that English verse never lets the ear forget everyday speech, and that French poetry takes pride in standing apart from it: the verse and the prose of French drama, he said, are two languages.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[0].say] (a numbered remark)

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[1].say] (a numbered remark)
Twelve syllables of parentage become one name.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[2].say] (a numbered remark)
Racine’s tutor answers with her name. Boswell has used the name already, and his tutor gives her rank.

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[1].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
asyndeton

[plates[1].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
clauses set side by side with no joining word between them

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The first line holds three clauses with nothing to join them: I saw him, I blushed, I turned pale. The verbs are <b>vis</b>, <b>rougis</b>, <b>pâlis</b>, and each closes a group of three syllables. A handbook of rhetoric calls this asyndeton and says it often gives a hurried or vehement effect; its example is Caesar’s <i>I came, I saw, I conquered</i>. Three lines down Racine does the opposite. Her body goes cold and burns, <b>et transir et brûler</b>, and each verb has its own <i>et</i>, the French for “and.” Littré’s dictionary says <i>et</i> repeated gives a phrase more force. The play, Racine writes in his preface, shows the passions only to show the disorder they cause.

[plates[1].lenses[0].parts[4].label] (in the key)

### Tab “Halves” (beat)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Halves

[plates[1].lenses[1].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
twelve counted syllables, cut after the sixth; an accent on the sixth and on the twelfth

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Phèdre lists what happened at the sight of him: a blush, a pallor, trouble in her soul, sight gone, speech gone, her body cold and burning. In all six lines a phrase ends on the sixth syllable, where the measure asks the sense to pause. The third line gives one loss to each half; the first and fourth fall into four groups of three. Auden noted that French poets work to hold the pause to a few fixed places, and English poets to shift it. He wrote too that a journalist’s telling would show Phèdre for a pathological case, and that her poetry is a splendid robe over nakedness.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The last two lines name the cause. <b>Reconnus</b> is <i>recognized</i>, known again or known by its signs: earlier in the scene Phèdre spoke of the hatred of Venus and of what love did to her mother and her sister. <b>Feux</b>, fires, is the poets’ word for the passion of love; the line before ends on <b>brûler</b>, to burn. <b>Sang</b>, blood, means race or family, and <b>poursuit</b> is pursues or persecutes: Venus’s fires are the unavoidable torments of a bloodline she pursues. Auden set this Venus against Euripides’ Aphrodite, who cares about fish and birds as much as about people. Racine’s, he wrote, cares nothing for animals or the lower orders.

### Tab “Ear” (beat)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Ear

[plates[2].lenses[0].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
twelve counted syllables, cut after the sixth; an accent on the sixth and on the twelfth

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Auden quotes the seventh line. It reminded him of the line on the Assyrian in Byron’s “The Destruction of Sennacherib”: an English ear, he wrote, hears most such lines as anapests, two off-beats then a beat, which the English associate with light verse. The line does fall into four threes, a stop after each: I see him; I speak to him; and my heart; I lose my way. A history of French verse calls threes the commonest division and speaks of the line’s pendulum-like swing. But only the accents on the sixth and twelfth syllables are fixed: four of the six lines before open with a group of two. Auden knew the rules and called his hearing wrong.

[plates[2].lenses[0].points[0].say] (a numbered remark)
A group of two. The stress falls on the second syllable, where an anapest has no beat.

[plates[2].lenses[0].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)
The final *e* runs into the next vowel across the question mark and is not counted.

[plates[2].lenses[0].points[2].say] (a numbered remark)

[plates[2].lenses[0].points[3].say] (a numbered remark)

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The lines rhyme in pairs of two kinds. <b>Morts</b> and <b>bords</b> end on the accented syllable: a masculine rhyme. <b>Renvoie</b> and <b>proie</b> add a mute <i>e</i> after the accent, a feminine rhyme. The kinds alternate through all 827 couplets of the play. A history of French verse says the rule was urged when poems were meant to be sung, and kept after verse parted from music. Auden wrote that sound has always counted for much more in traditional French verse than in English. The third pair rhymes <b>en vous</b>, in you, the stepson, with <b>époux</b>, the husband; the last rhymes <b>m’égare</b>, I lose my way, with <b>se déclare</b>, shows itself.

[plates[2].lenses[1].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage, and a count of the couplets in the French text of the play. L. E. Kastner, *A History of French Versification*, pp. 39 and 63 to 67, on the two kinds of rhyme and the rule that they alternate. Auden on sound in French verse: *The Dyer’s Hand*, p. 24. Littré under *égarer* and *déclarer*.

Break

[plates[2].lenses[2].name] (the name of the figure)
aposiopesis

[plates[2].lenses[2].plain] (the figure in plain words)
a sentence broken off in the middle, with the rest left unsaid

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The verb <i>voir</i>, to see, comes four times. No one sees the shore of the dead twice, and Thésée has seen it. <b>Que dis-je</b>, what am I saying: he is not dead, he breathes in his son. Then the seeing is hers: <b>je crois voir</b>, I think I see; <b>Je le vois</b>, I see him. A clause begins <i>and my heart</i> and stops: rhetoric calls this aposiopesis. Her next words are <b>Je m’égare</b>, I lose my way.

### Tab “English” (compare)

[plates[2].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
English

[plates[2].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
The last two lines differ in who acts. In Racine the subject of the last verb is <b>ma folle ardeur</b>, my mad passion, and the verb is <b>se déclare</b>: Littré quotes this line under the sense <i>to be made manifest</i>. Boswell makes her the subject: <b>I cannot hide my passion</b>. Racine writes in his preface that she would rather die than declare her passion. In his line the passion declares itself, <b>malgré moi</b>, against her will, and Hippolyte, answering, takes it for love of Thésée.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden calls it a heresy to want poetry to come as near to music as it can. The poets of France have been readier for it than the English, he says, perhaps because effects of sound have always counted for more in their verse. The English have wanted poetic speech kept near everyday talk, and French poetry has prided itself on standing apart. He cites from Valéry a report that the actress Rachel ranged over two octaves in reciting; an actress who treated Shakespeare so would be laughed at. Reading Racine to oneself, he says, is like studying an opera score with barely the skill to play or sing it.

[auden[0].source]
“Writing,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 24–25

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Reading the *Hippolytus* of Euripides, Auden says, he finds its world akin to Shakespeare’s, while Racine’s world, like opera’s, is another planet. The Greek Aphrodite cares about fish and birds as much as about people. Racine’s Venus cares nothing for animals, and nothing for the lower orders either. Nobody in Racine could be imagined sneezing: his world has no weather and no nature. So the passions that eat up his characters can live only on the stage, where the speech and gesture of the players give them a body. Opera works the same way, but a speaking voice cannot match a great singing voice with an orchestra behind it.

[auden[1].source]
“Writing,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 25–26

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden doubts that tragedy can fully succeed among Christians, who do not hold that whoever suffers must be guilty. A noble and innocent sufferer gives pathos, and there can be no noble sinner, since sin makes a person ignoble. Shakespeare and Racine both meet the difficulty by having the sinner speak noble poetry, and both must privately have known it for a conjuring trick. The stories of Oedipus and Hippolytus would stay as tragic in a journalist’s telling as in the Greek poets’. Let him tell Phèdre’s, and she is a pathological case. Her poetry does not come from a noble nature: it is a splendid robe over nakedness.

[auden[2].source]
“The Globe,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 176

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Writing on Marianne Moore, Auden says an English ear can hardly take in verse that counts syllables and ignores accents and feet. The French twelve-syllable line is his example, and the line he quotes is the seventh of the third passage here: try as he may, it calls up Byron’s line on the Assyrian. In Racine, he adds, the lines at least all have twelve syllables.

[auden[3].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
our ear cannot help hearing most alexandrines as anapaestic verse which, in English poetry, we associate with light verse

[auden[3].source]
“Two Bestiaries,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 297

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Introducing Valéry, Auden doubts that a Frenchman ever learns to hear English verse, and is sure the English never hear French verse rightly. He knows the classical rules as a student knows them, and his ear goes on as before: the French twelve-syllable line sounds to him like the anapests of Byron’s line on the Assyrian. He admits that this hearing is mistaken and reports it all the same. English, he adds, does not let anapests carry a tragic subject. He is convinced that an Englishman who goes to *Phèdre* at the Comédie Française, whatever his French and however he admires the players’ delivery, is bound to find Racine comic.

[auden[4].source]
“Un Homme d’Esprit,” in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), pp. 358–59

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In a footnote on the same page Auden names a second difficulty for his ear, the pause inside the line. French poets, he says, take pains to hold it to a few fixed places, and English poets take as much trouble to shift it from one line to the next.

[auden[5].source]
“Un Homme d’Esprit,” in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), p. 359, footnote

[auden[6].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Two reports at second hand. In 1947, in talk that Alan Ansen wrote down, Auden rates Racine below his reputation. Charles Miller, who took the class of 1941 and shared Auden’s house that term, remembered forty years later that Auden disliked Racine. No account has been found of what Auden said about *Phèdre* in the class itself.

[auden[6].source]
Alan Ansen, *The Table Talk of W. H. Auden*, edited by Nicholas Jenkins (1990); Charles H. Miller, *Auden: An American Friendship* (1983), p. 149

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
The French follows an edition of 1854, and its punctuation is that editor’s. The edition of 1697 has the same words in the lines shown but punctuates several of them differently. The dots after the word for heart are in both.

[caveats[1]]
French verse fixes only two accents, on the sixth and twelfth syllables. The lighter stresses marked inside each half-line are one reader’s hearing, and another reader could fairly place a few of them otherwise.

[caveats[2]]
Auden’s remarks on how the French line sounds to an English ear come from an essay on Marianne Moore and an introduction to Valéry. No essay of his on *Phèdre* has been found, nor any account of what he said about it in the class of 1941.

[caveats[3]]
That Auden thought Racine overrated, and that he disliked him, are reports by a friend and by a student. Their books were not seen in full.

[caveats[4]]
The counts of couplets and of the word *sang* were made on the French text of 1854, with words counted as spelled. The English given for *Je m’égare*, I lose my way, keeps the first sense of the verb; it can as fairly be put as my mind wanders.
