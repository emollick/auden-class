## The head of the page

[title]
A word said twice

[where]
*Othello*, Act 3, Scene 3; Act 4, Scene 1; Act 5, Scene 2

[about] (under “The passage”)
Othello, a Moor and a general of Venice, has married Desdemona, a senator’s daughter. Iago, his ensign, wanted the lieutenancy that Othello gave to Michael Cassio, and is working to make Othello believe that Cassio and Desdemona are lovers. In the first passage Iago begins, soon after Cassio has been seen leaving Desdemona; in the second he tells Othello that Cassio has talked; in the third Othello stands over his sleeping wife, come to kill her.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden compares Iago to an analyst with a patient, except that this one works to kill. Iago never has to lie, Auden says, because everything he tells Othello is meant to bring to the surface what is already in Othello’s mind. Even where a plain lie would be safe, Iago gives an answer that can be taken two ways and leaves the taking to Othello.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
his answer is equivocal and its interpretation is left to Othello

[view.source]
“The Joker in the Pack,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 266

## Passage 1: Othello and Iago  (plates[0], id "echo", from othello/a3s3)

[plates[0].heading]
Othello and Iago

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[0].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
anadiplosis

[plates[0].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
a doubling back: the word that ends one sentence begins the next

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
A word ends one speech and begins the next: <b>indeed</b>, then <b>honest</b>, then <b>think</b>. Rhetoric calls this anadiplosis, a doubling back, and here the doubled word changes speakers. Iago speaks four times, thirteen words in all. Three of the four are questions, and two of those are Othello’s last word handed back with <i>my lord</i>. Iago’s one statement goes no further than <i>for aught I know</i>. Auden writes that Iago never has to lie, because what he says is meant to bring up what is already in Othello’s mind. It is Othello who names the device, <i>he echoes me</i>, who says <b>monster</b>, and who concludes that Iago means something.

### Tab “Beat” (beat)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Beat

[plates[0].lenses[1].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
ten syllables and five beats, the line beginning off the beat, without rhyme

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The first line is whole: ten syllables, five beats. Iago’s <b>Indeed?</b> has two syllables and one beat. After another full line six short pieces follow, none longer than six syllables. Editions join them into lines differently. The Folger and Globe editions both print Iago’s <b>Indeed?</b> alone, and each leaves other pieces short. In Shakespeare’s verse a line left short can mark a pause. A dozen lines on, Othello calls Iago’s pauses <i>stops</i> and says they frighten him the more.

[plates[0].lenses[1].points[0].say] (a numbered remark)
Two syllables, one beat. Othello’s reply is a full line without it.

[plates[0].lenses[1].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)
Eleven syllables. Othello says Iago’s word back and then answers it. *Ay* can be heard as a sixth beat.

[plates[0].lenses[1].points[2].say] (a numbered remark)
The Folger edition prints Iago’s six syllables and these four as one line of ten. The Globe edition makes its line of ten from this question and the three words said twice after it. A Victorian grammar of Shakespeare’s English has a name for a part-line that ends one line and begins the next: *amphibious*.

[plates[0].lenses[1].points[3].say] (a numbered remark)
Iago’s three words again. Here they begin a line of five beats, which starts on a beat and counts *heaven* as one syllable. The Globe edition gives these three words to the line before.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
<b>Honest</b> and <i>honesty</i> have been said twenty-one times before this line, twelve of them about Iago, and never yet to ask whether someone is honest. Here Othello asks it of Cassio. Auden told his class that Othello sets great store by simple virtues such as honesty, and that Iago sees this is the key to him. The word could also mean chaste, and later in the scene Othello says it of his wife, in doubt. The noun <b>monster</b> is first heard here, from Othello; Iago takes it up later for jealousy. Auden reads it as Othello’s own fear: that Venice values him only for his use.

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[0].gloss] (set under the word)
honorable · upright · chaste

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[0].note] (a note on the word)
The lexicon of Shakespeare’s words puts this line under honorable, respectable; the word could also praise a man as upright and truthful or a woman as chaste, and was a vague word of praise, often said to an inferior.

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[1].gloss] (set under the word)
Latin *monstrum*, a warning sign

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[1].note] (a note on the word)
Latin *monstrum* was first a portent sent as a warning, from *monere*, to warn; the Latin verb for to show, *monstrare*, is formed from it.

## Passage 2: Othello and Iago  (plates[1], id "lie", from othello/a4s1)

[plates[1].heading]
Othello and Iago

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Iago’s answer is one syllable, and two verbs share it: to lie down and to tell a lie. Asked <i>With her?</i> he gives two prepositions and <i>what you will</i>. Auden quotes these lines: even here, where a plain lie would be safe, the answer can be taken two ways and the taking is left to Othello. Othello takes it both ways. <i>Lie with her</i> is the bed. <i>Lie on her</i>, he says, is said when they <b>belie</b> her, when lies are told about a woman. He has named slander, and he goes back to the bed: <b>fulsome</b>, foul. When Othello has fallen in a trance, Iago’s own word for such women is <i>guiltless</i>.

[plates[1].lenses[0].notes[0].gloss] (set under the word)

[plates[1].lenses[0].notes[0].note] (a note on the word)
Two Old English verbs, *licgan*, to lie down, and *leogan*, to tell a lie, had come to be spelled and said alike.

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[1].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)
antimetabole

[plates[1].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)
the same words said again in the opposite order

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
<i>Confess and be hanged</i> was a proverb. The Oxford dictionary says it appears to have been a degree ruder than saying <i>you lie</i>. Othello says it of Cassio, then turns it round: <b>hanged</b> first, <b>confess</b> after. In the play the death sentence has come first: Othello orders Cassio’s death in Act 3, and what he here calls Cassio’s <i>confessions</i> he first hears of in this scene. In Act 5 Othello tells his wife to confess and, in the same speech, that she is to die. Once Othello doubts her love, Auden writes, it no longer matters what she has or has not done.

### Tab “Sentence” (sentence)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Sentence

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Iago has said eight words since Othello’s <i>What? What?</i> Othello answers with seventy-four. He begins by saying Iago’s phrases back, and he ends in pieces of one to four words: <i>Noses, ears, and lips</i>. Near the end three whole sentences stand together, and they are about himself. He trembles. Nature, he says, would not put on such passion <i>without some instruction</i>: without some prompting, as the lexicon of Shakespeare’s words explains it. What shakes him, he says, is <i>not words</i>. Auden’s account is that Iago’s words are meant to bring up what is already in Othello’s mind. After the last piece the stage direction is <i>Falls in a trance</i>.

### Tab “Play” (shape)

[plates[1].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
Play

[plates[1].lenses[3].what] (printed over the drawing)
how much Othello and Iago speak in verse and in prose, act by act

[plates[1].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
Before <i>With her?</i> Othello has spoken 3,653 words, all of them verse. His prose begins here: 360 words, all in Act 4. In Act 5 he speaks verse and nothing else. A Victorian grammar of Shakespeare’s English gives this speech as an example of the highest passion going into prose. Iago has used both forms since the first scene: 2,431 words of prose, most of them to Roderigo and to Cassio. Writing of how the part should be acted, Auden sees a different honest Iago for each person Iago talks to. To Othello Iago speaks verse, except once: later in this scene the two settle the deaths of Desdemona and Cassio in prose.

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[2].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
antanaclasis

[plates[2].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
the same words said again with a different meaning

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
One line says the same four words twice. The first time they mean the flame burning in the room, the second time her life. Nothing in the line itself marks the change; the six lines after it do. He speaks first to the flame, <i>thou flaming minister</i>, then to her, <i>thou cunning’st pattern of excelling nature</i>, and the word <b>light</b> comes back three times, once as the flame’s and twice as hers. The flame’s he can restore, <i>should I repent me</i>. For hers he does not know where the fire is that can.

### Tab “Sentence” (sentence)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Sentence

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Two sentences are built to one plan. Each begins with a condition: <i>If I quench thee</i>, <i>But once put out thy light</i>. Each turns aside to name the one spoken to, the flame in three words and the woman in a whole line. In each the thing acted on comes before its verb, so the verb comes last: <b>restore</b> ends its clause and <b>relume</b> its sentence. The end of a sentence is usually its place of most weight. The two differ in who can. In the first sentence it is <i>I</i>. In the second it is a fire, and he does not know where it is.

### Tab “Word” (word)

Word

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
He says <b>cause</b> three times and will not name it. The word meant a reason, a lawsuit, sometimes a charge. Auden writes that what exactly she has done no longer matters to Othello: in Act 4 he charges her with nameless things, not with adultery with Cassio. Here too nothing is named, and the one reason given is <i>more men</i>. <b>Monumental</b> alabaster is the stone of a tomb, of the figure placed over a grave, and he says it of a sleeping woman’s skin.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden begins from the villain. He can think of no other play in which one character alone does deeds and all the rest only show behavior: every act in it is Iago’s. Othello’s fall, he writes, is another man’s work, so that an audience pities Othello without respecting him and keeps for Iago the respect Auden calls aesthetic.

[auden[0].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
nothing he says or does originates with himself

[auden[0].source]
“The Joker in the Pack,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 246–47

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden asks the actor of Iago for the greatest contrast between Iago in company and Iago alone. With others he should show every skill a great actor has, and be a different honest man for each person he talks to: one for Roderigo, one for Cassio, one for Othello, one for Desdemona. Alone, he should speak his soliloquies badly, pausing where the verse asks for no pause and stressing words that do not matter. The reason Auden gives is that what Iago wants in the end is nothingness, for the others and for himself.

[auden[1].source]
“The Joker in the Pack,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 258

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
At the end of Act 3, Scene 3, Othello tells Iago he is now his lieutenant, and Iago answers that he is Othello’s own for ever. Auden calls the answer a mocking repetition of a line Othello spoke earlier, and reads *lieutenant* here as a private and unlawful commission, to have Cassio killed in secret, and no public rank. He does not say which line he means. Earlier in the scene Othello tells Iago that he is bound to him for ever.

[auden[2].source]
“The Joker in the Pack,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 249

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Donald Pearce took the class in 1941 and wrote down what he could afterward. His notes say that the lectures on Shakespeare were mostly about the minds of the characters and the society around them, and hardly touched the verse or the rhetoric, though Auden asked the class to read Wilson Knight, who does. Auden called Othello deliberately naive, a man who lays great stress on simple virtues such as honesty, and said that Iago sees this is the key to him. Iago he called the one brilliant mind in the play: cold, unable to love, reasoning without a flaw, moving other people like pieces in a game.

[auden[3].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), pp. 139 and 142–43

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Pearce’s notes give six diagrams that Auden put on the blackboard for the lecture on *Othello*. Each sorts the people of the play: by rank, by self-confidence, by sex appeal, by passion, by sophistication, by intelligence. The last sets Iago alone as intelligent and the rest as stupid. Charles Miller, another student in the class, remembered the diagrams as meant to prove Othello a stupid man and Iago more honest than anyone else in the play. Miller adds that he and other students were put off by what looked like flippancy about Shakespeare.

[auden[4].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), pp. 139–40; Charles H. Miller, *Auden: An American Friendship* (1983), p. 29

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
What Auden told his class in 1941 is known from notes that a student, Donald Pearce, made after the lectures. It is not Auden’s own text. Charles Miller’s sentence about the blackboard was seen only in search results from a scanned copy of his book.

[caveats[1]]
Auden’s sentence that Iago never has to lie goes further than the play. In [Act 3, Scene 3](#/read/othello/a3s3:319) Iago says he saw Cassio wipe his beard with the handkerchief that day, when he has it himself. And that Cassio has talked, in the second passage, rests on Iago’s word alone.

[caveats[2]]
Editions divide the short speeches of the first passage into lines differently. The Folger and the Globe do it as the Beat tab says; the Cambridge edition of 1866 prints each speech by itself. The stresses marked on the short pieces can be heard in more than one way.

[caveats[3]]
The counts of verse and prose follow the text printed here, which prints Othello’s *With her?* in the second passage as prose. The Folger edition sets those two words in a verse line. Counted that way, his prose comes to 358 words, not 360.

[caveats[4]]
Two early printings differ: the Quarto of 1622 lacks the long speech of the second passage from *To confess* onward, and has another word for *relume*. In the first passage the Folio of 1623 has *thou* for *he*. The Globe edition prints Iago’s three short speeches there with exclamation marks.
