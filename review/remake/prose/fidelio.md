## The head of the page

[title]
Leonore’s answer

[where]
*Fidelio*, Act 2, Scene 5, with Act 1, Scene 6 and Act 2, Scene 3

[about] (under “The passage”)
Leonore, dressed as a man and calling herself Fidelio, has taken service with the jailer Rocco to find her husband Florestan, whom the governor Pizarro holds in a dungeon of the state prison. In the first passage Pizarro has just failed to kill him, and husband and wife are alone. The second is her scene alone in Act 1, after she has watched Pizarro and Rocco go off together. The third comes before the first: the moment in the dungeon when she steps between her husband and Pizarro’s dagger.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
No account of this opera by Auden has been found. In a 1947 lecture on *King Lear*, known from students’ notes, he said this of Cordelia. When Lear wakes and says she has cause not to love him, she twice answers that she has none. Compare, Auden said, Leonore’s answer to Florestan.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
Cordelia wants to love freely, without compulsion, and paradoxically she describes love as a duty.

[view.source]
*Lectures on Shakespeare*, reconstructed and edited by Arthur Kirsch (2000), pp. 225–26

### Tab “Versions” (compare)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Versions

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
In the German libretto, printed here with its spoken dialogue uncut, Leonore says <b>Nichts</b>, nothing, once, and then gives a reason. Two American librettos print a shortened German text at this point, with the word twice and no reason, and the English shown here follows it. A word said again at once, with nothing between, is the figure of emphasis called epizeuxis. The students’ notes of Auden’s lecture give her answer in this doubled form. He set it beside Cordelia’s <i>No cause, no cause</i>, her reply when Lear, waking, says she has cause not to love him. Cordelia, Auden said, has no wish for authority; she wants her love to be free.

[plates[0].lenses[0].ties[0].say] (a numbered remark)
In the German Florestan exclaims twice: true wife, woman without equal. The shortened text keeps the first.

[plates[0].lenses[0].ties[2].say] (a numbered remark)
Her reason: her soul was with him, and she asks how her body could not have felt strong, fighting for its better self. The shortened text has none of this. Its next word is the first cry of the duet.

[plates[0].lenses[0].ties[3].say] (a numbered remark)
Nameless joy becomes *joy unknown*.

[plates[0].lenses[0].ties[4].say] (a numbered remark)
The German calls the suffering unnameable. The English calls it *great*.

[plates[0].lenses[0].ties[5].say] (a numbered remark)
The German calls the delight very great. The English calls it *past utterance*: the two descriptions have changed places.

### Tab “Verbs” (sentence)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Verbs

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Each spoken speech opens with an exclamation and then comes to a sentence with its verb: <i>hast</i> … <b>erduldet</b>, have borne; <b>war</b>, was; <i>hätte</i> … <b>gefühlt</b>, would have felt; <b>kämpfte</b>, fought. Leonore’s last sentence is a question that argues. After the heading <b>Duett</b> the six lines shown have no verb: they name joy, husband, breast, Leonore, suffering, delight. The whole duet, eighteen lines, has three verbs, each a form of <i>to be</i>. Auden called the sensible passages of a libretto conversation, not song, and held that what is written for singing cannot be poetry: poetry reflects, and will not rest in the outcries of feeling, while music is immediate.

### Tab “Word” (word)

Word

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
<b>Namenlose</b> is nameless and <b>unnennbarem</b> unnameable: the sung lines say that this joy and that suffering are beyond naming. Adelung’s dictionary, in a volume of 1798, gives <i>namenlos</i> in this sense to the higher style of the moderns: what cannot be named or expressed because of its amount or its intensity. Twelve lines of the libretto have one of three such words, these two or <i>unaussprechlich</i>, unspeakable, and eleven are sung. The twelfth is Leonore’s, spoken in the dungeon shortly before her husband turns his face to her. The right plot for a libretto, Auden wrote, is melodrama: it sets its people in situations too strange or too tragic for words.

Auden held that a librettist’s verses are written to the composer, not the public, as a private letter is.

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The four lines of recitative shown rhyme from the outside in: <b>hin</b> waits two lines for <b>Tigersinn</b>, while <b>Grimme</b> is answered at once by <b>Stimme</b>. The lines to Hope rhyme by turns. The last four close in again around the two short lines, which end on <b>wanke nicht</b>, do not waver, and <b>die Pflicht</b>, duty. In a stanza so rhymed, a handbook of English verse says, the third line’s rhyme gains emphasis from its nearness to the second: here that word is duty. Around the pair, <b>Triebe</b>, drive, waits for <b>Gattenliebe</b>, married love. Adelung’s dictionary, in a volume of 1801, noted that poets often use <i>Trieb</i> for love, for the convenience of the rhyme.

[plates[1].lenses[1].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[2].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
epistrophe

[plates[2].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
the same word at the end of phrase after phrase

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Three speeches in a row end on one word, <b>Weib</b>, wife. Ending one phrase after another on the same word is the figure called epistrophe; here four voices share it. Leonore says the word first, and speaks of herself as she would of someone else: <i>sein</i>, his. Rocco and Pizarro give her last two words back as a question. Florestan changes the first of them to <b>Mein</b>, my: the man she spoke of speaks for himself. Only then does she turn to him with her name, which he repeats, and to the others with <b>Ich bin</b>, I am, and the word a fourth time.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
<b>Weib</b> was once the common German word both for a woman and for a wife. Adelung’s dictionary, in a volume of 1801, calls its use restricted: in common life, of humble married or elderly women; in familiar talk, of a wife, more often in Upper Germany, the south, where nothing was more common than <i>mein Weib</i>, my wife; in poetry and the higher style, commonly only of a stout-hearted, manly woman, with a secondary idea of strength. Leonore says it in man’s clothes, between her husband and a dagger. The libretto has the word once before this line, in Jaquino’s wooing of Marzelline. Soon after, Pizarro asks whether he is to tremble before a <i>Weib</i>.

[plates[2].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
In Act 1 of the German libretto the heroine is called <i>Fidelio</i> fifteen times and <i>Leonore</i> never: under the name <i>Fidelio</i> Marzelline loves her and Rocco chooses her for a son-in-law. In the dungeon Florestan says <i>Leonore</i> three times before he knows her. In the lines of this passage she says it herself, the only time in the opera, and nobody says <i>Fidelio</i> afterward. Beethoven, Grove’s dictionary says, wished the opera called <i>Leonora</i>. Auden wrote that drama rests on a mistake and that good drama moves in two steps: the mistake is made, then found out. One of his examples, not from this opera, is a chambermaid who is really a young nobleman, disguised.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden names the opera once in *The Dyer’s Hand*, and it is the overture he speaks of. Leonora No. 3, he grants, is fine music for a concert; played in the theater between the two scenes of Act 2 of *Fidelio*, it is, he says, acutely boring for all its twelve minutes. His reason is that an opera’s orchestra plays for the singers and not for the listeners. A lover of opera bears an orchestral interlude, and may even like it, so long as he understands that the singers need rest or the scene is being changed; any other playing by the orchestra alone is, to him, time wasted.

[auden[0].source]
“Notes on Music and Opera,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 472

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In the same notes he writes that drama rests on a mistake; one example is a chambermaid who is really a young nobleman, disguised. A good play moves in two steps: the mistake is made, and then it is found out. In a spoken play the finding out may be slow; in a libretto recognition must be sudden, because music cannot live with uncertainty. The plot a libretto needs, he says, is melodrama: it sets its people in situations too strange or too tragic for words, and a sensible mood is not one for singing. He is writing of librettos in general, not of *Fidelio*.

[auden[1].source]
“Notes on Music and Opera,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 471–72

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Of a libretto’s words he writes that poetry is at bottom reflection: it will not stop at the outcries of feeling as it comes, and tries to understand what is felt. Music is immediate, so what is written to be sung cannot be poetry. A librettist’s verses are meant for the composer, not for the public, as a letter is for one reader, and their great moment is the one in which they give him a melody. A listener to song, he believes, hears syllables and not words, and it is the syllables that decide the vocal line; for that reason he is, in general, against performing operas in translation.

[auden[2].source]
“Notes on Music and Opera,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 472–73

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
The typed reading list for the course Auden gave at the University of Michigan in the fall of 1941 has a heading, Opera Libretti, with nine titles under it. *Fidelio*, with Beethoven’s name beside it, is one of the nine. No remark of his on this opera from that class has been found.

[auden[3].source]
The reading list for English 135, University of Michigan, 1941, first leaf, as reproduced by Alan Jacobs, “Auden’s Syllabus” (blog.ayjay.org, October 1, 2012)

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
Auden’s remark comes from students’ notes of a lecture of 1947, put together by an editor in 2000, and was read for this page only in short extracts of a library copy. He said it of Cordelia, and told the class only to compare Leonore’s answer.

[caveats[1]]
Leonore’s answer exists in three forms. The uncut German here has the word once, with a reason. A German vocal score (Peters), giving the dialogue as spoken on stage, has it once, with no reason. Two American librettos have it twice in their German; the English shown here follows one.

[caveats[2]]
The stresses marked are one reader’s hearing of the German, and a few syllables could fairly be marked otherwise. The handbook cited for the rhyme describes English verse. The tempo words were read in the Peters vocal score, not in a full score.

[caveats[3]]
The counts of names and words follow the uncut German libretto. The English printed here is a shortened text and has fewer: *Fidelio* fourteen times and *Leonora* nine. That the three short speeches of the third passage fill one line is also a count; the libretto prints them apart.

[caveats[4]]
The glosses are plain modern renderings, and the phrase about the better self can be taken two ways.
