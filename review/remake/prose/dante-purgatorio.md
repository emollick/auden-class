## The head of the page

[title]
Three places on the mountain

[where]
*Purgatorio*, Cantos 15, 17 and 28

[about] (under “The passage”)
Dante, a living man, climbs the mountain of Purgatory with the dead poet Virgil as his guide. On the stair above the terrace of envy he asks how a good can make its owners richer the more of them share it. A terrace later, at nightfall, when no one may climb, Virgil explains how the mountain is ordered. At the top Virgil tells him that his will is now free, upright and whole, and Dante walks into the garden of Eden and sees a lady across a stream.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden went back to Virgil’s speech in canto 17 more than once. In 1941 he copied nine lines of it into the notes of *New Year Letter* and quoted their conclusion in a review. In *The Dyer’s Hand* he set three of those lines against *The Tempest*, which in his reading blames nature and makes spirit innocent. Dante, he wrote, holds the exact opposite: what is natural cannot err, and only the mind’s love can.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
The natural can never desire too much or too little because the natural good is the mean

[view.source]
“Balaam and His Ass,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 130–31; *New Year Letter* (London: Faber, 1941), the note just before the note to line 1432

### Tab “Simile” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)

[plates[0].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)

[plates[0].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
a comparison that says one thing is like another

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Dante has asked how a good can make its owners richer the more of them share it. Virgil answers with a comparison, marked by <b>così</b> (so) and <b>com’</b> (as): the good above runs to love as a ray comes to a <b>lucido corpo</b>, a bright body. <i>Lucido</i> is said of a polished surface that throws light back. Landino, commenting in 1481, gives the point: a ray is not made less by the number of bodies it lights. Virgil’s reasoning ends on a second comparison, <b>come specchio</b>, like a mirror: each gives back to the other. Auden copied these last three lines into the notes of <i>New Year Letter</i> in 1941.

### Tab “Sentence” (sentence)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Sentence

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
After the comparison come three statements, and each is a proportion. <b>Tanto</b> and <b>quanto</b>: it gives as much as it finds. <b>Quantunque</b>: however far love reaches, the worth grows on it. <b>Quanta</b>, with <b>più</b> three times: the more souls, the more to love and the more love. A sentence of this build ties two amounts together, so that as one rises the other does. Dante’s question took sharing for division: more owners, so a smaller share, which is what Virgil has just said of earthly goods (lines 49 to 51). <i>Più</i>, more, comes ten times between lines 55 and 75, three of them in the last three lines.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Dante’s question is in the words of property: <i>distributo</i>, shared out, <i>posseditor</i>, owners, <i>ricchi</i>, rich (lines 61 to 63). The answer has no word of owning. Its words are of light and love, and two verbs are of giving: the good <b>si dà</b>, gives itself, and each soul <b>rende</b>, gives back. <i>Rendere</i> is first to return what is owed, and is also said of a mirror that sends back an image. Auden quoted the last three lines in an essay on <i>The Merchant of Venice</i>, to show that love breeds love, and went on to usury, where money breeds money.

The English beside the Italian is Okey’s prose, the version Auden set his class to learn and the wording he copied into his notes. It follows Dante closely: <i>bright body</i>, <i>giveth back</i>. At one word the translators part. <b>S’intende</b> becomes <i>comprehend each other</i>, and when Auden quotes the last three lines he brings them in to show that both love and understanding breed their like. On that reading lines 73 and 74, the seventh and eighth here, hold three verbs for loving and none for knowing.

Figure

[plates[1].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)

[plates[1].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
two opposed things set side by side, often in phrases of the same build

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Two lines set two loves against each other, turning on <b>ma</b>, but. The first is the love a creature has by nature, and gets <i>è sempre</i>, is always. The second, the love of the mind, gets another verb: <b>puote</b>, can. The same root stands on both sides, <i>errore</i> and <i>errar</i>, first denied and then allowed. In the next canto Virgil explains: the first desire is in man as the urge to make honey is in the bee and earns no praise or blame, and merit begins with the power that keeps the threshold of assent, free will. Auden’s comment: what is natural obeys necessity and cannot picture what might be.

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Dante’s rhyme is a chain. In each group of three lines, a tercet, the middle line gives its rhyme to the outer lines of the next. Here the middle line of the first tercet ends on <b>amore</b>, love. The second tercet has to answer it, and answers with <b>errore</b>, error, and <b>vigore</b>, the force that love can have too much or too little of. So the lines about error are rhymed on love. The conclusion states the tie outright: love is the seed of every virtue and of every act that deserves punishment.

[plates[1].lenses[1].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage. *Terzina* in the Treccani *Vocabolario*: the middle line of each group of three gives its rhyme to the first and third lines of the next. The conclusion is in the lines themselves.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
<b>Errore</b> and <b>errar</b> come from the Latin for wandering and going astray; being wrong is the second sense. The poem opens with the straight way lost (<i>Inferno</i> 1, line 3). In the lines left out love moves: it <i>si torce</i>, twists, toward evil, or <i>corre</i>, runs, at the good with too much or too little care. In the conclusion love is <b>sementa</b>, seed, of every virtue and of every act that earns <b>pene</b>, penalties or pains. Auden quoted these words near the end of a review of 1941. The love in them, he wrote, is the basic will of every creature to become itself, and grace changes that love and does not add another.

[plates[1].lenses[3].what] (printed over the drawing)
the mountain of Purgatory: its seven terraces sorted by the three ways love goes wrong, the cantos given to each level, and where these lines stand in the poem

[plates[1].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
The three ways of going wrong that Virgil names are the plan of the mountain. Love of an <b>evil object</b> is wept for on the three terraces already climbed: pride, envy, anger. Love with <b>too little</b> force is made good on this one. Love with <b>too much</b> is wept for on the three above. Virgil’s plan of lower Hell in <i>Inferno</i> 11 sorts by injury done, through force or fraud; this one sorts by love. The speech comes at night, when no one may climb, and by count of lines the middle of the whole poem falls inside it. Cantos 14 to 20 match in length, pair by pair. Auden noted that Dante’s structure settles even the number of verses.

### Tab “Sentence” (sentence)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Sentence

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The verb comes first: <b>m’apparve</b>, there appeared to me. Its subject is kept back through the rest of that line and two more, and until then the sentence does not say what appeared. The wait is filled with a comparison: as a thing appears, suddenly, that through wonder turns every other thought aside. Only then comes <b>una donna soletta</b>, a lady all alone. The poem names her once, five cantos later: Matelda. What appears in the comparison is only <i>cosa</i>, a thing, and its verb is the verb of the event again: <i>apparve</i>, <i>appare</i>. Okey’s prose, the version Auden’s class had to write out from memory, keeps the order: <i>appeared</i>, then the comparison, then <i>a lady solitary</i>.

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[2].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)
zeugma

[plates[2].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)
one verb is made to serve two clauses: it is said in the first and left out of the second

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
<b>Perdette</b>, lost, is said once and serves two clauses: the mother lost the girl, and the girl lost the spring. With the verb in the line before, the last line holds two pairs: the mother and <b>lei</b> (her); <b>ella</b> (she) and the spring. The two words for the girl meet at the comma. On one side she is what is lost, on the other the one who loses. Okey’s prose says the verb twice. <b>Primavera</b> returns at the canto’s end: here, the lady says, it is always spring. In the myth Pluto, god of the underworld, carries Proserpina off. Chiavacci Leonardi’s commentary reads it as the loss of innocence, which Christians could take for the loss of this garden.

eleven syllables: the last accent always on the tenth and, as a rule, another on the fourth or the sixth

[plates[2].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
Dante’s line has eleven syllables, the last accent always on the tenth and, as a rule, another on the fourth or the sixth. Vowels that meet between words usually count as one syllable. The first three lines each open on five syllables stressed on the fourth: <i>e là m’apparve</i>, <i>subitamente</i>, <i>per maraviglia</i>. The two lines that bring the lady have no stress on the fourth: their main stress is on the sixth. Their first seven syllables hold the whole subject, <b>una donna soletta</b>, then two things she is doing, <b>cantando</b>, <b>scegliendo</b>. In the last line <b>lei</b> and <b>ella</b> take the fourth and sixth places, and the vowels that meet at the comma between them are kept apart.

[plates[2].lenses[3].points[0].say] (a numbered remark)
Two vowels meet between two words and count as one syllable. It happens four times in these nine lines.

[plates[2].lenses[3].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)

[plates[2].lenses[3].points[2].say] (a numbered remark)

[plates[2].lenses[3].points[3].say] (a numbered remark)
*Lei* on the fourth, *ella* on the sixth. At the comma the vowels of *lei* and *ed* meet and are kept apart.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In the notes to *New Year Letter*, printed in 1941, the year of the course, Auden copied out two passages of the *Purgatorio* in the prose he would set his class. One is the nine lines of the second passage here, with the same six lines left out. The other is the three lines that close the first passage. Two lines from earlier in the same exchange stand in Italian at the head of a section of “Balaam and His Ass,” the essay in *The Dyer’s Hand* that sets Dante against *The Tempest*.

[auden[0].source]
*New Year Letter* (London: Faber, 1941), the note just before the note to line 1432 and the note to line 1705; “Balaam and His Ass,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 139

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In the poem itself a poet may choose which of the dead masters will judge him, and when Auden’s name is called it is Dante who presides on his case. Dante, the lines say, wrecked a career in the world, brought all his powers of feeling, sense, will and mind to what lies beyond it, and had rational love, which Auden names in Latin, for his guide through the three realms of the dead.

[auden[1].source]
*New Year Letter* (1941), Part I, in *The Collected Poetry of W. H. Auden* (1945), pp. 269–70

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In an essay on *The Merchant of Venice* Auden quoted the three lines that close the first passage, in the same prose, as an instance of something well known: love and understanding breed their like. He went straight on to money. Once money could breed money in a trading economy, he wrote, it amused poets to use usury, a base trade, as a figure for love, the noblest of human acts.

[auden[2].source]
“Brothers & Others,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 230

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In June 1941, near the end of a review of a book about romantic love, Auden quoted the words about the seed in Italian. Eros, he wrote, is that love: the basic will of every creature to become itself, and no creature can exist without it.

[auden[3].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
Agape is that Eros mutated by Grace, a conversion, not an addition, the Law fulfilled, not the Law destroyed

[auden[3].source]
“Eros and Agape,” a review of Denis de Rougemont, *Love in the Western World*, *The Nation*, 28 June 1941; in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 137–40

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Setting three lines of the second passage against *The Tempest*, Auden added that no animal can fall. The tempter’s promise is in the future tense; a creature that only obeys necessity cannot picture doing what has never been done. The Italian he prints has too little before too much, the other way round from the Italian printed here.

[auden[4].source]
“Balaam and His Ass,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 130–31

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
By Donald Pearce’s account, the final examination was to write out cantos of the poem from memory, in the prose version that, Auden told the class, Eliot had used for *The Waste Land*. Pearce gives canto 28 as his one example of the *Purgatorio* cantos Auden announced. When the class protested, Auden cut seven cantos to five. Pearce wrote for three hours and filled five examination books.

[auden[5].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), pp. 153–54

[auden[6].says] (a paraphrase of him)
For Auden the master of dream literature is Dante. In him, he wrote, an endless flow of images goes together with a strict structure of logic and number that settles even how many verses there are.

[auden[6].source]
“George MacDonald,” the introduction to *The Visionary Novels of George MacDonald* (1954), in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), p. 269

[auden[7].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Writing on Kierkegaard in 1952, Auden quoted the soul on the sixth terrace who says that he calls his suffering pain and ought to call it solace (canto 23, lines 70 to 72). The lines, he wrote, state the paradox of the place suffering has in Christianity: its message is in the end good news, and to a man’s self-love it is at first the worst of news.

[auden[7].source]
“Søren Kierkegaard,” the introduction to *The Living Thoughts of Kierkegaard* (1952), in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), p. 169

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
Auden’s two notes in *New Year Letter* and his review of 1941 were read here only in extracts from scanned copies, not in full.

[caveats[1]]
The examination is known from one student’s memoir, Donald Pearce’s. He names Canto 28 among the cantos Auden first announced, and does not say which five were kept. Other students remembered six cantos.

[caveats[2]]
The verb *s’intende* in the first passage is read three ways: comprehend each other (Okey), aspire (Longfellow) and love (the *Enciclopedia Dantesca* and several commentators). Auden’s remark on love and understanding matches Okey’s wording.

[caveats[3]]
In the second passage the Italian printed here has too much before too little. The Temple Classics edition, whose prose Auden set, prints its Italian with too little first, and so does Auden when he quotes the lines.

[caveats[4]]
The second passage leaves out lines 97 to 102 of its canto, and the third leaves out lines 43 to 48. The stresses marked in the third are one reader’s count by the rules of Italian verse; in two of its lines a syllable could fairly be marked otherwise.
