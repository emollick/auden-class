## The head of the page

[title]
Orpheus calls Eurydice

[where]
*Orfeo ed Euridice*, Act 3, Scene 1, with Act 1, Scenes 1 and 2

[about] (under “The passage”)
Orpheus, a singer, has gone down among the dead to bring back his wife, Eurydice. The god Love set the terms: he must not look at her until they are out of the underworld, and must not tell her why. On the way up she begs for one look; he turns, she dies a second time, and he sings this aria. The second passage sets Love’s terms beside his words before and after the aria; the third is his song at her tomb in Act 1.

### Tab “Count” (beat)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Count

[plates[0].lenses[0].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
the line of eight: an accent on the third syllable and on the seventh; a line may stop on the seventh

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Italian verse counts syllables, and a vowel that ends a word runs into a vowel that begins the next, as after <b>senza</b>. Counted so, all seven lines stress the third syllable and the seventh. Four go on to an eighth and three stop on the seventh. This is the <i>ottonario</i>, the line of eight, since Petrarch’s time a line of religious verse and of verse for music. The speech before the aria is recitative, sung speech: lines of eleven syllables and one of seven, unrhymed until its close. The third line here is three cries with stops between them and still counts eight.

[plates[0].lenses[0].points[0].say] (a numbered remark)
Two vowels meet between these words and count as one syllable. Both are still said. The Italian name for this is *sinalefe*.

[plates[0].lenses[0].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)
The line stops on its stressed seventh syllable. It is still a line of eight: an Italian line takes its name from the place of its last accent.

[plates[0].lenses[0].points[2].say] (a numbered remark)
The name is four syllables, stressed on the third: half a line of eight. Its last vowel counts as one with the cry that follows, across the stop. The fifth line begins the same way.

### Tab “Endings” (rhyme)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Endings

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Three lines stop on a word cut short: <b>ben</b> for <i>bene</i>, <b>fedel</b> for <i>fedele</i>, <b>Ciel</b> for <i>cielo</i>. A word cut on a consonant at the end of a line is a mark of the <i>canzonetta</i>, a poem in short lines, usually set to music. Each cut ends a group: the two opening lines, the order to answer, the last three lines. Italian arias are as a rule strictly rhymed; here only the last four lines rhyme: <b>fedel</b>, faithful, with <b>Ciel</b>, heaven, and two lines in <i>anza</i> between them. The two opening lines, which return after the seventh, rhyme with nothing. They are alike inside the line: a verb ending in a stressed <i>rò</i>, <i>I shall</i>, then <b>senza</b>, without.

[plates[0].lenses[1].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage. The cut words and their place in the canzonetta: the *Vocabolario Treccani* under *troncamento* and *canzonetta*, and the *Enciclopedia dell’Italiano* under *rima*. That arias are strictly rhymed: the same encyclopedia under *melodramma, lingua del*. The meanings: the *Vocabolario* under *fedele*, *cielo*, *senza* and *futuro*. The three groups are also the sections of the score.

### Tab “Calls” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Calls

[plates[0].lenses[2].name] (the name of the figure)
apostrophe

[plates[0].lenses[2].plain] (the figure in plain words)
a turning away: the speaker turns from the hearers to speak to someone or something else; here, from speaking about her to speaking to her

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The first two lines speak about her: what to do without <b>Euridice</b>, where to go without his <b>ben</b>, his good, the one he loves. The third turns to her, though she has just died: it calls the name and orders <b>Rispondi</b>, answer. Rhetoric names this turn apostrophe. Earlier in the scene she gave him that order and called him <i>infido</i>, faithless, and both times his reply was to tell her to come and be silent. Now the order is his, and the fourth line says the opposite of her charge: he is her <b>fedel</b>, her faithful one, and <b>pure</b>, truly, even so, insists on it.

[plates[0].lenses[3].what] (printed over the drawing)

[plates[0].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
The score has the two opening lines three times, each time in C major and at the first tempo. Together they take about half its 70 bars. Between them come the order to answer and the last three lines; each slows to Adagio, stops on a pause and gives way to the same two lines. An idea that keeps returning makes a rondo. The libretto prints the two lines twice, so the middle return is the score’s. Gluck wrote in 1770 that the least change of pace or expression makes this aria a tune for puppets, and a contemporary is reported to have said that the melody would suit words of the opposite sense.

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)

Serious opera before the Romantics drew its language from the line of Petrarch, Tasso and Metastasio.

### Tab “Return” (compare)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Return

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Love’s terms are in Act 1; Orpheus’s cry is in Act 3, when Eurydice has died. He puts his loss in Love’s words. <b>Perdi</b>, you lose, becomes <b>perdo</b>, I lose, and the next line begins with the same six words, <i>and again, and forever</i>. A threat in Act 1 is a fact in Act 3, word for word, and the same line goes on to name the <b>legge</b>, the law, and death. After the aria <b>per sempre</b>, forever, comes a third time, now in a wish of his own: that pain may end with life. Love had said he would live on, wretched.

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Each half of the strophe, a stanza of the song, is two lines that rhyme at once and a third that waits: <b>così</b> with <b>dì</b>, then <b>asconde</b>; <b>dolor</b> with <b>cor</b>, then <b>risponde</b>, which rhymes with <b>asconde</b> three lines later. The four lines that rhyme at once stop on their last accent, and <b>dolor</b> and <b>cor</b> are words cut short, <i>dolore</i> and <i>core</i>: such rhymes are a mark of the <i>canzonetta</i>, a poem in short lines, usually set to music. The strophe’s one long reach of rhyme ends on <b>risponde</b>, answers. The sentence it closes says that she does not. The next two strophes end on the same word and the same rhyme.

### Tab “English” (compare)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
English

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The English is Manfredo Maggioni’s, from a libretto in both languages published for the Metropolitan Opera House. He keeps the short lines and in each half rhymes the first with the third: <i>call</i> with <i>fall</i>, <i>sighs</i> with <i>replies</i>. The rhymes move the sense in two places: <b>asconde</b>, hides, becomes <i>fall</i>, and <b>dolor</b>, grief, becomes <i>sighs</i>. His first line says <i>thee</i>, and so does the Italian printed with it in that libretto, a reworked text in four acts. Calzabigi’s line is about her, his <b>ben</b>, his good. In the three strophes Orpheus does not address her; he does that in the recitative between them, and in the aria of Act 3.

## What Auden said

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
No account of this opera by Auden has been found. The one place found where he names it is an essay of 1948 on the Greeks, and there it marks a date. He grants that, for sustained creative work in a single medium, the seventy-five years or so of Athenian drama are surpassed by the 125 years from Gluck’s *Orpheus* to Verdi’s *Otello*, which he calls “the golden age of European opera.” *Orpheus* stands first among the nine opera libretti on his list for the course.

[auden[0].source]
“The Greeks and Us” (1948), in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), p. 8

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
The Italian is Calzabigi’s libretto as Ricordi printed it in Milan in 1879, read by machine and checked against the printed pages and against a libretto printed in Bologna in 1771. No libretto or score printed in Vienna was seen.

[caveats[1]]
That print has a wrong letter in the aria’s first line, which is shown here as the print gives it where the two opening lines return. The print sets the aria as four lines and three; the space after the second line is not the print’s.

[caveats[2]]
The English is Manfredo Maggioni’s, from a libretto published for the Metropolitan Opera House, where it faces a reworked Italian text in four acts and the aria stands in Act 4; it does not always give Calzabigi’s sense.

[caveats[3]]
The stresses marked are one reader’s hearing, and a few light stresses could fairly be marked otherwise; the counts follow the rule that vowels meeting between words count as one syllable, also across a stop.

[caveats[4]]
The aria’s form and its seventy bars are those of Dörffel’s score for Peters, which follows the Paris version of 1774 with Orpheus’s part put back in its first range; this page was made without hearing the record on it. Boyé’s remark is known through Hanslick, Gluck’s through Newman’s English.
