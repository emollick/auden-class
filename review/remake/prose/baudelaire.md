## The head of the page

[title]
The dandy’s warning

[where]
*Intimate Journals*: *Rockets*, XIII and XXVI; *My Heart Laid Bare*, LXXII and LXXIX

[about] (under “The passage”)
Baudelaire writes these notes to himself on loose sheets in about his last ten years. He has a stroke in 1866 and dies in 1867; the notes are printed after his death, in the order his publisher pasted them. The first passage is from the last entry of *Rockets*, which says the world is ending and foretells an age that honors only money. The other two are from late entries of *My Heart Laid Bare* that begin with the word *Hygiene*; beside the third stands a maxim from *Rockets*, XIII.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden reads the journals through Baudelaire’s dandy, whose one ambition is to become conscious that he is uniquely himself and like no one else. A thought contrary to the dandy, he says, runs through them from the start: to love is to give oneself where a neighbor needs it. It seems to surface only now and then before the crisis of January 23, 1862. After it he finds the heart truly changed.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
a man fighting against time to eradicate a lifetime’s habits of thought and feeling, and set himself in order and acquire a history

[view.source]
Introduction to Charles Baudelaire, *Intimate Journals*, translated by Christopher Isherwood (Hollywood: Marcel Rodd, 1947), pp. 18–20 and 25–27; the closing pages are also in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 314–15

### Tab “Likeness” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Likeness

[plates[0].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
simile

[plates[0].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
a comparison that says one thing is like another; here the word is *as*

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The writer likens himself to someone, <b>I am as a tired man</b>, and keeps the man. The next sentence is about <b>that man</b>: <b>he</b> speaks, the <b>me</b> in the quotation is his, and no <i>I</i> comes back before the paragraph ends. The French does the same: <i>un homme</i>, <i>cet homme</i>, <i>il</i>. So the pride of the last lines is told of a third person, and by comparison: he is <b>proud</b> of being <b>less base</b> than the people who pass. Auden lists this pride among the dandy’s traits. The dandy’s mistake, he says, is to think one becomes an individual in spirit by becoming unlike other people.

### Tab “Delay” (sentence)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Delay

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The last sentence opens with a time, <i>The evening</i>, and forty-nine words pass before its main clause, the part that could stand alone. After the clause of time come six phrases, each headed by a word that describes the man: <b>cradled</b>, <b>forgetful</b>, <b>content</b>, <b>resigned</b>, <b>intoxicated</b>, <b>proud</b>. A handbook of composition calls this suspense: describing words set before their subject keep the sentence incomplete and throw the emphasis on its end. Six states come first, the main verb is <b>he said</b>, and the end is his question. In the other journal Baudelaire writes <i>A dandy does nothing</i>. Auden calls the dandy no man of action.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
<b>Sangfroid</b> is French for cool blood: in Littré’s dictionary, calm of soul, presence of mind. <b>Intoxicated</b> translates the French <i>enivré</i>, made drunk, and Littré adds a figurative sense: a moral drunkenness, as on praise. The phrase makes one man drunk on his own calm. It joins two terms that seem to contradict each other: the figure is called an oxymoron. <b>Dandyism</b> is built on an English word that French borrowed about 1830; Littré’s dandy is a man who carries the fashions to the point of ridicule. Auden says Baudelaire’s dandy looks to the bourgeois like a man of every excess and to himself like an exacting ascetic.

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[0].gloss] (set under the word)
French: *la destinée*

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[0].note] (a note on the word)

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[1].gloss] (set under the word)
rocked, as a baby is

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[1].note] (a note on the word)
The French is *bercé*; Littré’s first sense of *bercer* is to rock in a cradle, and a later one is to lull.

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[2].gloss] (set under the word)

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[2].note] (a note on the word)
The French is *enivré*, and under this verb Littré records a familiar saying: he gets drunk on his own wine, which means he thinks too well of himself.

Littré’s first sense of the French *conscience* is the feeling of oneself; his second is the soul’s secret judgment, which approves good acts and reproaches bad ones.

### Tab “French” (compare)

[plates[0].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
French

[plates[0].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
Shipley keeps Baudelaire’s order phrase for phrase and moves the last sentence into the past. Baudelaire writes <b>a volé</b>, has stolen, <b>passent</b>, pass, and <b>il se dit</b>, he says to himself: the time is the present. Shipley writes <i>stole</i>, <b>passed</b> and <b>he said</b>, and leaves out <i>to himself</i>. So in the French he asks himself, now, why it should matter to him where these <i>consciences</i> go. The paragraph opens by disclaiming <i>the charity of a doctor</i>, and Littré’s first sense of <i>charité</i> is love of one’s neighbor. The thought contrary to the dandy, Auden says, is that to love is to give oneself where a neighbor needs it.

[plates[0].lenses[3].ties[0].say] (a numbered remark)
The French is *he says to himself*: the tense is present, and the verb turns back on the speaker. Shipley’s *he said* is past and leaves out *to himself*.

[plates[0].lenses[3].ties[1].say] (a numbered remark)
The French is *those who pass*, in the present. The past tense is Shipley’s.

[plates[0].lenses[3].ties[2].say] (a numbered remark)
The French is *has stolen*, a compound past that looks back from the present of *he says*. Shipley’s *stole* puts the theft in a finished past. *Destinée* is the word he translates as *destiny* in the third passage.

[plates[0].lenses[3].ties[3].say] (a numbered remark)
*Sang-froid* goes over unchanged: English has had the phrase since 1712. *Dandysme* is the English word *dandy* with a French ending.

[plates[0].lenses[3].ties[4].say] (a numbered remark)
The aside is Baudelaire’s own and Shipley keeps it. This copy of the French has lost the first of its two dashes; the Paris edition of 1908 prints both.

## Passage 2: My Heart Laid Bare, LXXII: January 23, 1862  (plates[1], id "warning", from baudelaire/my-heart-laid-bare)

### Tab “Time” (sentence)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Time

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The first sentence says <b>always</b>. The second paragraph says <b>Now</b>, and <i>always</i> again, and then the time narrows to <b>to-day</b> and a date. No other entry in Shipley’s translation of the two journals gives the day on which it was written. The verbs change at the date. Before it stands something he has done: he has <b>cultivated</b> his hysteria. After it the verb is <b>felt</b>, twice, and what he feels passes <i>over me</i>. Baudelaire’s first verb there is <i>subi</i>, underwent. Auden calls this day the crisis. In another essay he writes that Baudelaire’s pose led to disaster, to his hearing this wind, as Baudelaire himself saw too late.

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)

[plates[1].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)

[plates[1].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The entry does not say what happened. This metaphor gives <b>imbecility</b> a <b>wing</b>. Imbecility itself stays two steps off: what he feels is a <b>gust</b>, the gust is from the wing, and the wing is imbecility’s. So what reaches him is moving air, and he calls it a <b>warning</b>. His sonnet “Le Gouffre,” The Gulf, has the wind and no wing: the wind of Fear, felt <i>mainte fois</i>, many a time. Auden quotes the entry as the crisis, and the last lines of his introduction have a bird: as Baudelaire spoke, it came down and struck.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
<b>Cultivated</b> is a farmer’s verb: Littré’s first sense of <i>cultiver</i> is to work the land so that it bears, and a later one is to form and develop. Its object here is a disease: in Littré, a nervous illness that comes in fits. So the entry presents the <b>hysteria</b> as his own work, done <i>with joy and terror</i>; then, with <i>Now</i>, come <b>vertigo</b> and the wing of <b>imbecility</b>. Auden takes up the phrase in two essays. A religion that trusts the mood of the moment leads first to this cultivation, he writes, and at last to despair; Baudelaire’s error, he says, was to make the aesthetic his religion.

### Tab “Maxim” (compare)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Maxim

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Auden sets these two notes side by side: the writer of the maxim now writes the budget. The maxim is one sentence, said to anyone, <i>you</i>, for <i>each letter</i>. It speaks of no payment: the letter is met with fifty lines written on a subject the French calls <i>extra-terrestre</i>, outside the earth, and <b>you are saved</b>. The budget begins with two other people, Jeanne Duval (<i>Jean</i>) and his mother, and their monthly sums; then come hours of work. Its first paragraph ends <b>We shall see the result</b>. Auden says these last pages show him racing time to undo the habits of a lifetime and put his life in order.

[plates[2].lenses[0].ties[0].say] (a numbered remark)
The maxim meets any creditor, at each letter. The budget has three names, a sum against each, and a total by the month.

[plates[2].lenses[0].ties[1].say] (a numbered remark)
The maxim orders writing and counts it in lines: fifty, on an abstract subject. The budget orders work and counts it by the clock, from six till noon.

[plates[2].lenses[0].ties[2].say] (a numbered remark)
The maxim promises: *you are saved*. Baudelaire’s French has the future, *you will be saved*. The budget promises nothing: *we shall see*.

### Tab “Verbs” (sentence)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Verbs

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The account has no verb: three names, three sums, a total. The two resolutions begin <b>To work</b>. The infinitive names an act and fixes no person, so no one is yet said to do the work. The first verb with a person is plural and future: <b>We shall see</b>. The first <i>I</i> only supposes: <b>I suppose</b>. Then the plain present: <b>There is still time</b>. In French the entry before this one begins, after its heading, <i>Trop tard peut-être</i>, too late perhaps, words Shipley’s text lacks. Auden’s introduction ends on the same question. By nature’s reckoning, he writes, Baudelaire was too late; by the spirit’s, one may believe, he came in time.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Three words come back from the first passage. There the man stole a few <i>hours of pleasure</i> from <i>fate</i>. Here he supposes he bases his <b>destiny</b> on <b>a few hours</b> of <b>toil</b>. <i>Fate</i> and <i>destiny</i> are one French word, <i>destinée</i>, and <i>heures</i>, hours, is in both sentences, so work stands where pleasure stood. Five entries earlier he names pleasure and work as the two ways to forget time and writes <i>Let us choose</i>. <b>Pleasure</b> then returns twice: in a question that breaks off, and as the pleasure of <i>a project carried out</i>, which he has <i>not yet known</i>. Auden says the heart has really changed between the dandy and what these last pages resolve.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden says that Baudelaire met his own nature and his century with two opposed figures of his own making, and held to them until shortly before his mind gave way: the dandy, the individual as hero, and the mass, which he thought boorish and found in Woman, in the man of business and in what he called the Belgian spirit. Auden then lists the dandy’s traits from the journals: he lives and sleeps before a looking glass, does nothing useful, is solitary and unhappy, and is proud of being less base than the people passing. That last trait is taken from the second sentence of the first passage.

[auden[0].source]
Introduction to Charles Baudelaire, *Intimate Journals* (1947), pp. 18–19

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
He then sums the dandy up. Like a hero of poetry he depends on fortune, on money and leisure for instance; like a hero of philosophy he must have the will to make a dandy of himself from the corrupt nature every man is born with. He is no man of action and no seeker of wisdom, and wants neither the admiration of men nor the knowledge of God. His one ambition is to become conscious that he is uniquely himself and like no one else. Auden calls him the hero of religion inverted: Lucifer, who proves himself free by refusing every order, whether God’s, society’s or his own nature’s.

[auden[1].source]
Introduction to Charles Baudelaire, *Intimate Journals* (1947), pp. 19–20

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Before he turns to the contrary thought, Auden names the dandy’s mistake. A man unaware of himself, he says, can rest in being by nature what no one else is. Once he is conscious of himself that is not enough, and he must set about becoming an individual in spirit. The danger then is the dandy’s mistake: to try to turn the first kind of individual into the second, and so to think that one becomes an individual in spirit by becoming unlike others, and not by becoming what one chooses to be.

[auden[2].source]
Introduction to Charles Baudelaire, *Intimate Journals* (1947), pp. 24–25

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
From the start, Auden says, a line of thought runs through the journals against the dandy and his pride in being unique. He quotes two sayings: no high pleasure is unrelated to prostitution, and no being is more prostituted than God. In terms meant to provoke, Baudelaire there recognizes the Christian idea of love against the Platonic one the dandy holds. He grants that to love is to give oneself, not to desire, and that the will to become oneself can only be a will to give oneself where a neighbor needs it. Before the crisis of January 23, 1862, Auden thinks, such ideas came only now and then.

[auden[3].source]
Introduction to Charles Baudelaire, *Intimate Journals* (1947), p. 25

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
His last lines are about time. By nature’s reckoning Baudelaire was too late. Seen by the spirit, he goes on, Baudelaire may be believed to have come in time: spirit needs time too, but a moment of it will do.

[auden[4].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
To the eye of nature, he was too late. As he spoke, the bird stooped and struck.

[auden[4].source]
Introduction to Charles Baudelaire, *Intimate Journals* (1947), p. 27; *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 314–15

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
He sets Baudelaire’s change of heart beside Rimbaud’s later and more showy turn from poet to trader, where he thinks the heart did not change: there, it seems to him, one kind of dandy was merely swapped for another, with the same pride and the same wish to be unique in both. Baudelaire’s humility sounds true to him because nothing showy is planned, no new career and no public farewell to poetry. Baudelaire asks in prayer only to use his gifts better.

[auden[5].source]
Introduction to Charles Baudelaire, *Intimate Journals* (1947), pp. 26–27; *Prose*, vol. II (2002), p. 314

[auden[6].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In *The Enchafèd Flood* the entry of January 23, 1862 is one of Auden’s instances that the hero too can perish. He quotes it in Isherwood’s translation, where *imbecility* has become *madness*. Of the dandy he says there that to the bourgeois he looks like a wild man who denies himself nothing, and that in his own eyes he is an exacting ascetic who scorns the bourgeois for being natural. He then quotes the journal on woman as the dandy’s opposite and on the dandy who should live and sleep before a looking glass.

[auden[6].source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), pp. 42 and 84

[auden[7].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Writing on Goethe’s *Italian Journey*, he says that the aesthetic religion, as Kierkegaard named it, trusts wholly to the mood of the moment and ends in despair, and that its first stage is what Baudelaire called cultivating one’s hysteria. Writing on Tennyson, he says that Baudelaire’s error, one Tennyson did not make, was to turn the aesthetic into a religion; that his pose was a way to avoid the need for a faith and ended in disaster, in his hearing the wind that the second passage records; and that he saw it too late.

[auden[7].source]
*Forewords and Afterwords* (1973; Faber and Faber printing of 1974), pp. 136 (“Italian Journey”) and 231–32 (“Tennyson”)

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
Auden’s introduction of 1947 was read only in snippets of a scanned copy, not page by page. Its page numbers are counted from the page headings as that copy prints them, and several of the headings, that of page 25 among them, are garbled there.

[caveats[1]]
The order of the entries is not Baudelaire’s. After his death his publisher pasted the loose sheets in an order the editors of 1908 call arbitrary, so the budget follows the dated entry only in the printed book, which is the order Auden reads.

[caveats[2]]
The English printed here is Shipley’s of 1919, not the Isherwood translation that Auden set and quotes. Isherwood has *madness* where Shipley has *imbecility*, and Shipley lacks some lines of the French, among them *Trop tard peut-être*.

[caveats[3]]
The French *il se dit* could be a past tense as well as a present. It is read as a present because *passent*, beside it, is present.

[caveats[4]]
That no other entry gives the day of its writing is true of Shipley’s text. The French edition of 1908 has a list of errands headed with a day of 1856 and a quoted sentence with its date in 1863; Shipley has neither.
