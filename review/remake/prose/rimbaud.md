## The head of the page

[title]
The experiment and its record

[where]
*A Season in Hell* (*Une saison en enfer*, 1873): the chapters “Alchemy of the Word” and “Adieu”

[about] (under “The passage”)
Rimbaud dated this short prose book April to August 1873, when he was eighteen. A first-person speaker gives what he calls the account of his hell. In the chapter “Alchemy of the Word” he tells the story of one of his follies, his attempt to invent a new language for poetry, and quotes seven of his own poems; “Adieu” is the last chapter. The French is that of the first edition, and the English beside it was printed, unsigned, in *The Dial* in 1920.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden writes that the Romantic artist becomes his own hero. He makes the experiment on himself and keeps the record, and a catastrophe drives him to the point of exhaustion. Rimbaud’s account of himself in the first passage below holds to some degree, Auden says, for every Romantic artist. Some came to see that the artist is no religious hero, only a man exploring what is possible.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
The artist who has thus to be at once the subject of his experiment and the recorder enjoys excitement and suffers terrors hardly known before.

[view.source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), chapter 3, pp. 147–49

### Tab “Tense” (sentence)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Tense

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The French here tells the past in two tenses. In a school grammar of the time the past definite shows a thing done in a time completely over, the imperfect a thing going on at a past time. Three steps are in the past definite: <b>Je m’habituai</b>, I got used; <b>Puis j’expliquai</b>, then I explained; <b>Je finis par</b>, I ended by. What he saw, was and envied is in the imperfect. The Dial marks the difference once, in <i>used to see</i>. So each step is told as done and over; the chapter’s first line announces the story of one of his follies.

### Tab “List” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
List

[plates[0].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)
asyndeton

[plates[0].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)
phrases set side by side with no joining word between them

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
After <b>je voyais</b>, I saw, come four things and then two nouns alone, with no <i>et</i>, the French for <i>and</i>, to join them. This is asyndeton; Littré’s dictionary says the joining words are dropped to gain speed. The first thing is set against what was there: the mosque stands <b>à la place d’une usine</b>, in place of a factory. The next three have no such anchor: a drum school made by angels, carriages on sky roads, a drawing room at a lake’s bottom. Nothing is said to be like anything; the verb is plain seeing. In the last clause a play’s title raises <b>épouvantes</b>, terrors.

### Tab “Count” (beat)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Count

[plates[1].lenses[0].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
five counted syllables, the accent on the last; the fourth line has three

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
French verse counts syllables, and its fixed accent falls on the last sounded syllable of the line. Auden says an English ear finds verse measured by syllables very hard to grasp. Three lines here count five. The fourth stops at three: <b>Au soleil</b>, with the sun. In the next three stanzas of the poem the fourth line counts five, and printed on its own in 1886 the poem has five syllables in this line too. Earlier in the chapter Rimbaud says he prided himself on inventing a poetic language with instinctive rhythms; this poem is one of the seven he gives.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[0].say] (a numbered remark)
The mute *e* of *Elle* runs into *est* and is not counted, so the line has five syllables.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)
A question of one word. It can be heard with its own stress and a pause after it.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[2].say] (a numbered remark)
Five syllables again. Lines of five are mostly mixed with longer ones. A history of French verse finds whole stanzas of them now and then in Old French pastourelles and romances, and less rarely in Rimbaud’s century, in Banville, Richepin and Verlaine.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[3].say] (a numbered remark)
A mute *e* at the end of a line is never counted.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[4].say] (a numbered remark)
Three syllables, where each line above has five.

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Three of the four lines end on one sound, <i>é</i>: <b>retrouvée</b>, found again; <b>éternité</b>, eternity; <b>mêlée</b>, mixed. By the rules of French verse only the first and third can rhyme together. They end in a silent <i>e</i>, which French calls a feminine ending; <i>éternité</i> does not. A history of French verse says the rules forbid rhyming the two kinds together even where they sound alike, and that a few modern poets break the rule. So the ear joins three lines where the rule joins two. The last line, <b>soleil</b>, sun, has no partner. Early in the chapter Rimbaud lists silly refrains and naive rhythms among the things he loved.

[plates[1].lenses[1].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the stanza. Kastner, *A History of French Versification*, chapter 3, on masculine and feminine rhyme. Rimbaud’s [list of what he loved](#/read/rimbaud-fr/s5:2), in the Dial’s words.

[plates[2].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)

[plates[2].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
the same word at the head of sentence after sentence, or of phrase after phrase

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Three sentences begin <b>J’ai</b>, I have. This is anaphora, one word set at the head of each sentence. Inside them the repeating goes on: <i>all</i> three times, <i>new</i> four times. What he claims goes from feasts, triumphs and dramas to new flowers, stars, flesh and tongues, and then to supernatural powers. Set side by side, the verbs claim less each time: he has created, he has tried to invent, he has believed that he was gaining. The next sentence begins <b>Eh bien!</b>, well, and says he must bury his imagination.

Time

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The main verbs go from past to present to future. The three claims stand in the past indefinite, <b>J’ai</b> with a participle: I have created, tried, believed. A school grammar of the day says this tense can be used of a time not yet over, where the past definite of the first passage is kept for a time completely past. The last verb is a command in the form for <i>we</i>: <b>allons</b>, let us go. Auden says this book is where Rimbaud spoke of himself before he took leave of art.

### Tab “Sentence” (sentence)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Sentence

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The middle paragraph is one sentence and one word. It begins <b>Moi! moi</b>, I, I, and before any main verb says he called himself magus or angel and was excused from all morality. The main clause comes at the fourteenth word, with a passive verb: <b>je suis rendu au sol</b>, I am given back to the ground. He did the naming; the giving back is done to him. Two things remain to do: a duty to look for, a rough reality to clasp. Then <b>Paysan!</b>, peasant. In “Bad Blood” masters and workmen alike are peasants, ignoble. Auden quotes this paragraph for the artist who finds he is no religious hero.

Three of the Dial’s choices change what the French says. <b>Dispensé</b>, permitted not to do what is commanded, becomes <i>free</i>. <b>Devoir</b>, duty, becomes <i>a job</i>, and <b>je dois</b>, I must, becomes <i>I am going to</i>. Littré defines a duty as what law or morality obliges one to do. So the French excuses him from morality and gives him a duty to look for; in the Dial that pair is gone. <b>Mage</b> is in Littré a priest of ancient Persia, one of the three Magi, or, in a use it calls out of date, a magician; the Dial has <i>a wizard</i>. The English Auden quotes keeps the magus and the duty.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[0].say] (a numbered remark)
Littré gives *mage* three senses: a priest of ancient Persia, one of the three Magi, and a magician, a use it calls out of date. The Dial takes the last. A chapter earlier it writes *Magi* for the same word.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[1].say] (a numbered remark)
To dispense someone from a thing, in Littré, is to permit him not to do what is commanded, as to let him off a fast. *Free* keeps the freedom and drops the permission.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[2].say] (a numbered remark)
*Sol* is the ground and, in particular, Littré says, the top layer of farmland. The paragraph ends on *Paysan*, a man of the country.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[3].say] (a numbered remark)
Littré defines *devoir*, duty, as what law or morality obliges one to do, and *morale* as the rules that show a man his duties. *A job* answers neither.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[4].say] (a numbered remark)
*Dois* is must. The noun *devoir* is the infinitive of this verb. The Dial has *am going to*, and the obligation is gone.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[5].say] (a numbered remark)
*Rugueuse* is said of a rough surface and comes from the Latin for a wrinkle. *Étreindre* is to bind or squeeze tight. A French proverb sets it against *embrasser*, to take in one’s arms.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[6].say] (a numbered remark)
The French says carried off. A familiar French oath, Littré notes, has the devil do the carrying, and the Dial writes him in.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[7].say] (a numbered remark)
Two words, a sentence to themselves, in the form for *we*: let us go. The Dial joins them to the sentence before and keeps to *I*. In “Bad Blood” it renders the same word *Come!*

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden’s two figures come from earlier in his chapter. Don Quixote is for him literature’s greatest picture of the religious hero, a man committed to something with absolute passion and forgetful of himself. Ishmael is the outcast son of Abraham in Genesis, whose name Melville gave to the teller of *Moby-Dick*; for Auden he is self-conscious, and a wanderer having no fixed commitment. Auden then quotes, in another English version, the last two paragraphs shown under “Adieu.” Artists of his own day, he adds, can neither have such heroic importance nor want it; reading the logs of the Romantics’ voyages, they should remember the voyagers’ courage.

[auden[0].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
the artist is not, as he had thought, Don Quixote, the Religious Hero, but only Ishmael, the explorer of possibility

[auden[0].source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), chapter 3, pp. 94, 96–97, 100–102 and 149–50

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Before quoting Rimbaud, Auden describes the Romantic artist. Earlier artists, he says, recorded what other men did and thought; the Romantic artist is himself the epic hero, and the deeds he sets down are his own. From about 1770 to 1914 the heroes of the age are individual geniuses and not men of action, and what an artist can learn he learns mostly from himself. He is a *poète maudit*, a poet under a curse: a disaster has singled him out and gives him the passion to go on. Auden asks whether a longing for innocence does not belong to a life spent exploring the unknown and unconscious.

[auden[1].source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), chapter 3, pp. 147–48

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden’s sonnet “Rimbaud” gives the life in fourteen lines. Cold made the child a poet; drink, paid for by a weaker poet, disordered his senses and rid him of the nonsense he had grown up with, until he turned from poetry and from weakness alike. Verse had come to seem a sickness of the ear. The sonnet leaves him riding through Africa and dreaming of another self, an engineer with a son, whose truth liars could accept.

[auden[2].source]
“Rimbaud,” in *The Collected Poetry of W. H. Auden* (1945), pp. 121–22

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In *New Year Letter*, published in 1941, the year of the course, Auden pictures the tribunal before which each poet must answer for his work; each may choose whom he faces. Three sit on Auden’s bench. The one presiding was taken through the three realms of the dead: Dante’s journey. William Blake sits on his right. On his left sits the young Rimbaud, frowning, an adolescent whom the poem calls quick and intolerant and credits with killing off an old rhetoric. He mutters a phrase from “Bad Blood”: *terror is not French*. Auden’s notes to the poem give that passage in French.

[auden[3].source]
*New Year Letter* (1941), Part I, in *The Collected Poetry of W. H. Auden* (1945), pp. 269–70, and his note to line 195

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Reviewing a life of Rimbaud in 1939, Auden judged that Rimbaud the man of action failed and tormented himself, because the will alone is not enough to live by. In 1947, writing on Baudelaire, he found no real change of heart in Rimbaud’s turn from poet to trader: to him the trader was the old dandy in a new form, as proud as before and as bent on being unlike everyone else.

[auden[4].source]
“Heretics” (1939) and the introduction to Baudelaire’s *Intimate Journals* (1947), in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 32 and 314–15

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In his inaugural lecture at Oxford in 1956 Auden speaks of the Rimbaud Myth: the story that a great poet gave up writing because he chose to and not, as Coleridge did, because he had run out of things to say. He is fairly sure the story is not true. As a myth, he says, it weighs on the conscience of artists in his century. He brings it up while saying that a poet’s wish to find a justification for writing verse in the first place has lately grown stronger.

[auden[5].source]
“Making, Knowing and Judging” (1956), in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 53–54

[auden[6].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Writing on Marianne Moore, Auden says that an English ear can hardly take in verse measured by nothing but the count of its syllables. His case is the French twelve-syllable line: the English ear, he says, hears most such lines as anapests, two light syllables and a stress, a measure that in English goes with light verse.

[auden[6].source]
“Marianne Moore,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 297

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
Auden set this book for his class in a translation by Schwartz, published by New Directions, which is in copyright. The English printed here is the unsigned version from *The Dial* of 1920. The English Auden quotes in 1949 is not the Dial’s.

[caveats[1]]
No record was found of what Auden said about Rimbaud in the 1941 class. The view given here comes from lectures of 1949, printed as *The Enchafèd Flood*; the page numbers are those of the Vintage Books edition of 1967.

[caveats[2]]
Three things given here as Auden’s were not seen in full copies of his text: the review “Heretics” (1939), the introduction to Baudelaire’s *Intimate Journals* (1947) and his note to line 195 of *New Year Letter*.

[caveats[3]]
In this book the last line of the stanza has three syllables; printed on its own in 1886 it has five. It is not known whether Rimbaud shortened it on purpose or wrote the poem down from memory.

[caveats[4]]
The stress marked on *Quoi?* is one reader’s hearing. In the first passage the form *finis* could also be a present tense; it is read as a past, as the Dial reads it. The third passage leaves out the short sixth paragraph of “Adieu,” as Auden’s quotation does.
