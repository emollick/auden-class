## The head of the page

[title]
The dynamo and the Virgin

[where]
*The Education of Henry Adams*, chapters 25, 19 and 1

[about] (under “The passage”)
Henry Adams, grandson and great-grandson of Presidents, wrote the story of his own life in 1905, at sixty-seven, and by 1907 had a hundred copies printed privately. It was published in 1918, the year he died. He tells it in the third person. The passages here come from 1900, when the scientist Samuel Langley shows him the dynamos at the Paris Exposition; from 1870, when his sister dies of lockjaw in Italy after a cab accident; and from the first page, where he is born in Boston in 1838.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden took the chapter’s two symbols for his own essay. For him they are two real worlds: the Dynamo’s, of masses and recurring events, told in numbers, and the Virgin’s, of faces and single events, told only in speech. Treating the first as the second is an illusion; its religion prays to the Dynamo. He says Adams took Venus and the Virgin for one person, when Venus is an impersonal force of nature in disguise.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
it was the Dynamo he worshiped, not the Virgin

[view.source]
“The Virgin & The Dynamo,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 61–63

### Tab “Felt” (compare)

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The passage under The Virgin comes six paragraphs later, and both passages use the words <b>force</b> and <b>felt</b>. Between them Adams gives his reason: he can bring such forces to one value only by how they have been felt, and he calls this <i>translating rays into faith</i>. The machine is likened to the Cross, and the Virgin’s force to X-rays. To the dynamo <b>one began to pray</b>; of Venus and the Virgin no American, he says, was ever <b>truly afraid</b>. Auden quoted from both in one note of 1941. For him these are two worlds, one told in numbers and one only in speech, and to treat either as the other is an illusion.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
The essay names two illusions. The aesthetic one takes the world of masses for a world of faces and makes a nature that is magical and has many gods; the sentence quoted here gives its religion. The scientific one takes the world of faces for one of masses and makes history a mechanism; its religion turns the Virgin into a statistic. Without art, he adds, there would be no idea of the sacred, and without science the gods people worship would always be false ones.

[auden[0].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
The aesthetic religion says prayers to the Dynamo.

[auden[0].source]
“The Virgin & The Dynamo,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 62

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
By nature, he says in the same pages, people tend to put a face on any power they imagine is responsible for their lives, and they do it to avoid responsibility: they want to say that they cannot help what they do. The pagan nature gods have masks and no real faces, because a real face answers for itself and those gods answer for nothing. Nature may rightly be given a real face, the Madonna’s for one, but only after the pagan mask is off and she is seen to be a world of masses, responsible for no one.

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In his essay on American poetry Auden quotes the sentences printed here under The Virgin, with one from the paragraph before, and gives his reason for what they say. A European writer, he says, could count on a nature that had its myths, had been made human and was friendly on the whole, and on landscapes with faces a person could know and trust. The wild nature of America cannot be thought of as human or as a person. The cause, for him, was the dissenters of the Mayflower, who feared images, and also that the nature Americans had reason to fear was nothing anyone could picture as a mother.

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In 1941, the year of the course, Auden published *New Year Letter*. One line of the poem names the Virgin and the Dynamo, and his note to it quotes Adams twice and adds nothing: the sentence that opens the first passage here, and then the whole paragraph that ends with the sentences under The Virgin. The American edition, *The Double Man*, lists the *Education* among its modern sources.

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
Auden’s note of 1941 and the list of sources in *The Double Man* were read only in search snippets of scanned copies. No full page of them was seen.

[caveats[1]]
The word *Omit* beside this book on the reading list is in an unknown hand, and it is not known whether the class read the book.

[caveats[2]]
The count of *pray* is a count of the word. Adams also speaks of himself as kneeling, without that word, at the foot of the Cross in [chapter 5](#/read/adams/c05:5) and before the Virgin’s window in [chapter 32](#/read/adams/c32:15).

[caveats[3]]
The counts follow the text printed here and their own rules: only *force* and *forces* are counted, a chapter’s years are those in its title, and quotation marks decide which sentences are the storyteller’s own.

[caveats[4]]
The book does describe the bronze figure that Saint-Gaudens made for Adams at Rock Creek cemetery, in [chapter 21](#/read/adams/c21:24), without saying whose grave it marks.
