## The head of the page

[title]
Abraham told four ways

[where]
*Fear and Trembling*: the Preparation, the Panegyric on Abraham and the Preliminary Expectoration, in the English of L. M. Hollander (1923)

[about] (under “The passage”)
Kierkegaard published this book in Copenhagen in 1843 under the name Johannes de silentio, John of silence. Its subject is Genesis 22, where God tells Abraham to sacrifice his son Isaac. The first passage comes from four paragraphs on weaning a child, one after each of four retellings of the story. The second comes early in a speech in praise of Abraham, and in the third the narrator, who says he cannot understand Abraham, imagines meeting a man of the same faith in the Copenhagen of his day.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden cites Bonhoeffer, who died a martyr: an earthly happiness that God grants should simply be accepted. Kierkegaard could not truthfully have claimed as much for himself, Auden writes, but his Knight of Faith, who is drawn in this book, shows he knew this was a defect in him and no virtue. Kierkegaard, he says, holds such a man to be what everyone ought to be and admits he cannot understand him.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
The ideal Christian he describes is happily married, looks like a cheerful grocer, and is respected by his neighbors.

[view.source]
“A Knight of Doleful Countenance” (1968), in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), p. 192

### Tab “Refrain” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Refrain

[plates[0].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
refrain

[plates[0].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
words that come back at a set place in a song or poem; here, in the paragraph after each of four tellings, the same opening and a matching last sentence

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Kierkegaard tells Abraham’s journey four times, and after each telling comes a paragraph about a mother weaning her child. Three of these open on the same seven words, and the second adds <b>has grown and</b>. All four close on a sentence that begins <b>Happy</b> and says who: three times <b>he who</b>, once <b>the child who</b>. In a song, a phrase that returns at a set place is a refrain, and the book’s subtitle is <i>Dialectical Lyric</i>. The returning paragraph marks each telling as one of a set. The narrator says so: a man thought about the event in these and <i>in many similar ways</i>, and always ended by asking who can understand Abraham.

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Each last sentence takes up a word of its own paragraph: <b>wean</b>, <b>mother</b>, <b>sorrow</b>, <b>stronger food</b>. The paragraphs say what a mother does, as a rule. Three last sentences turn to the past: <b>needed not</b>, <b>lost not</b>, <b>needed not</b>. ## Passage 2: Greater than all  (plates[1], id "greater", from kierkegaard/p3)

Greater than all

### Tab “Build” (sentence)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Build

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Two sentences run on one plan. First come others who were great, two in the first sentence and four in the second. Then <b>but</b> turns to one who is greater than all of them. The first time that one is <b>he who believed in God</b>. The second time the place holds a name, <b>Abraham</b>, which the speech has not yet said. Before the name comes, the paragraph that ends here gives this verdict five times, each time to an unnamed <i>he who</i>. The school pattern for a speech of praise compares its subject with someone else, to raise the praise. This speech builds the top rank first, so the name arrives already defined.

[plates[1].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)

[plates[1].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)
a statement that contradicts itself on the surface and is still meant as true; here, four times, a ground of greatness defined by a word of blame

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The second sentence names four things heroes are great by: <b>strength</b>, <b>wisdom</b>, <b>hopes</b>, <b>love</b>. Then it gives Abraham the same four in the same order, each defined by a word of blame: <b>weakness</b>, <b>folly</b>, <b>madness</b>, <b>hatred of one’s self</b>. Each of the four is a paradox, and the book later uses the word of Abraham: his life, the narrator says, has <i>the enormous paradox</i> for its content.

[plates[1].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
In this translation the speech has eighteen paragraphs and 3,375 words. The first two never say <i>Abraham</i>. They are about the poet, who cannot do a hero’s deeds and can only admire and remember. The third ranks the great, and its last sentence is the first to name Abraham, 749 words in. Every later paragraph names him, fifty more times. The school pattern for such a speech runs: introduction, origins and upbringing, deeds, a comparison that lifts the subject, a closing exhortation or prayer. Kierkegaard’s comparison comes early, ahead of the life and of the name, so the name arrives at the top of a ranking already built.

## Passage 3: The man at the window  (plates[2], id "window", from kierkegaard/p4)

The man at the window

### Tab “Yet” (sentence)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Yet

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Three statements give what anyone could see: the pipe, a likeness to the <b>green-grocer</b>, the unconcern of an idler. Then <b>and yet</b> turns to what cannot be seen: the price of his leisure, <b>the absurd</b>, <b>the movement of infinity</b>. <b>Yet</b> comes four times, as <i>dog</i> does in the Danish, and before the last the narrator breaks in: he could be furious, if only from envy. The paragraphs before say that no outward sign betrays the man. So the look comes first, and what it does not show comes after <i>yet</i>. Auden’s summary of this portrait keeps both halves, the ideal Christian and the cheerful grocer, and adds that Kierkegaard finds the man incomprehensible.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Two kinds of word meet here. From the street: <b>pipe</b>, <b>green-grocer</b>, <b>happy-go-lucky fellow</b>. From the book’s own stock: <b>the absurd</b>, <b>the movement of infinity</b>. Between them stands a phrase of the Danish New Testament: <b>purchases his leisure</b> is, in the Danish, <i>buys the opportune time</i>, as in Ephesians 5:16. The sentence needs both kinds, since it says of one man what the street sees and what the book claims. In lines this translation leaves out just after, Kierkegaard names what this knight of faith alone can do: express <i>the sublime in the pedestrian</i>. The <i>Expectoration</i> of the section’s title is properly a coughing up from the chest, and figuratively a pouring out of the heart.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In 1947, in his introduction to Baudelaire’s *Intimate Journals*, Auden wrote that the Old Testament has its own idea of the hero, which orthodox Christianity works out more consciously. His example is Abraham, whom poetry would not count a hero, since he has no gifts out of the ordinary. Auden sets him beside Agamemnon, who gives up his daughter for the army of the Greeks and suffers for it. Abraham, by contrast, is told to give up his son to try his faith. He tells no one and obeys, and so the son is spared and a blessing follows. The passage does not name this book.

[auden[0].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
What makes him exceptional is that he, outwardly an average man, is called by God to an exceptional task and obeys.

[auden[0].source]
Introduction to Charles Baudelaire, *Intimate Journals* (1947), in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 309–10

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
On the same page as his sentence about the cheerful grocer, Auden writes that Kierkegaard’s mastery of his own language shows even through translation: he commands every style, lofty or homely, abstract or concrete. His one great fault as a writer, which Auden finds common in lonely men of genius, is that he does not know where to end.

[auden[1].source]
“A Knight of Doleful Countenance” (1968), in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), p. 192

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Introducing a selection of Kierkegaard in 1952, Auden wrote that his pages are often those of a poet or a philosopher, and that he was neither. He was a preacher, whose work was to set out and defend Christian teaching and Christian conduct. Auden compares him with Newman: both had to preach to a society that had turned secular and still called itself Christian.

[auden[2].source]
“Søren Kierkegaard” (1952), in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), p. 168

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Reviewing a translation of *Either/Or* in 1944, Auden advised a Christian reader to begin Kierkegaard with this book or with *Training in Christianity*.

[auden[3].source]
“A Preface to Kierkegaard,” *The New Republic*, 15 May 1944, in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 213–14

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Donald Pearce, a student in the class, wrote his notes just after the lectures, since note-taking in class was forbidden. By his account, on a Thursday late in October 1941 Auden described the aesthetic and the ethical attitudes, said that Kierkegaard treats these categories in full, and called him the last of Europe’s great thinkers on ethics. His scheme, as Pearce gives it: ethics gives up the individual for the universal, aesthetics does the reverse, and religion makes the individual a martyr. Religion, he said, is where the other two meet. What was seen of these pages does not name this book.

[auden[4].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), pp. 129–31 and 134

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Kenneth Millar, who took the class in the fall of 1941 and later wrote novels as Ross Macdonald, kept notes on it. Michael Kreyling reports that his fullest notes were on this book and on Kafka’s *The Castle*.

[auden[5].source]
Michael Kreyling, *The Novels of Ross Macdonald* (2005), pp. 39–40

[auden[6].says] (a paraphrase of him)
The grocer of Auden’s sentence of 1968 is already in a poem of 1940. Edward Mendelson finds that the sonnets of “The Quest” vary themes of Kierkegaard. In one, “The Hero,” the man who has succeeded in his quest wins little respect, since to other people he seems too much a grocer, and all that sets him apart from people who have run no such risk is the pleasure he takes in small things and in routine. Mendelson sets him beside the knight of faith, whom one would take for a shopkeeper.

[auden[6].source]
“The Hero,” in *The Collected Poetry of W. H. Auden* (1945), pp. 259–60; Edward Mendelson, *Early Auden, Later Auden: A Critical Biography* (2017), pp. 459–60

[auden[7].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Two more poems of these years use the book, by Mendelson’s account. “In Sickness and in Health” (1940) calls the command to rejoice absurd because Kierkegaard’s Abraham believed by virtue of the absurd. The idea of two lines of “At the Grave of Henry James” (1941), about dancing lightly, comes from the book’s description of the knights of infinite resignation as dancers. That description stands just after the third passage here, in lines this translation leaves out.

[auden[7].source]
Edward Mendelson, *Early Auden, Later Auden: A Critical Biography* (2017), pp. 476 and 485

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
The English is L. M. Hollander’s selection of 1923: about a quarter of the book (he says a third). It leaves out the three Problems and much else, and trims the speech in praise of Abraham. Counts and paragraphs follow his text. Which translation Auden’s class read was not found.

[caveats[1]]
Auden’s sentences of 1947 and 1944 were read only in search snippets of the volume reprinting them; those of 1947 do not name this book. His lecture of October 1941 is known from notes a student, Donald Pearce, wrote afterward; what was seen of them does not name it either.

[caveats[2]]
The second and fourth paragraphs on weaning are shown whole; the marks of omission there only divide opening from close. The first and third are shown without their middles. Hollander’s *he* and *his child* say more than the Danish, which has *the one who* and *the child*.

[caveats[3]]
The count of twelve likenesses in the portrait takes each named figure once and leaves out one general phrase about worldly people.

[caveats[4]]
The New Testament sources for the second passage are those proposed in the notes of the Danish edition, which says *probably* of one and *perhaps* of another. *The sublime in the pedestrian* is this page’s word-for-word English for Kierkegaard’s Danish, in lines the translation printed here leaves out.
