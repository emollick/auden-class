## The head of the page

[title]
Violetta alone

[where]
*La Traviata*, Act 1, Scenes 3 and 5, and the last lines of Act 3

[about] (under “The passage”)
Violetta Valery, a Paris courtesan, is the title’s *traviata*, the woman led astray. At a party in her house Alfredo Germont tells her he has loved her for a year; she has only friendship to give, she says, and hands him a flower to bring back the next day. The guests leave, and for the rest of the act she is alone on the stage. The third passage is the end of the opera: she is dying of consumption, with Alfredo, his father, her maid and the Doctor beside her.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden wrote no essay on this opera but took an example from its last scene. Opera, he held, is about human willfulness: people will have their feelings, cost what it may. What is sung is so, and a singer cannot be shown deceiving herself as a character in a play can. At most the orchestra may contradict the singer, as it does, he says, when Germont comes to Violetta’s deathbed.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
Self-deception is impossible in opera because music is immediate, not reflective; whatever is sung is the case.

[view.source]
“Notes on Music and Opera,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 470–71

### Tab “Measure” (beat)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Measure

[plates[0].lenses[1].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
seven syllables by the Italian count: the last accent always on the sixth

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
By the Italian count every line here is a seven: its last accent falls on the sixth syllable, and eight, seven or six syllables are heard as two unstressed ones follow, or one, or none. Alfredo’s four go eight, seven, seven, six and stop on <i>cor</i>, heart, rhymed with <i>amor</i>, love. Violetta’s song is in the same measure and ends its eighth line on <i>all’amor</i>, to love, so his four lines close her stanza without a seam. The mixed endings mark the form that became the opera aria. Auden and Kallman wrote that a convention of forms, settled before Verdi’s middle years, assured the composer a text he could set.

[plates[0].lenses[1].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)

[plates[0].lenses[1].points[2].say] (a numbered remark)

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The four lines call love the soul (in her song, the heartbeat) of the whole universe, mysterious, <i>altero</i>, proud, and <i>croce e delizia</i>, cross and delight, to the heart. Torment and pleasure are joined by nothing more than <i>e</i>, and. <i>Cor</i> for heart and <i>accenti</i> for words are forms the dictionary marks as poetic, the old stock of libretto verse. The woman who repeats them is a courtesan, Auden’s own word for Violetta. He held that everybody has joyful, tender and noble feelings, and that opera can show it where spoken drama cannot: a song may come at the wrong moment, never from the wrong person.

### Tab “English” (compare)

[plates[0].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
English

[plates[0].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
The English is from a New York libretto that names no translator. It keeps the frame: four lines that come back, and a last line that joins two nouns and stops on the word heart. Inside it the soul and the universe are gone, a <b>chain</b> comes in, the cross becomes <b>pain</b>, and three lines running rhyme on two syllables. Auden and Kallman wrote that the translation of an aria need give only its emotion. They also wrote that two-syllable rhymes, the commonest in Italian, are mostly comic in English, and that in a tender scene no rhyme is better than a laughable one.

The recitative, her sung speech before the song, ends in two lines of eleven syllables. Then the line turns to eight, stressed on the third and seventh syllables in all eight lines. It is an old line, used in dance songs, religious songs and carnival songs. The guests took their leave in it, with <i>gioir</i>, rejoicing, and <i>piacer</i>, pleasure, at line ends; <i>gioire</i> and <i>piacer</i> end lines here. A fast song that closes an aria is a cabaletta, and its effect lies in the even return of one rhythmic figure. Auden and Kallman say it is written to let the singer show her skill.

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Like her first recitative, this one closes on two lines that rhyme, and the rhyme joins <i>gioire</i>, to take joy, to <i>finire</i>, to end, a verb that can mean to die. Opera, Auden held, imitates human willfulness: people will have their feelings whatever it costs them. In the song, printed as two groups of four lines, rhyme ties the halves together, the second line to the fifth and the fourth to the eighth. As sung, six of the song’s eight lines have other words, and every rhyme is kept. For Auden a librettist’s verses are expendable once they have prompted the tune.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The question is <i>che far degg’io</i>, what must I do, and the song answers with the same verb: <i>sempre libera degg’io</i>, always free I must. <i>Deggio</i> is an old form of I must; by the dictionary the verb names an obligation and, often, a will set on something. The song states a duty she lays on herself, and its first words are always free. The English begins <b>Let</b> me and has no word for free. In Auden’s view a sung role commands its fate more than a spoken one, even a self-destroyer’s, and the great age of opera, Mozart’s to Verdi’s, fell in the age that believed in freedom without question.

### Tab “Scene” (shape)

[plates[1].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
Scene

[plates[1].lenses[3].what] (printed over the drawing)
Violetta’s scene alone, part by part

[plates[1].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
The scene has four parts: recitative, slow song, recitative, fast song, in eight, twenty-four, eight and eight lines. A short fast song to close an aria, the cabaletta, was characteristic of Italian opera in the first half of the nineteenth century. In the slow song she wonders whether he is the man she once pictured; in the fast one she chooses pleasure. Between them the libretto gives her an instant of thought, and her next word is <i>Follie</i>, follies; she calls the thought a vain delirium. The convention sets a fast song after the slow one; in this scene the eight lines of recitative between them are where she changes her mind.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Her last speech begins <i>È strano</i>, it is strange, and breaks off in <i>Oh gio... ia</i>, oh joy, the word broken in two: the two exclamations of her first speech alone in Act 1. There the strange thing was his words in her heart and the joy was being loved; here the strange thing is that the pain has stopped, and the stage direction calls her <i>rianimata</i>, revived. Self-deception, Auden held, cannot be sung, since what is sung is so; at most the orchestra can contradict the singer, and his example is its music as Germont comes to her deathbed, when this scene begins. Then the Doctor says <i>È spenta</i>, she is extinguished: a word used of flames.

### Tab “Shared” (figure)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Shared

[plates[2].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)
antilabe

[plates[2].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)
one line of verse divided between two or more speakers, the pieces together keeping the measure

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Thirteen printed lines make eight lines of seven syllables by the Italian count, the measure of her slow song, rhymed like the eight lines sung to her just before. Four are shared between speakers; the first printing sets each continuation further to the right, and the marks join the pieces. In the fifth she sings <b>ritorno a vivere</b>, I return to living. In the sixth, her broken <i>gio... ia</i>, joy, is the first half and the others’ <i>muor</i>, she dies, the second. It stops on its last accent and rhymes with <i>dolor</i>, grief, the libretto’s last word. The others finish the line she breaks off, and then the stanza, in its measure and its rhymes.

### Tab “English” (compare)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
English

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The English is from the libretto published for the Metropolitan Opera House, the Italian with an English translation, in a copy printed between 1935 and 1950. It gives the first exclamation as <b>strange</b> here but as wondrous in Act 1, and the last as <b>happiness</b> where Act 1 had joy, so its reader does not meet the same words twice. Piave’s one word for her pain and the others’ grief becomes <b>pains</b>, <b>sorrow</b> and <b>grief</b>. Auden and Kallman wrote that in the opera house opera should be sung in its own language, and that those who want to follow should buy such a libretto and read it beforehand.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In talk that Alan Ansen wrote down in 1947, Auden called *La Traviata* a great work and said that he had discovered Verdi in the past five years.

[auden[0].source]
Alan Ansen, *The Table Talk of W. H. Auden*, edited by Nicholas Jenkins (1990)

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden wrote that joyful, tender and noble feelings are not kept for noble characters. Everybody has them, the most depraved included. Opera can show this and spoken drama cannot, because nobody talks in song: a song can come at the wrong moment but cannot be wrong for the person who sings it.

[auden[1].source]
“Notes on Music and Opera,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 469–70

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In a sense, he wrote, no opera can be tragic: whatever goes wrong for its people, they are getting their own way. A singer playing a jilted bride on the point of suicide is, as every listener can tell, enjoying herself greatly.

[auden[2].source]
“Notes on Music and Opera,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 468

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Music, he wrote, is by nature active: it expresses will, and a will that gives way cannot be put into song. In the same essay he says that verismo, the naturalist school of opera, exchanged the gods and princes of the old serious opera for exotic settings, the courtesans of *La Traviata* and *Manon* among them, and that these are as far from the ordinary operagoer’s life as Olympus or Versailles.

[auden[3].source]
“Cav & Pag,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 478

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
A librettist’s verses, he wrote, are not written for the public. They are a letter meant for the composer alone. Their one moment comes when they prompt a melody in him, and after that they can be spared. In song, he adds, it is the syllables that cannot be spared.

[auden[4].source]
“Notes on Music and Opera,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 473

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
A play can let a mistake be found out slowly. In a libretto, he wrote, the finding out has to be abrupt, because music cannot exist where things are uncertain.

[auden[5].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
song cannot walk, it can only jump

[auden[5].source]
“Notes on Music and Opera,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 471

[auden[6].says] (a paraphrase of him)
He set the great age of opera between Mozart and Verdi and tied it to an age that believed in freedom without question. For him a high C sung dead on pitch disproves the idea that chance or fate pulls the strings.

[auden[6].source]
“Notes on Music and Opera,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 474

[auden[7].says] (a paraphrase of him)
With Chester Kallman he wrote that a translator must find the right style for each opera: the Queen of the Night, who is supernatural, cannot talk like Violetta, who is a courtesan. A translation, they add, must fit the music as it is sung, and for an aria it need give only the emotion.

[auden[7].source]
W. H. Auden and Chester Kallman, “Translating Opera Libretti,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 485, 491 and 493

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
Auden wrote no essay on *La Traviata*. The view given here is his general account of opera, from pages where this opera is one of his examples; fitting it to these passages is this page’s doing. That he called the opera a great work is known at second hand.

[caveats[1]]
The Italian is the libretto as first printed for Venice, in a transcription that differs from the page in a few marks of punctuation and spells out the speakers’ names. As sung, to judge by old librettos, the fast song has other words in six lines, and Alfredo sings *palpito*.

[caveats[2]]
The English is an old libretto version by an unnamed translator. It is free, and in the fast song it follows the words as sung, not the lines shown beside it.

[caveats[3]]
Syllables are counted by the Italian rules, and the stresses marked are one reader’s. Three of the last lines of the opera count as lines of seven only if the vowel that ends one speaker’s words runs into the vowel that begins the next speaker’s.

[caveats[4]]
The return of Alfredo’s tune in Violetta’s song is reported from Abramo Basevi’s study of 1859 and Gustav Kobbé’s opera guide of 1919; no score was seen. *Antilabe* is a term of Greek drama, taken here from a Greek lexicon; no handbook of Italian verse was found that uses it.
