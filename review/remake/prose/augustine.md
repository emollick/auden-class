## The head of the page

[title]
The restless heart

[where]
*Confessions*, Books I, II and X

[about] (under “The passage”)
Augustine, a bishop in Roman North Africa, writes the *Confessions* in Latin between 397 and 400, in his forties. He tells his life to God: his boyhood, a night in his sixteenth year when he and some friends rob a pear tree and throw the fruit to hogs, his conversion at Milan at thirty-one, his mother’s death. Then he turns to what he is now, and to memory, time and the opening of Genesis. The English is E. B. Pusey’s of 1838.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden said that the idea of the gratuitous act, done for nothing, first comes up in the *Confessions*, with the pear tree, and is alien to Greek thought. A Christian like Augustine, he wrote, begins ethics with it: an act neither reasonable nor pleasant, a bare assertion of the self as its own master. A man acts from love of self or of God; reasons and appetites come second.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
Man, that is to say, always acts either self-loving, just for the hell of it, or God-loving, just for the heaven of it

[view.source]
“Augustus to Augustine” (*The New Republic*, 25 September 1944), in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), pp. 36–37; the lecture on *Othello* in *Lectures on Shakespeare*, edited by Arthur Kirsch (2000), p. 197

The first sentence is borrowed: two psalm verses, turned from <i>the Lord</i> to <b>Thou</b>. Augustine’s own words begin at <b>And</b>. ### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Pusey’s <b>restless</b> and <b>repose</b> hide a repetition. In Augustine’s Latin they are <i>inquietum</i>, not at rest, and <i>requiescat</i>, comes to rest, both built on <i>quies</i>, rest. So the sentence names rest twice: first as what the heart is without, then as what it comes to in God. <b>For Thyself</b> is <i>ad te</i>, and the first sense of <i>ad</i> is direction, toward. Read that way, God made <b>us</b> toward himself, and the heart is restless until it rests in him. <b>Awakest</b> is <i>excitas</i>, you rouse. At the head of Book XI Augustine uses the same verb of himself: by telling his story he rouses his own devotion and his readers’.

### Tab “Itself” (figure)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Itself

[plates[1].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
antithesis

[plates[1].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
two opposed things set side by side, often in phrases of the same build; here, three times, a missing motive and the deed

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Three times a clause turns on <b>but</b>, and each time the noun after it takes up the word before it: <i>ill</i> and <b>the ill itself</b>, <i>faulty</i> and <b>my fault itself</b>, <i>the shame</i> and <b>the shame itself</b>. The first half is the place for a motive, and each time it says there was none. Pusey’s <b>temptation</b> is the Latin <i>causa</i>, a cause: his ill had no cause but itself. Two paragraphs later Augustine gives the rule this breaks: nobody believes in a crime done for no cause, and even the conspirator Catiline had one. Auden calls such an act gratuitous and says the idea first comes up here. The repeated nouns are Augustine’s; Pusey adds <i>itself</i> twice.

### Tab “Loved” (figure)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)

[plates[1].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)

[plates[1].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
<b>I loved</b> heads three clauses. Words repeated at the head of clause after clause are called anaphora. The verb stays the same and its object changes: first <i>it</i>, the ill, then <i>to perish</i>, then <i>mine own fault</i>. The lines give the love and no reason for it. The verb is <i>amavi</i>, the one Augustine uses of his love of God in the passage from Book X. In Auden’s summary of Augustine a man always acts from love of himself or of God, and his reasons and appetites come second. The Latin says <i>amavi</i> a fourth time, closing the next clause; Pusey leaves that one out.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
<b>Gratuitously</b> is Pusey’s word for <i>gratis</i>: for nothing, with no return. Auden’s name for the act told here is the French for a gratuitous act, <i>acte gratuit</i>. <i>Gratis</i> is a form of <i>gratia</i>, favor, the word that gives English <i>grace</i>. Augustine uses both. Six paragraphs later he ascribes to God’s <i>gratia</i> his pardon and the evil he did not do, and he calls the crime he loved <i>gratuitum</i>. So his Latin names the crime and the grace from one root. Pusey loses the pair there: he writes <i>a sin for its own sake</i>. <b>Fault</b> is <i>defectus</i>, a defection or a lack, and Augustine says later that the theft he loved <i>was nothing</i>.

### Tab “Book” (shape)

[plates[1].lenses[3].what] (printed over the drawing)
Book II: how many words tell the theft, and how many come after it

[plates[1].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
Book II has 3,781 words, and the theft takes 78 of them. In the 1,945 words after it, just over half the book, he examines what he loved in it, with 33 question marks. Auden told his 1941 class that Augustine’s account of the conflict between self and ego is the earliest classic of self-analysis. One answer Augustine tries, five paragraphs later, is that he was a prisoner imitating freedom: he did unpunished what was forbidden, a dim likeness of God’s power. Auden says why so many gratuitous acts, Augustine’s among them, are crimes: by breaking a law a man asserts that he is free. The last paragraph asks who can untie the knot.

## Passage 3: Too late  (plates[2], id "late", from augustine/book-10)

[plates[2].heading]
Too late

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
<b>Too late</b> is one Latin word, <i>sero</i>. <b>Loved</b> is <i>amavi</i>, the verb of the theft in the passage from Book II. Augustine uses the same verb for both loves, and so does Auden’s summary of him. <b>Within</b> is <i>intus</i>, inside, and <b>abroad</b> is <i>foris</i>, out of doors. The beautiful things are <i>formosa</i>, finely formed, and he is <i>deformis</i>, misshapen, both from <i>forma</i>, shape. Pusey keeps the pair in <b>deformed</b> and <b>forms</b>. The things stay beautiful, and God made them. What is wrong is where he looks and what he is. Auden notes that the doctrine Augustine held finds nothing in matter evil in itself.

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
One short sentence names the two persons twice, in opposite order: <b>Thou</b>, <b>me</b>, then <b>I</b>, <b>Thee</b>. This crossing is called chiasmus. The word that joins each pair is the same, <b>with</b>, and the second half adds <b>not</b>. So one relation is said twice, and it holds in one direction and fails in the other. It is not a matter of place: the paragraph before says <i>Place there is none</i>. Auden, setting out the doctrine Augustine held, says the love it means is a relation that goes both ways. Here the way from Augustine to God is missing. The crossing is Augustine’s: <i>mecum eras, et tecum non eram</i>.

### Tab “Senses” (figure)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Senses

[plates[2].lenses[2].name] (the name of the figure)
enumeration

[plates[2].lenses[2].plain] (the figure in plain words)
a counting out: one thing told part by part; here, sense by sense

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The last five sentences count out the five senses, one each: hearing, sight, smell, taste, touch. Thirty paragraphs earlier Augustine asks what he loves when he loves God, and answers with the same five, known inwardly: a light, a melody, a fragrance, a food, an embrace. There sight comes first. Here hearing does, with <b>Thou calledst</b>. The earlier paragraph says how his love began: God struck his heart with his word, and without God’s mercy heaven and earth speak to deaf ears. Three paragraphs after this passage he starts on what still tempts him and takes the senses in turn again: first sexual desire, then food and drink, smells, sounds and sights.

### Tab “Verbs” (sentence)

[plates[2].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
Verbs

[plates[2].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
The marks show who acts. In the first two marked sentences every verb is God’s, six of them, each with the old ending <i>-st</i> that goes with <i>Thou</i>. Then Augustine’s verbs come in, as answers: God breathes and he draws breath, God touches and he burns. One sentence has no <i>Thou</i>: <i>I tasted, and hunger and thirst</i>. Its last two verbs are in the present tense, as <b>panted</b> is in the Latin. The hunger goes on as he writes. Two paragraphs later he asks God to act first again: <i>Give what Thou enjoinest</i>. Auden set the prayer’s Latin in <i>New Year Letter</i> (1941), with a note to Book X.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
A student in the 1941 class, Donald Pearce, wrote down part of a lecture on the ego in Augustine and Kafka. Auden called what Augustine wrote on the conflict between self and ego the earliest classic of self-analysis. Since Freud, he went on, the ego no longer looks like the single, steady thing it was for Augustine, so the account now matters less as psychology than as ethics, where he said it will always be a founding text.

[auden[0].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
the subtlest introspectionist between Aristotle and Freud

[auden[0].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), p. 134

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In his review of 1944 Auden sets out what Augustine put in place of the classical picture of man. Man is made in God’s image, and is fallen. The dividing line runs between a self-loving state of all his powers, of body and of mind, and a spirit in him that still witnesses to what his life was meant to be, able to love God as God loves him. Nothing in matter is evil in itself, and the love meant is a relation that goes both ways.

[auden[1].source]
“Augustus to Augustine,” in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), pp. 35–36

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Many gratuitous acts are crimes, Auden wrote, and he gave the reason. People want to be free and also to feel that they matter. By breaking a law a man asserts that he is free, and since it is a weighty law he still feels that he matters. In an essay printed in 1948 he says the same and gives Augustine’s act as the example. There he credits Augustine with being the first to see that man in his natural state hates nature, and that only an act done for nothing can content him.

[auden[2].source]
“Postscript: The Frivolous & The Earnest,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 430; “Squares and Oblongs,” in *Poets at Work* (1948), pp. 167–68

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
Only Auden’s words on the pear tree are about a passage shown here. What comes from his essay of 1944, a review of C. N. Cochrane’s *Christianity and Classical Culture*, sums up Augustine’s teaching as that book sets it out. It is not said of these passages.

[caveats[1]]
Auden’s lecture on *Othello* survives in notes taken by people who heard it. That lecture, “Squares and Oblongs” and *New Year Letter* were read here only in snippets of scanned copies. The 1941 lecture on Augustine and Kafka is known from a student’s notes, which do not name the *Confessions*.

[caveats[2]]
Auden’s course assigned the Loeb edition, with William Watts’s English of 1631. The English here is Pusey’s as Project Gutenberg prints it. In the third passage it reads *panted*, where Pusey’s first printing and the Latin have the present, *pant*.

[caveats[3]]
*Ad te*, glossed here as *toward you*, can also be taken as *for you*, which is how Pusey has it. Apostrophe usually names a turn made inside a speech; calling the whole book one rests on Augustine’s saying that he tells his story to his own kind.

[caveats[4]]
The counts are of Pusey’s English, not of the Latin. The words for *Thou* are every *Thou*, *Thee*, *Thy*, *Thine* and *Thyself* with a capital, and at least two of the 2,889 are not said to God.
