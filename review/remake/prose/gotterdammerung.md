## The head of the page

[title]
The end foretold

[where]
*Götterdämmerung*, the Prelude and Act 3, with the English of Margaret Armour (1911)

[about] (under “The passage”)
*Götterdämmerung* is the last of the four operas of Wagner’s *Ring of the Nibelung*; he wrote the words himself. In the Prelude the three Norns, the Fates, sing as they pass a golden rope, and the third foretells the burning of Valhalla, the hall of the gods. In Act 3 the Rhinemaidens tell Siegfried he will be killed that day unless he gives them the ring; he refuses, and they pass judgment on him. At the end Brünnhilde, at his pyre, sends Wotan’s ravens home and throws the torch.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden told the English Institute in 1940 that the people of the *Ring* behave oddly: their mistakes are ones they know in advance to be mistakes. The audience of *Oedipus Rex* knows what its hero does not; here the actors know as well. His example is Valhalla, set on fire with wood of the World Ash Tree; he asks why Wotan keeps such fuel there.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
the actors are never tired of explaining exactly what is going to happen and how awful it will be; they deny their own knowledge

[view.source]
“Mimesis and Allegory” (1940), in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 81–82

### Tab “Stave” (rhyme)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Stave

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
These lines do not rhyme. The colored letters are staves, or alliteration: one sound at the head of stressed words, the binding of the oldest German verse. Wagner wrote in 1851 that end-rhyme was a cheat and that this verse bends to the accents of speech. Auden, in 1944, called the words of the <i>Ring</i> alliterative verse full of old and embarrassing expressions; <b>sehrend</b>, wounding, is a word that written German lost early. After the first, the lines pair off: <b>br</b> and <b>h</b>, <b>s</b> and <b>gl</b>, then vowels, which count as alike. Wagner held that a stave makes one image of the words it joins: here <b>ewigen</b>, eternal, and <b>Ende</b>, end.

### Tab “Dawn” (word)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Dawn

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The opera’s title is never sung in it. Its two halves stand here: <b>Götter</b>, gods, and <b>dämmert</b>. <i>Dämmerung</i> is the half-light of morning or evening, and the title word, on record from 1772, came from confusing two Old Norse names, the fate of the gods and their darkening. With <b>auf</b>, up, the Norn’s verb means to dawn: the end of the eternal gods dawns. Armour keeps the dawn and adds <i>in darkness</i>. The scene’s second line asks, in this verb, whether day is dawning; the answer is that the light is fire. Here too the dawn comes with fire: Valhalla burning with wood of the World Ash, which is Auden’s example.

### Tab “Against” (figure)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Against

[plates[1].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
antithesis

[plates[1].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
two opposed things set side by side, often in phrases of the same build

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The Rhinemaidens sum Siegfried up four times, each in two halves: something to his credit, then what stands against it. He thinks himself wise and strong, and is bound and blind. The two middle sentences match, three words then four, both closing on <b>sie nicht</b>, them not: oaths he swore and does not heed, runes he knows and does not read. A highest good was given him, and he does not know he threw it away. Auden says the people of the <i>Ring</i> deny their own knowledge.

### Tab “Knowing” (word)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Knowing

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Words of knowing run through the verdict. Two of them, <b>weise</b>, wise, and <b>weiß</b>, knows, come from one root, which meant to see; the man who thinks himself wise is called <b>blind</b>. <b>Runen</b> are secrets, and letters of a secret script; Brünnhilde gave him hers. The verb <b>rät</b>, the same word as English <i>read</i>, means to counsel, interpret or guess. Wagner’s line says he does not read them; Armour’s <i>cannot</i> is her own. Auden says the people of the <i>Ring</i> explain exactly what is coming and deny their own knowledge. Here the same voices have just told Siegfried he will be killed today, and he keeps the ring.

### Tab “English” (rhyme)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
English

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The colored letters are staves, Wagner’s in the German and Armour’s in the English. In his second and third sentences the stave ties what Siegfried has to what he fails to do with it: <b>Eide</b>, oaths, to <b>achtet</b>, heeds, and <b>Runen</b> to <b>rät</b>. In this verse vowels count as alike. Wagner held that a stave makes one image of the words it joins. Armour keeps three of his staves on the same letter: <i>blind</i> and <i>bound</i>, <i>Runes</i> and <i>rede</i>, <i>glorious gift</i>. Two of these are the same words in both languages. The oldest English verse was bound this way too. She drops his <b>w</b>, which opens and closes the passage, and his vowels, and adds an <i>f</i> of her own.

### Tab “Now” (word)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Now

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Brünnhilde’s sentence is the third Norn’s, come true. <b>Götter Ende</b>, the gods’ end, is sung twice in the libretto, there and here, each time with <b>dämmert</b> and <b>auf</b>, dawns. The Norn’s gods were eternal, and their end was to dawn then, <i>da</i>, and forever, <i>ewig</i>. Here the gods are not called eternal, and the word is <b>nun</b>, now. The Norn said the god would throw the fire among the logs; Brünnhilde says <b>werf ich</b>, I throw. She sends Wotan’s ravens home to whisper it, <b>Raunt</b>, Waltraute’s verb in Act 1 for the wish he whispered. Auden observes that she and Siegfried make no new world: they burn with the old one.

Armour stays close, and where the two languages share a word she keeps the stave: <i>ravens</i> and <i>Rhine</i> for <b>Raben</b> and <b>Rhein</b>. <b>Raunt</b>, whisper, becomes <i>Tell</i>. ## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In the lecture of 1940 Auden tries two readings of the *Ring* as allegory and finds that each goes vague in this opera. To a historian it is the Germany of Wagner’s century: the gods are the old nobility, the dwarfs the men of money, the giants the workers, and Siegfried and Brünnhilde the pair who set the country free; yet at the end the ring only returns to the Rhine. On a psychoanalytic reading one would expect the pair to grow up and marry elsewhere, she Gunther and he Gutrune; the libretto shows those marriages as unfaithfulness that magic has worked, and fatal to both.

[auden[0].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
Siegfried and Brünnhilde do not create the new world, they go up in flames with the old

[auden[0].source]
“Mimesis and Allegory” (1940), in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 81–82

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden took Wagner for the test case of his lecture because he thought him the greatest modern artist and the most typical. The story and its people are allegory, he says, yet Wagner treats them as realistically as opera can, turning recitative, sung speech, into an unending melody that follows the speaking voice. His explanation of the characters’ illogic is the composer: suffering was what Wagner’s art could express, and they act against sense so that he can keep writing the music he did best. For expressing failure, betrayed love like Brünnhilde’s included, Auden ranks him with the greatest geniuses. The best of him, he says, is in the monologues.

[auden[1].source]
“Mimesis and Allegory” (1940), in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 80–81 and 83

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
*New Year Letter*, the long poem Auden published in 1941, says that the hero Wagner put on the stage has done all his thinking once he knows that his ruin is fated, and lives to suffer. The note to those lines makes the same charge as the lecture. No Romantic artist is greater than Wagner, it says, because none goes further in the Romantic heresy of setting the necessity of cause above the necessity of logic. The operas are masterly case histories of people in trouble and, as plots, absurd: the fire at Valhalla, fed with wood of the World Ash, is the instance here too.

[auden[2].source]
*New Year Letter* (1941), lines 1432 ff., p. 66, and the note to line 1432, pp. 143–44

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In 1944 Auden wrote notes for a college radio broadcast of *Die Walküre*, the second opera of the cycle. He says there that Wagner’s text for the *Ring*, dated by him to 1853, is in an alliterative meter full of old and embarrassing expressions.

[auden[3].source]
Program notes for a broadcast of *Die Walküre* (1944), in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), p. 503

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Alan Ansen, a friend who wrote down Auden’s conversation between 1946 and 1948, has him say that just three Wagner operas please him from beginning to end: *Die Walküre*, *Götterdämmerung* and *Tristan*. Within the *Ring* he puts this one second, after *Die Walküre*, and he names two things in it: the oath on the spear in Act 2, which he calls wonderful, and the duet of Brünnhilde and Waltraute in Act 1.

[auden[4].source]
Alan Ansen, *The Table Talk of W. H. Auden*, edited by Nicholas Jenkins (1991), p. 16

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In a BBC talk of 1955 on Tolkien, Auden sets Wagner’s ring beside Tolkien’s. The allegory of the *Ring*, he says first, is only political and psychological: Wotan is no true god, just an old feudal lord, no wiser and no better behaved than anyone else. Each ring gives its wearer rule over the world, and each can be made only by someone who gives up love. Wagner’s ring, because of Alberich’s curse, brings bad luck on every owner, yet it leaves them uncorrupted. Siegfried himself, although the woodbird has told him what it is, takes no interest in it and comes close to handing it back to the Rhinemaidens.

[auden[5].source]
“History, Feigned and Real” (BBC talk, 1955), in *Prose*, vol. III (2008), pp. 689–90

[auden[6].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Nietzsche’s *The Case of Wagner* was on the list of critical reading Auden recommended for the Michigan course, and the lecture of 1940 quotes it twice. In *The Dyer’s Hand* he says Nietzsche’s book first opened his ears to Wagner, and rates it above any other critical study of one figure he knows: an attack that never lets the reader forget how great its subject is. Elsewhere in *The Dyer’s Hand* Auden is mostly against opera sung in translation, because a listener hears syllables and not words. Wagner in English is unbearable to hear, he says, however good the translation: the new syllables do not fit the notes.

[auden[6].source]
*The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 48 and 473; Charles H. Miller, *Auden: An American Friendship* (1983), p. 27

[auden[7].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Reviewing a life of Wagner in 1969, Auden sets out the theory of Wagner’s *Opera and Drama* (1851): the words matter as much as the music, or more; nobody sings together with anybody else; no word is said twice; regular meter is to be left alone, and rhyme too. Only *Das Rheingold* and Acts 1 and 2 of *Die Walküre*, he says, keep its rules. *Götterdämmerung* has, in a phrase he quotes, poisoned drinks, an ensemble of plotters and a massed chorus, and Bernard Shaw, he says, was right that it shares a good deal with Verdi’s *Un ballo in maschera*.

[auden[7].source]
“The Greatest of the Monsters,” in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), pp. 251–52

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
The German printed here is the text as sung. The poem Wagner printed in 1872, which Armour’s English appears to follow, words the first three lines of the second passage a little differently, with *strong* before *wise*, and has another word for *once* in the first.

[caveats[1]]
Auden’s lecture of 1940, his radio notes of 1944, his talk of 1955, the verse and note of *New Year Letter*, Ansen’s *Table Talk* and Miller’s memoir were read only in snippets of scanned copies, a sentence at a time, and not in a full copy.

[caveats[2]]
The praise of the oath on the spear comes from a friend’s record of Auden’s talk, not from Auden’s own text.

[caveats[3]]
Which lines count as telling an end beforehand is a judgment. The drawing marks places where a character says that the end will come, or how; other lines only hint at it or call for it.

[caveats[4]]
A stave is a matter of the ear. In the first line of the first passage *Weltesche* can be heard to bind with *war*, and that is left unmarked.
