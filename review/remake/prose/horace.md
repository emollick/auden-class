## The head of the page

[title]
A ship, a crossing and a bee

[where]
*Odes* I.14, I.3 and IV.2

[about] (under “The passage”)
Three passages from the *Odes*, each in Horace’s Latin and in John Conington’s English verse, first published in 1863. In the first the poet calls to a ship that fresh waves are about to carry back out to sea. The second comes from a poem that begins with a prayer to the ship carrying Virgil to Attica, in Greece. The third is about writing poems: Horace addresses Iulus Antonius, Mark Antony’s son and himself a poet, first on the Greek poet Pindar and then on his own songs.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden quotes the start of the first passage and all of the second early in his book on the sea in Romantic writing, where he describes the attitude that came before. In it the sea stands for disorder, so the ship of state is a figure used only when society is in danger, and a ship belongs in port. To try to cross the sea is rash, close to hubris.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
to try to cross it betrays a rashness bordering on hubris

[view.source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), pp. 6–7 and 9

### Tab “Measure” (beat)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Measure

[plates[0].lenses[0].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
long and short syllables in a fixed order: two lines of twelve, one of seven, one of eight

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Under each syllable a dash marks a long one and a curve a short one. A long syllable takes longer to say, and counts as two short ones. Latin verse is measured by that pattern. Auden, writing with Chester Kallman, set two systems apart: Greek and Latin verse count syllables as long or short, English verse as accented or unaccented. The dots are the accents of the Latin words, and they fall on long and short syllables alike. Each twelve-syllable line breaks after its sixth syllable. In the second line a sentence stops at the break: the cry to the ship, asking what it is doing, ends one half, and the command, <i>bravely seize</i>, fills the other.

[plates[0].lenses[0].points[0].say] (a numbered remark)
This adjective means *new*. Its noun, the waves, is the first word of the next line, and the two together are the subject of the verb that stands before the break.

[plates[0].lenses[0].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)
The question to the ship, about what it is doing, runs up to the break. After the break come an adverb, *bravely*, and a command, *seize*.

[plates[0].lenses[0].points[2].say] (a numbered remark)
The harbor, the thing to be seized, is kept for the head of the third line, and the sentence ends on it.

[plates[0].lenses[0].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)

### Tab “Allegory” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Allegory

[plates[0].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)
allegory

[plates[0].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)
one thing presented in the words and another in the meaning, usually by a run of metaphors (Quintilian’s definition)

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Three words are marked: the ship spoken to (<b>navis</b>), the waves (<b>fluctus</b>) and the harbor (<b>portum</b>). None of the ode’s twenty lines names Rome. Quintilian, who taught rhetoric there, quotes the opening, as far as the harbor, as his example of allegory: ship for the state, waves and storms for civil wars, harbor for peace and concord. Cicero has a ship of the commonwealth tossing in storms of discord, and the edition printed here says the ode seems a close imitation of one the Greek poet Alcaeus wrote during the troubles of his own city, Mitylene. Auden quotes the same opening. The metaphor, he wrote, is used only when society is in danger, and a ship belongs in port.

### Tab “English” (compare)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
English

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The English is John Conington’s, and it follows the Latin line for line. His preface asks of a translator some metrical likeness to the Latin. For this stanza he follows Milton’s version of another ode in the same measure, ten syllables for each long line and six for each short one, beginning off the beat as English verse usually does, and he adds rhyme. Auden wrote, of Greek, that unrhymed verse measured by length and rhymed verse measured by accent share only that both are patterns of rhythm. Latin endings let <b>novi</b> (new) close one line and its noun open the next. Conington sets the pair side by side, leaves out the question in Horace’s second line and adds <b>luckless</b>.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[0].say] (a numbered remark)
The word for *new* ends Horace’s first line and its noun, the waves, begins his second. The endings of the two words show that they belong together. Conington sets them side by side.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[1].say] (a numbered remark)
*Bravely seize.* The Latin dictionary of Lewis and Short cites the line under this verb, among Italy, cities, a mountain and a kingdom, each taken possession of. Conington’s ship is told to hurry.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[2].say] (a numbered remark)
The harbor comes first in Horace’s third line and ends his sentence. Conington puts it inside his second line, before the word that rhymes.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[3].say] (a numbered remark)
Horace says only *ship*. The adjective in front of it is Conington’s.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[4].say] (a numbered remark)
Horace asks the ship whether it does not see its own damage. Conington makes a statement of it.

### Tab “Order” (figure)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Order

[plates[1].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
synchysis

[plates[1].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
the interlocked order: the adjective of one pair stands between the two words of another pair

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Each color joins words that belong together, as the Latin endings show. The verb, tore apart, waits a line for its object, the lands, with the Ocean named between. In the last two lines two pairs interlock: the adjective for the ships, the phrase for the waters, then the ships, the verb, the waters. Allen and Greenough’s Latin grammar calls this order a favorite of the poets. Its rule for any phrase, shown there from prose, is that the most significant word comes first. By that rule the weight is on <b>impiae</b>, impious, and <b>tangenda</b>, to be touched, with a <i>not</i> before it. Auden quotes the stanza for the older view that crossing the sea is rash, close to hubris.

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The glosses are from Lewis and Short’s Latin dictionary. The stanza opens on <b>nequicquam</b>, to no purpose. The god is <b>prudens</b>, a short form of <i>providens</i>, seeing ahead; the dictionary puts this line under <i>knowing, wise</i>. The ships are <b>impiae</b>, without reverence for gods, parents or country, and the waters are not to be touched. The edition printed here finds in the ode a lament for the presumption of mankind. ### Tab “English” (compare)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
English

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Conington’s preface says he rhymed this ode in pairs, against his rule of alternate rhymes, so that readers might compare. In his English no adjective is parted from its noun: the god and his foresight make one phrase, the verb takes its object at once, and <b>sacred</b> stands next to its noun. Auden wrote, of Greek, that a language which marks its words by their endings does not need their order to carry the sense, as English does. The ships lose their adjective, and <b>reckless</b> goes to their plunge. For the leap Conington writes <b>transgress</b>, from the Latin for stepping across, which in English means breaking a law.

[plates[1].lenses[2].ties[0].say] (a numbered remark)
Horace’s first word means *to no purpose*. Conington keeps the phrase for the end of his line, where it rhymes.

[plates[1].lenses[2].ties[1].say] (a numbered remark)

[plates[1].lenses[2].ties[2].say] (a numbered remark)
The lands, object of the verb that ends Horace’s first line. A whole line of the Latin stands between them. In Conington the object follows its verb at once.

[plates[1].lenses[2].ties[3].say] (a numbered remark)

### Tab “Contrast” (figure)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Contrast

[plates[2].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
antithesis

[plates[2].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
two opposed things set side by side, often in phrases of the same build

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Two sentences stand against each other. A strong breeze lifts the swan of Dirce, the Greek poet Pindar, whenever he makes for the high tracts of cloud. Nearly in Horace’s order: <b>Ego</b>, I, in the way of a bee that gathers thyme through labor, around the grove and banks of wet Tibur (now Tivoli), small, shape laborious songs. The swan is lifted and the bee works. <b>Multa</b>, much, is said of the breeze, and <b>parvus</b>, small, of the speaker. The edition printed here reads the ode as declining a poem in Pindar’s manner. Auden’s poem “The Horatians,” as Edward Mendelson quotes its close, also sets its speakers beside Pindar: they count themselves small makers, however polished.

### Tab “Measure” (beat)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Measure

[plates[2].lenses[1].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
the Sapphic stanza: long and short syllables in a fixed order, three lines of eleven and one of five

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Dashes mark long syllables, curves short ones, dots the accents of words. This is the Sapphic stanza, named for Sappho of Lesbos: three lines of eleven syllables and one of five. Each long line here has a word-end after its fifth syllable. In the third line the swan’s sentence stops at that break; the next word is <b>ego</b>, I, whose last syllable runs into the next word. The new sentence crosses the short line and the stanza end, and keeps its verb, <b>fingo</b>, I shape, for the last word of the next stanza. Earlier the ode says that in his dithyrambs, songs for Bacchus, Pindar is carried along in measures freed from law. It keeps this one stanza fifteen times.

[plates[2].lenses[1].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)

### Tab “English” (compare)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
English

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Conington’s preface gives his stand-in for the Sapphic stanza: three lines of eight syllables and a fourth of four, to give the effect of the short last line, with alternate rhymes. He counts twenty-eight English syllables against thirty-eight Latin ones, and says he was constantly obliged to leave out part of the Latin. Here the high tracts of the clouds become <b>skies</b>, the thyme is only <b>sweets</b>, and the grove and the wetness of Tibur are gone. He keeps the turn in the middle of the third line, the full stop and then <b>I</b>. Horace’s sentence ends on its verb, <b>fingo</b>, I shape. Conington moves the verb up a line.

[plates[2].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
The <i>Odes</i> are 103 poems in thirteen measures. Thirty-seven are in the Alcaic stanza and twenty-five in the Sapphic: sixty-two of the 103 in two stanzas named for Alcaeus and Sappho, Greek poets of Lesbos. Thirty-four are in the five stanzas called Asclepiadic, the odes of the first two passages here among them. Seven odes share the other six measures. In the last ode of his third book Horace says what he will be known for: that he first brought Aeolian song over to Italian measures. The edition printed here takes Aeolian song to mean Alcaeus and Sappho, his chief models. Edward Mendelson writes that Auden shaped Horatian stanzas in English by counting syllables, as in his elegy for Freud.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In *The Enchafèd Flood* Auden also says what the older writers put in the ship’s place. A society in its ordinary state is pictured as a City or a Garden, the places where people wish to be and should be. A voyage is then an evil that has to be accepted, a crossing of what divides. Odysseus and Jason do not sail for the sake of sailing: one wants only to get home, and the other is after the Golden Fleece, in a far country. A ship of state that takes to the high seas by choice stands for a state in disorder: he calls it the Ship of Fools.

[auden[0].source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), pp. 7–8

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Against this older view he sets what he calls the new notes of the Romantic attitude. He lists four. The first is that any man of feeling and honor wants to leave both land and city. The second is that the sea is where man really is, and that to be on a voyage is his true state. The book is about that later attitude. It quotes Horace twice, both times for the earlier one, which Auden for convenience calls the classic attitude.

[auden[1].source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), pp. 10 and 12–13

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Donald Pearce, who took the class in 1941, recalls going to see Auden about it that September, before the term began. Auden named some of the writers the course would take up, and Horace was the first name he gave.

[auden[2].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), p. 133

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Writing with Chester Kallman about translating opera, Auden sets the two ways of measuring verse side by side. In verse measured by quantity, as Greek and Latin verse is, each syllable counts as long or as short, with a long counted as equal to two shorts. In verse measured by accent, as English and German verse is, the length of syllables is left out of account, and what tells them apart is whether they are accented. Music, the two authors say, measures in both ways at once.

[auden[3].source]
“Translating Opera Libretti” (written with Chester Kallman), in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 485

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Of Greek, not Latin, Auden wrote that poetry always loses a great deal when it is moved from one language to another, and that between two languages as distant as Greek and English one can nearly say that a version which succeeds as an English poem is by so much less like the Greek one. Unrhymed verse measured by length and rhymed verse measured by accent share only that both are patterns of rhythm. Greek, he adds, marks its words by their endings, so that its sense does not hang on where a word stands, as the sense of an English sentence does.

[auden[4].source]
“The Greeks and Us,” in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), pp. 5–6

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Edward Mendelson’s biography records that Auden took Marianne Moore’s way of counting syllables and shaped it into Horatian stanzas, first in the poem “Where do They come from?” and then in his elegy for Freud. It also records what Auden said to James Michie, a translator of Horace whom he admired, about his “Thanksgiving for a Habitat”: he had set out on purpose to write the poem Horace might write as a living poet whose first language was English. Of “River Profile,” a poem of 1966, Mendelson says that it is written in twelve Sapphic stanzas.

[auden[5].source]
Edward Mendelson, *Early Auden, Later Auden: A Critical Biography* (2017), pp. 416, 747 and 773

[auden[6].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In the spring of 1968 Auden wrote “The Horatians,” a poem about people of Horace’s kind, among them, by Edward Mendelson’s account, country clergy, curators and writers of short poems. It ends with the creed they have learned from Horace: set beside Pindar as makers, and beside real martyrs such as Regulus as lives, they rank themselves low; all they can do is the work they are fitted for, taking a glad and sober view of the world. Mendelson calls this creed quietly self-satisfied, and says its speakers are wrong about Regulus in a way clear to Auden.

[auden[6].source]
“The Horatians” (written 1968; in *City Without Walls*, 1969), as described and quoted in Edward Mendelson, *Early Auden, Later Auden: A Critical Biography* (2017), pp. 778–79

[auden[7].says] (a paraphrase of him)
At the end of an essay on Pope, Auden wrote that poetry is no escape from life, though it gives a short rest from the troubles of the moment. He adds that with age, and in darker and harder times, Horace and Pope are the kind of poet he goes back to more and more often for that rest.

[auden[7].source]
“A Civilized Voice,” in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), p. 124

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
The dots mark the accent each Latin word has in speech, by the rule in Allen and Greenough’s grammar; words of one syllable are marked as one reader would say them. That grammar also teaches a beat of the voice within each foot, which is not drawn here.

[caveats[1]]
Auden’s remarks on verse measured by length, on what translation loses and on languages that mark words by their endings were made of Greek, or of Greek and Latin together. He did not make them about these lines.

[caveats[2]]
What is said here of Auden’s poems “The Horatians” and “River Profile,” of his elegy for Freud and of his remark to James Michie comes from Edward Mendelson’s biography; the poems were not read whole. Mendelson calls the creed that closes “The Horatians” quietly self-satisfied, its speakers wrong about Regulus.

[caveats[3]]
In the third passage *plurimum*, very much, is left unmarked. The edition printed here takes it with the word for labor; the translator of the Loeb edition takes it with the grove, and puts the grove and the banks with the bee’s gathering, where Conington puts them with the speaker.

[caveats[4]]
The Latin here is a school edition of 1895, the English a translation of 1863 as printed in 1865. Auden’s reading list names the Loeb edition, and his quotations follow its Latin: in line two of the first passage it has an exclamation mark, not the question mark printed here.
