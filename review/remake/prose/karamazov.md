## The head of the page

[title]
The Grand Inquisitor

[where]
*The Brothers Karamazov*, Part II, Book V, Chapter 5

[about] (under “The passage”)
Ivan Karamazov, twenty-three, sits in a tavern with his brother Alyosha, twenty and a novice in a monastery. Alyosha has just said that there is a Being who can forgive everything, and Ivan tells him a story he made up about a year ago and calls a poem. In Seville in the sixteenth century Christ comes back for a moment, and the Grand Inquisitor, a cardinal of almost ninety, has him arrested and comes alone at night to the prison. The English is Constance Garnett’s, of 1912.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden wrote that the evidence for Satan’s argument, when he holds out the world’s kingdoms to Jesus, is perhaps nowhere put so fully and forcefully as by the Grand Inquisitor. Jesus gives him no answer, as he gave none to Satan or to Pilate, because none is needed. Such men are answered by their own experience. They know they have failed, and that knowledge, with the hatred they have stirred up, makes them cry out.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
their own experience answers for them and they know very well what they do, and that they have failed

[view.source]
*The Prolific and the Devourer* (written 1939; Ecco Press, 1994), p. 51

### Tab “Questions” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Questions

[plates[0].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
hypophora

[plates[0].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
a speaker asks a question and at once answers it himself

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The old man asks four questions here. The first, asked twice, gets no reply, and he forbids one: <b>Don’t answer, be silent</b>. After each of the others he goes straight on with a statement of his own. To ask and answer oneself is an old figure of argument, hypophora; one form of it asks an opponent what he can say: <b>What canst Thou say, indeed?</b> Of a later question Ivan tells Alyosha that the old man asks it and <i>answers the question for Him</i>. The old man’s reason for the silence is that the Prisoner has <b>no right to add anything</b>. Auden’s is that Jesus does not need to answer.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The old man says <b>Thou</b> twelve times here and <b>Thee</b> twice. The Russian has its ordinary familiar pronoun, which the brothers also use. Garnett gives the brothers <i>you</i> and keeps <i>thou</i>, long since a word of church and literary English, for what is said to Christ and God: 220 times in the chapter, and for no one else. So in her English the threat of burning comes in the pronoun of the chapter’s prayers. The threat names the Prisoner among the <b>heretics</b>, a word from the Greek for one able to choose. Later in the speech the old man complains that the Prisoner left men <i>the fearful burden of free choice</i>.

### Tab “Sentences” (sentence)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Sentences

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
These hundred words are eleven sentences: five questions, two commands and four statements, the last of them the secret. In the rest of the old man’s long argument a sentence averages 23 words; here, nine. The first two questions ask why, and no answer follows either. The first command is <b>Be angry</b>. The later questions he answers himself, and one answer is that whatever he has to tell <b>is known to Thee already</b>. Then he tells the secret, which nobody has asked for. The short questions are Dostoevsky’s; the two commands are sentences of their own only in Garnett’s English. Auden quotes the opening of these lines as the cry of men who know they have failed.

### Tab “Answer” (sentence)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Answer

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The old man wants words: he <b>longed for Him to say something</b>. Auden writes that Jesus gives him no answer, having no need to. What comes is a kiss, told in one sentence of eighteen words, and then five words that name it: <b>That was all His answer</b>. The Prisoner’s verbs are <b>approached</b>, <b>kissed</b> and <b>went away</b>, none of them a verb of speech. The old man, after 4,977 words, says eleven more and lets out the man he was to burn. The sentences are as short in the Russian. Asked what becomes of the old man, Ivan says: <b>The kiss glows in his heart</b>, and his idea stays as it was.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden’s account of the temptations in the wilderness stands under a sentence from this chapter, signed Dostoyevsky: the old man’s remark that the three questions gather up the whole of mankind’s later history. Auden reads them as the temptations of childhood, of adolescence and of maturity. In the third, he says, Satan appeals to reason: truth is tested by success or failure, people love only themselves, and so the true way lies in being stronger than one’s neighbor. By claiming that he can give the kingdoms, Auden adds, Satan claims to be God.

[auden[0].source]
*The Prolific and the Devourer* (written 1939; Ecco Press, 1994), pp. 48–51

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden then writes the answer Jesus might have made if he had been listening to an attendant who only repeated his master’s words, and not to the Inquisitor. It takes up three of the old man’s sentences in turn: that men find nothing harder to bear than freedom, that the wish to worship in common is their chief misery, and that the Church has corrected Christ’s work and rested it on miracle, mystery and authority. The freedom Jesus holds out, the answer says, is freedom to know what is true, and each person must find that by his own faith and effort.

[auden[1].source]
*The Prolific and the Devourer* (written 1939; Ecco Press, 1994), pp. 51–58

In that answer Jesus says that the Inquisitor, and not he, has turned the Last Supper into a mystery. Later in the book Auden states what history must show if Jesus was right: a general movement toward unity and equality, which the way of love has always helped, and which the way of hatred and force has always hindered and has always failed in the end to prevent. If history does not show it, he writes, then Jesus was mistaken and the right lay with Satan and the Inquisitor.

[auden[2].source]
*The Prolific and the Devourer* (written 1939; Ecco Press, 1994), pp. 54 and 62

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Charles Miller, a student in the class who also shared Auden’s house, gives Auden’s words at the first meeting, in early October 1941, as he reconstructs them. Auden named *The Brothers Karamazov* among the great books the students would be examined on, with the *Divine Comedy*, Augustine’s *Confessions* and four plays of Shakespeare. No record has been found of what he said in class about the novel.

[auden[3].source]
Charles H. Miller, *Auden: An American Friendship* (1983), pp. 26–27

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
Auden’s view is from *The Prolific and the Devourer*, written in 1939 and not published by him, which was read here only in search snippets of a scanned copy. No mention of the kiss was found in it, so the third passage cites him only for the Prisoner’s silence.

[caveats[1]]
Nothing Auden said about the novel in the 1941 class is on record. That he named it at the first meeting comes from a student’s memoir written about forty years later.

[caveats[2]]
The English is Constance Garnett’s. She sometimes cuts Dostoevsky’s sentences shorter, tells the kiss in the past tense where he uses the present, and does not always keep his repeated words (*aged* for ninety-year-old, *glows* for burns).

[caveats[3]]
The counts follow the text printed here. A row of dots is not counted as a word, two words joined by a dash count as one, the quotation marks decide who speaks each word, and a sentence ends at a period, question mark or exclamation mark before a capital.

[caveats[4]]
The figure in the first passage has several names. The handbook used here lists it under anthypophora and gives hypophora, the name Peacham used for it in 1577, as another.
