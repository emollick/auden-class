## The head of the page

[title]
Ariel’s song

[where]
*The Tempest*, Act 1, Scene 2, with Act 3, Scene 2 and the Epilogue

[about] (under “The passage”)
Ferdinand, a young prince, has come ashore alone from a shipwreck and believes his father, the king, has drowned. Ariel, a spirit he cannot see, sings this to him. His father is alive, on another part of the island, and Ariel knows it. The second passage is spoken by Caliban, a slave, when Ariel’s unseen music frightens the butler and the jester he is leading to kill his master, Prospero, the magician who rules the island. The third is Prospero’s own, spoken to the audience when the play is over.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden wrote that this song is a dirge in form only. The feelings of a mourning son are no part of it. It is a spell, and what it does is change Ferdinand’s attitude to his grief, from rebellion to awe and acceptance. The loss is not made less. The song lets him accept that his father belongs to the past, and then his future is in front of him: Miranda.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
It is a magic spell, the effect of which is, not to lessen his feeling of loss, but to change his attitude towards his grief

[view.source]
“Music in Shakespeare,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 525–26

## Passage 1  (plates[0], id "song", from tempest/a1s2)

### Tab “Beat” (beat)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Beat

[plates[0].lenses[0].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
seven syllables and four beats, the line beginning on a beat

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
From its second line to its seventh the song keeps one measure: seven syllables, four beats, the line beginning on a beat. Oberon and Puck say their charms in this measure in <i>A Midsummer Night’s Dream</i>, and so do the witches in <i>Macbeth</i>. Auden called this song a magic spell, and this is the measure of those spells. The rule under each line is the measure and the dots are the speaking voice. They part most in the fourth and fifth lines, which say what is happening to the body. At <b>sea-change</b> two stresses fall together, which slows the voice on a word that was new. In the next line, <b>Into something rich and strange</b>, voice and measure agree again.

[plates[0].lenses[0].points[0].say] (a numbered remark)
The first line has eight syllables and begins off the beat. Three of its first four syllables are stressed.

[plates[0].lenses[0].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)
The last line has eight syllables too. It ends on three stresses in a row. The first two are the sound of the bell: *ding-dong* is a word made from it.

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The first four lines rhyme by turns: <i>lies</i> with <i>eyes</i>, <i>made</i> with <i>fade</i>. Each rhyme waits a line for its partner. From <b>sea-change</b> on the song rhymes in pairs, and <b>strange</b> answers <b>change</b> at once. A rhyme that comes back at once makes its two lines one unit, a couplet. This one holds the words Auden gave his class in 1941 as the central theme of the sea scenes in <i>Pericles</i> and <i>The Tempest</i>. Sounds come back inside the lines as well: <b>f</b> four times in the first line, and <b>s</b> four times across the couplet.

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[0].lenses[2].name] (the name of the figure)
chiasmus

[plates[0].lenses[2].plain] (the figure in plain words)
a crossing: two things are named, then named again in the opposite order

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Two lines say one thing in opposite order. The first goes from the body to the sea: <b>bones</b>, then <b>coral</b>. The second goes from the sea to the body: <b>pearls</b>, then <b>eyes</b>. Neither line says <i>like</i>. The verbs are plain ones, <i>are</i> and <i>were</i>: coral is made of his bones, and what are pearls now were his eyes. Auden told his class that the theme named in this song is transformation, almost transmutation in the alchemist’s sense, one substance changed into another. These two lines state such a change as fact, twice.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[0].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[0].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
The Oxford dictionary’s earliest example of <b>sea-change</b> is this song, and Shakespeare appears to have made the word for this line. <b>Suffer</b> here means <i>undergo</i>, and <b>fade</b> means <i>perish</i>. So the sentence says that every part of him that could perish is changed by the sea instead. <b>Strange</b> could mean <i>foreign</i> or <i>new</i> as well as <i>wonderful</i>. <b>Fathom</b> is six feet, at first the length of two outstretched arms. <b>Knell</b> is the sound of a bell rung at a funeral. The words are those of a dirge, and Auden’s point is that the song is a dirge in form only.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
<b>Noises</b> is Caliban’s first word for what Ariel has just played, unseen, on a pipe and a tabor, a small drum. The lexicon of Shakespeare’s words puts this line under the sense music; a band of musicians was called a noise. Then come <i>sounds</i> and <b>sweet airs</b>: an air is a piece of music, played or sung. He speaks of instruments and voices and of no player, and he never says Ariel’s name in the play. In Act 4 Ariel reports that with his tabor he charmed the ears of Caliban and his two companions. Auden wrote that without Prospero Ariel can be known to Caliban only as these sounds and sweet airs.

### Tab “Sentence” (sentence)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Sentence

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The last seven lines are one sentence. <i>And sometimes</i>, <i>and then</i> and <i>that, when</i> join waking to sleep, sleep to the dream, and the dream to waking. The lexicon of Shakespeare’s words puts <b>will hum</b> and <b>would open</b> under use and custom, what happens again and again, and <i>will make</i> is the same will. The sentence’s other verbs are past: <i>methought</i>, <i>cried</i>. No verb in this speech is a future, though earlier in the scene Caliban plots in the future tense. Auden took Caliban to stand for the natural, which keeps to what is necessary and cannot imagine possibility; a vague dream, he wrote, is the nearest it comes. Stephano answers in the future: <b>will prove</b>, <b>shall have</b>.

### Tab “Beat” (beat)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Beat

[plates[1].lenses[2].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
ten syllables and five beats, the line beginning off the beat, without rhyme

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Five of the nine lines run a syllable past the last beat: <b>noises</b>, <b>hurt not</b>, <b>voices</b>, <b>dreaming</b>, <b>riches</b>. A handbook of verse counts such extra syllables among the freedoms of Shakespeare’s late verse, and says the placing of a pause can fit verse to meaning. The long sentence pauses three times after a third beat, at <i>ears</i>, at <i>sleep again</i> and at <i>upon me</i>, and each time goes on in the same line. So sleep, the dream and the waking each begin in mid-line.

As this text prints it, the scene has 66 speeches. Stephano, a butler, and Trinculo, a jester, speak 42, all in prose; two lines are sung. Here the two servants keep to prose, and the slave, whom they call <i>monster</i> seventeen times, speaks verse, as Prospero, Miranda and Ferdinand do in every speech they have. ### Tab “Pardon” (compare)

[plates[2].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
Pardon

[plates[2].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
Set against the last two lines are Prospero’s words to Caliban near the end of Act 5. Auden found in these words Prospero’s attitude to all the enemies he pardons, and wrote that the thought that he too may have done wrong and need forgiving never comes to him. The two sentences are built alike: <i>as you</i> hope for pardon, then what the hearer is to do. To Caliban the pardon is <i>my pardon</i>. In the Epilogue the hearers are the ones who <b>would pardon’d be</b>, for their own <b>crimes</b>, and Prospero asks their <b>indulgence</b> for himself.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[0].say] (a numbered remark)
Both begin with *As you*: what the hearer hopes for comes first.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[1].say] (a numbered remark)
To Caliban the pardon is Prospero’s to give. In the Epilogue it is the pardon his hearers hope for themselves.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[2].say] (a numbered remark)
Then what the hearer is to do. Caliban is to make Prospero’s cell neat. The audience is asked to set Prospero free.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Donald Pearce, who took the class, wrote down what he could after the lectures. According to his notes, Auden said in December 1941 that the sea in Shakespeare is always where new life comes from, where the old is laid down and treasure is found. What this song says is the central theme of the sea scenes in *Pericles* and *The Tempest*. The change comes close to transmutation, as an alchemist would mean the word, and it works on people too, on their minds and their lives, not only on things.

[auden[0].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
What Shakespeare is dealing with here is transformation

[auden[0].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), pp. 134 and 148–49

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Of Ariel’s voice Auden wrote that it should be as little personal and as much like an instrument as a voice can be, because Ariel has no human feelings to express. The songs sung to the Duke in *Twelfth Night* and to Mariana in *Measure for Measure*, he wrote, do not change their hearers, and only settle each more firmly in a mood already there. The effect of Ariel’s two songs on Ferdinand is more like that of the instrumental music a physician orders for the unconscious Thaisa in *Pericles*.

[auden[1].source]
“Music in Shakespeare,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 508 and 525

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In Pearce’s notes of the December lecture Auden called the play a fairy tale: a hero who has erred comes home from a land of marvels to rule again. Prospero’s error was to neglect his dukedom out of an egotistical wish to perfect himself in secret arts. The theme is an old one, the giving up of more than ordinary powers for the blessings of ordinary life, as Faust gives them up. What Prospero learns on the island is the art of life, though he learns magic too. Caliban is what is beastly in human nature: it has to be held in its place and cannot be removed or ignored.

[auden[2].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), pp. 150–51

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In *The Dyer’s Hand* Auden takes Caliban to stand for the natural and Ariel for the spirit of imagination, ideally an unseen voice. Shakespeare, he says, has written the part so that Caliban, brutal and corrupt as he is, has the better of his argument with Prospero, who is largely to blame for his corruption. Prospero and Ariel are bound by a contract, and Ariel is released when the play ends.

[auden[3].source]
“Balaam and His Ass,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 129–34

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden called the play a disquieting work. The guilty repent and the injured pardon, he wrote, more in form than in fact, and only Alonso seems truly sorry. Prospero is to be admired for his gifts and for his strength, and cannot be liked. In “Music in Shakespeare” Auden adds that the play ends more sourly than the three comedies before it, and that the victory of justice seems to Prospero a duty more than a joy.

[auden[4].source]
“Balaam and His Ass” and “Music in Shakespeare,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 128–29 and 526–27

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden judged the play manichean, because it blames nature for the conflict of nature and spirit and makes spirit innocent. He set *The Magic Flute* against it, as too optimistic where this play is too pessimistic. In the opera Papageno, who stands for the natural, gets his Papagena as a reward, and their duet shows, humbly and innocently, the attitude that Caliban shows, guilty and defiant, when Prospero charges him with trying to rape Miranda.

[auden[5].source]
“Balaam and His Ass,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 130 and 134

[auden[6].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In “Music in Shakespeare” Auden says that Shakespeare uses instrumental music in two ways: on social occasions, and, unexpectedly, as an image in sound of a world of magic or of the supernatural. Spirits such as Oberon and Ariel may command such music, and so may a wise man such as Prospero, to work magically on human beings. Solemn music mostly comes from off stage, where no one sees who plays it, so that those on stage cannot choose how to answer it: either they do not hear it or it does to them what they cannot control.

[auden[6].source]
“Music in Shakespeare,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 507–8

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
What Auden said in class in 1941 is known from notes that a student, Donald Pearce, wrote down after the lectures. It is not Auden’s own text.

[caveats[1]]
The stresses marked are one reader’s hearing of the lines. Another reader could fairly mark a few syllables otherwise.

[caveats[2]]
Editions do not print Act 3, Scene 2 alike. The counts of verse and prose follow the text printed here. The Folio of 1623 sets two of Caliban’s first five speeches as verse, and sets several speeches of Stephano and Trinculo, the reply shown here among them, as short lines.

[caveats[3]]
The two full stops inside Caliban’s speech are an editor’s. The Folio has a comma and a colon there, and no full stop before the end of the speech.

[caveats[4]]
The third passage leaves out lines 11 to 18 of the Epilogue. In those lines a third sentence ends with its couplet.
