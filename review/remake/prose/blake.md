## The head of the page

[title]
The voice of the Devil

[where]
The section of that name, the first “Memorable Fancy” and “Proverbs of Hell”

[about] (under “The passage”)
William Blake wrote this short book in prose and verse between 1790 and 1793 and printed it from copper that he had etched with acid. Under the heading “The Voice of the Devil” it sets three errors, blamed on all Bibles or sacred codes, against three contraries. Then the speaker tells how he walked among the fires of Hell and collected some of its proverbs. Coming home, he sees a mighty Devil write one sentence with corroding fires, and after that sentence he gives the proverbs.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden wrote that a law of God imposed like men’s laws, with an eternal penalty, would imply two Gods: a Creator within the world, a Judge beyond it. He called the *Marriage* a great polemic, an argument against a doctrine, in liberal theology’s rightful reaction against that dualism. Blake detested the Judge, he wrote later, and tried to turn the dualism upside down: for Blake, God is and acts only in existing beings or men.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
not to overcome the dualism, but to stand it on its head

[view.source]
“Red Lizards and White Stallions,” *The Saturday Review of Literature*, 13 April 1946, p. 22; *The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), pp. 49–50

## Passage 1: The errors and their contraries  (plates[0], id "creed", from blake/marriage)

[plates[0].heading]
The errors and their contraries

[plates[0].label] (names the first of two versions)
The errors

[plates[0].pair.label] (names the second version)
The contraries

### Tab “Answer” (compare)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Answer

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Each error is set against its contrary, and the same number in the same color marks the words that answer each other. The contraries keep the errors’ nouns <b>Body</b>, <b>Soul</b>, <b>Energy</b> and <b>Reason</b>, and <i>Eternity</i> returns as <b>Eternal</b>. <b>Evil</b>, <b>Good</b> and <b>God</b> do not come back. The third error has God <b>torment</b> man in Eternity. Auden called it a misunderstanding of the doctrine of Hell to think of God’s law as imposed like men’s laws, with an eternal penalty.

[plates[0].lenses[0].ties[0].say] (a numbered remark)
The errors are given a cause, all Bibles or sacred codes. The contraries are given none. They are called *true*.

[plates[0].lenses[0].ties[1].say] (a numbered remark)
*Two* is answered by *no*: man has no Body that is distinct from his Soul.

[plates[0].lenses[0].ties[2].say] (a numbered remark)
Both nouns are kept. Body is no longer a second thing beside Soul. It is a portion of Soul, the one that the five senses discern.

[plates[0].lenses[0].ties[3].say] (a numbered remark)
Kept: Energy is from the Body. Dropped: the name *Evil* and the adverb *alone*. Added: the adjective *only*, with a larger claim. *Energy is the only life*.

[plates[0].lenses[0].ties[4].say] (a numbered remark)
Reason loses its name, *Good*, and its source, the Soul. It is given a place: the limit and outer edge of Energy.

[plates[0].lenses[0].ties[5].say] (a numbered remark)
*God*, *man* and the future tense are gone. *Eternity* comes back as the adjective *Eternal*. Where the error had *torment*, the contrary has *Delight*.

[plates[0].lenses[0].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage, and the lines themselves. Auden on the law and the eternal penalty: “Red Lizards and White Stallions,” *The Saturday Review of Literature*, 13 April 1946, p. 22.

### Tab “Grammar” (sentence)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Grammar

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Each error begins with <b>That</b>. None is a sentence of its own: each is a clause hung on the word <b>errors</b>, the way reported speech hangs on <i>he said that</i>. The errors are reported and nowhere asserted. Each contrary begins with its subject, and its verbs are present: <i>has</i> once, <i>is</i> five times. The one future tense in the passage is <b>will torment</b>, and its answer is the shortest sentence here, four words. Auden ranked the book among the great polemics. A polemic is an argument in a controversy. Here the errors blamed on the sacred codes stand in dependent clauses, and the reply in plain statements.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
<b>Energy</b> was not yet a word of physics: that sense is first recorded in 1802. Johnson’s dictionary of 1755 gives force and vigor and, of speech, spirit and life. The second contrary calls it <i>the only life</i>. Locke used <b>inlets</b> for the senses, the passages by which ideas enter. Auden quotes a statement of Blake’s in which unbelief means Bacon, Newton and Locke. The first contrary limits the word twice: the <i>chief</i> inlets, <i>in this age</i>. <b>Bound</b>, as <i>bounds</i>, and <b>circumference</b> are words Milton’s Son of God, the Messiah, speaks as he marks out the world with compasses. Soon the book says that in <i>Paradise Lost</i> Reason is called Messiah.

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[0].gloss] (set under the word)

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[0].note] (a note on the word)

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[1].gloss] (set under the word)

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[7].note] (a note on the word)

## Passage 2: The Devil’s sentence  (plates[1], id "sentence", from blake/marriage)

[plates[1].heading]
The Devil’s sentence

### Tab “Beat” (beat)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Beat

[plates[1].lenses[0].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
fourteen syllables and seven beats, the line beginning off the beat

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Each line has fourteen syllables and seven beats, the old long line that broke after its eighth syllable into the ballad stanza. The first line is nearly two lines of a ballad by Thomas Chatterton, with a <b>bird</b> for the ballad’s dart, and after two syllables voice and measure agree. In the second they part. <b>World</b> takes a stress straight after <b>immense</b>. Two stresses together slow a line, and here it slows on what every bird is. After the comma <b>closed</b> takes a stress where the measure has no beat. A handbook of verse counts such a syllable as a foot by itself, one unit of the measure, set off by the pause.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[0].say] (a numbered remark)
These two syllables can be heard two ways: with the stress on *How*, as marked, or on *do*, which is the measure itself. From the third syllable on, voice and measure agree to the end of the line.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)
The eighth syllable. The old long line paused here, and the ballad stanza breaks it here into eight syllables and six. In the second line the comma falls at the same place.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[2].say] (a numbered remark)
Two stresses side by side. The beats on either side of them fall on *an* and *of*, which the voice passes over.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[3].say] (a numbered remark)
A stress where the measure has no beat. This is commonly called an inversion, and a handbook of verse notes that it nearly always stands next to a pause.

[plates[1].lenses[0].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage. The line of fourteen syllables and the ballad stanza: Paull F. Baum, *The Principles of English Versification*, chapter 4, and Samuel Johnson’s *Life of Dryden*. The ballad: Thomas Chatterton, *Bristowe Tragedie*, lines 133–136; a reviewer in the *London Review of Books*, 20 September 2001, hears its phrases in the *Marriage*. Stresses that fall together, and a stress after a pause: Baum, chapter 5.

### Tab “Question” (sentence)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Question

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The Devil writes one sentence, a question. Its main clause is the first four words, and they are about <b>you</b>: the prose says men read it on earth. <b>But</b> after <i>know</i> has the sense Johnson’s dictionary gives as <i>otherwise than that</i>: the question is how you know that it is not so. The claim that every bird is a world is nowhere asserted. It hangs on a question about what the reader knows. Its subject fills out the first line and its verb, <b>Is</b>, opens the second, so the sense runs on where the measure pauses. The sentence begins with your knowing and ends on <b>your senses five</b>.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
<b>Immense</b> is from the Latin for <i>not measured</i>; Johnson gives unlimited, unbounded, infinite. <b>Closed</b> is the last verb of the ballad stanza that the first line resembles, said there of eyes shut by death. Later the book’s prose says that if the <i>doors of perception</i> were cleansed everything would appear infinite, and that man <i>has closed himself up</i>. The prose says <i>the five senses</i> three times; the verse turns the order and ends on <b>five</b>. <b>Delight</b> is what the third contrary says Energy is. Auden’s verse on Blake has him hear something holy singing within every thing that dies.

## Passage 3: Six Proverbs of Hell  (plates[2], id "proverbs", from blake/marriage)

### Tab “Frame” (figure)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Frame

[plates[2].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
parallelism

[plates[2].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
sentences of the same build, set side by side

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Four lines keep one frame: the X of a creature is the Y of God. The Book of Proverbs uses the same frame. In the first place stand <b>pride</b>, <b>lust</b> and <b>wrath</b>, three of the seven deadly sins, and <b>nakedness</b>, for which Adam in Genesis is afraid and hides. In the last place stands <b>God</b>. The verb between is <b>is</b>, with no <i>like</i> or <i>as</i>, and no line gives a reason. An aphorist, says a foreword Auden signed, states his point and does not argue for it. Later the book says that God acts and is only <i>in existing beings or men</i>, and Auden gives that as Blake’s position.

### Tab “Yoke” (sentence)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Yoke

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The line has two halves and one verb. <b>Are built</b> is said once, of <b>prisons</b>, and the second half leaves it out. Rhetoric calls this a yoking, in Greek <i>zeugma</i>: one verb governs two clauses. The second half borrows the verb, so its nouns pair with the first’s: <b>brothels</b> with prisons, <b>bricks</b> with <b>stones</b>, <b>religion</b> with <b>law</b>. The Law of Moses was written on stone, and the book later speaks of <i>the stony law</i>. Auden wrote that Blake detested a God who is a Judge, laying down the moral law and punishing. In this line law is what prisons are built with, and the one verb sets religion beside it.

[plates[2].lenses[1].spans[1].label] (in the key)

[plates[2].lenses[1].spans[3].label] (in the key)

[plates[2].lenses[1].spans[5].label] (in the key)
with what

[plates[2].lenses[1].spans[7].label] (in the key)
of what

[plates[2].lenses[1].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage, and the line itself. Zeugma and prozeugma as *Silva Rhetoricae* defines them. The tables of stone: Exodus 24:12 and 31:18, in the King James Bible. *The stony law*: near the end of the book, in [“A Song of Liberty”](#/read/blake/marriage:117). Auden on the Judge: *The Enchafèd Flood*, p. 49.

### Tab “Cross” (figure)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Cross

[plates[2].lenses[2].name] (the name of the figure)
chiasmus

[plates[2].lenses[2].plain] (the figure in plain words)
a crossing: two things are named, then named again in the opposite order

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The two halves are built alike: <b>excess</b>, a feeling, a verb. The ideas run the other way. Grief comes first and last, in <b>sorrow</b> and <b>weeps</b>; mirth stands in the middle, in <b>laughs</b> and <b>joy</b>. Rhetoric calls ideas that return in the opposite order a chiasmus, a crossing. Here the crossing gives each feeling the other’s verb. The noun the halves share is <b>excess</b>, from the Latin for going beyond, and it is the subject of both verbs. An earlier proverb sets wisdom’s palace at the end of <i>the road of excess</i>. A later one takes the expected verbs away: joys do not laugh and sorrows do not weep.

[plates[2].lenses[2].parts[1].label] (in the key)

[plates[2].lenses[2].parts[4].label] (in the key)

[plates[2].lenses[2].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage. Chiasmus as *Silva Rhetoricae* defines it: ideas repeated in inverted order. *Excess*: the Online Etymology Dictionary. Two other proverbs in [the same list](#/read/blake/marriage:34): the third, on the road of excess, and the fifty-ninth, on joys and sorrows.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[2].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[2].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
<b>Pride</b> is the first of the deadly sins, and in heraldry a peacock <i>in his pride</i> is one with its tail spread. <b>Lust</b> meant desire and pleasure in Old English; its sinful sense came from Bible translations, and Johnson’s dictionary names it as a quality of the goat. <b>Wrath</b> in the Bible is God’s as well as man’s, and Proverbs likens a king’s wrath to a lion’s roar. Each of the three sins stands with an animal already tied to it. <b>Nakedness</b> is the word of the prohibitions in Leviticus. Three of the four endings are Bible phrases: the glory, the wisdom and the work of God.

[plates[2].lenses[3].notes[0].gloss] (set under the word)
in Exodus, tables of stone

[plates[2].lenses[3].notes[0].note] (a note on the word)
In Exodus the Law is given to Moses on tables of stone. Near its end the book speaks of *the stony law*.

[plates[2].lenses[3].notes[1].gloss] (set under the word)
the sin · the spread tail

[plates[2].lenses[3].notes[1].note] (a note on the word)
The first of the seven deadly sins. In heraldry a peacock *in his pride* is one with its tail spread, and Johnson’s dictionary of 1755 lists ornament, show and splendor among the senses of *pride*.

[plates[2].lenses[3].notes[2].gloss] (set under the word)
a Bible phrase

[plates[2].lenses[3].notes[2].note] (a note on the word)
*The glory of God* comes sixteen times in the King James Bible, *the wisdom of God* five times and *the work of God* six. *The bounty of God* is not in it.

[plates[2].lenses[3].notes[3].gloss] (set under the word)
desire · once pleasure

[plates[2].lenses[3].notes[3].note] (a note on the word)
In Old English the word meant desire and pleasure; the sense of sinful desire came from its use in Bible translations. Johnson records an old sense, vigor, as no longer used, and defines *goatish* by rankness and lust.

[plates[2].lenses[3].notes[4].gloss] (set under the word)
goodness, generous giving

[plates[2].lenses[3].notes[4].note] (a note on the word)
From Latin *bonitas*, goodness, through French. Johnson: generosity, liberality, munificence.

[plates[2].lenses[3].notes[5].gloss] (set under the word)
anger · also God’s

[plates[2].lenses[3].notes[5].note] (a note on the word)
The Old English word was used especially of the anger of a god. *The wrath of God* is a phrase of the English Bible, and in Proverbs the wrath of a king is likened to the roar of a lion.

[plates[2].lenses[3].notes[6].gloss] (set under the word)
why Adam was afraid

[plates[2].lenses[3].notes[6].note] (a note on the word)
In Genesis the man and the woman are naked and not ashamed until the Fall; then Adam is afraid because he is naked, and hides. Leviticus 18 uses the word two dozen times in its prohibitions.

[plates[2].lenses[3].notes[7].gloss] (set under the word)
a going beyond

[plates[2].lenses[3].notes[7].note] (a note on the word)
From Latin *excessus*, a going beyond. Johnson: more than enough; exuberance; violence of passion; transgression of due limits.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In an essay of 1935, “Psychology and Art To-day,” Auden remarked in a parenthesis that Blake’s book holds all that Freud taught.

[auden[0].source]
“Psychology and Art To-day,” in *The Arts To-day*, edited by Geoffrey Grigson (1935); in *Prose*, vol. I (1996), p. 99

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In 1939 Auden began a prose book of his own on this model. He took its title, *The Prolific and the Devourer*, from the *Marriage*, and its epigraph is Blake’s passage on the two classes of men. In a letter of May 1939 he described it as his own new version of Blake’s book.

[auden[1].source]
*The Prolific and the Devourer* (written 1939; Ecco Press, 1994), with Edward Mendelson’s preface, which quotes the letter

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In *New Year Letter*, published in 1941, the year of the Michigan course, Blake is the second of the poets Auden imagines sitting in judgment on him. The lines call him self-taught, say that he broke with Newton’s universe, and have him talk with Isaiah in a London street; in the *Marriage* the speaker dines with Isaiah and Ezekiel. A note to the poem credits Blake with pointing out that dualism arises when the creation is pictured on the model of human politics.

[auden[2].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
And heard inside each mortal thing / Its holy emanation sing

[auden[2].source]
*New Year Letter* (1941), Part I, and the note to line 560; the lines also in *The Collected Poetry of W. H. Auden* (1945), pp. 269–70

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden wrote that Blake saw, as surely as ever, that the quarrel turned on the Incarnation and on the Forgiveness of Sins. If God is only a Judge, he has no power to forgive, only to find guilty or to clear. In a footnote Auden calls the theology of the deists and that of the romantics heresies alike. In his account the first has a God beyond the world, the second a God wholly within it.

[auden[3].source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), pp. 48–50

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden called Blake a strongly polemical writer. Blake’s Enemy, he wrote, was a way of picturing the universe that Blake tied to Newton and held to do grave harm to the mind, to religion, to politics and to art. In his review of 1946, after saying that liberalism went on to adopt a doctrine of inevitable progress, he remarked that Blake was no meliorist. A meliorist believes that human effort can make the world better.

[auden[4].source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), pp. 44–45; “Red Lizards and White Stallions,” *The Saturday Review of Literature*, 13 April 1946, p. 22

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In a conversation that Alan Ansen wrote down between 1946 and 1948, Auden named Blake among the greatest aphorists, with Pascal, Baudelaire, Nietzsche and Kafka. The foreword that he and Louis Kronenberger signed to their book of aphorisms says that an aphorism must convince every reader that it holds universally, or of the whole class of things it speaks of, and that the aphorist states his point and gives no argument for it.

[auden[5].source]
Alan Ansen, *The Table Talk of W. H. Auden*, edited by Nicholas Jenkins (1991); foreword to *The Viking Book of Aphorisms*, edited by W. H. Auden and Louis Kronenberger (1962), p. vii in the Faber edition of 1964

[auden[6].says] (a paraphrase of him)
The one remark on Blake in Donald Pearce’s notes of the 1941 class comes in the lecture on *The Tempest* that December. By those notes, Auden said that, as Blake put it, a man is ruled by his spectre until he sees its reflection, and can then tell the self he seems from the self he is. The saying is not in the *Marriage*. No notes of a class on the *Marriage* itself are among the sources read for this site.

[auden[6].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), p. 151

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
The text printed here is an edition of 1906 in modern spelling. On Blake’s own etched page the Devil’s sentence drops a syllable from two words, which keeps each line to fourteen, and has a comma after its first line; the proverb on excess is two sentences there.

[caveats[1]]
The view at the head joins two texts. The review of 1946 is about the *Marriage*. The pages of *The Enchafèd Flood* (1950) are about Blake and the romantics and do not name the book, though the sentence Auden gives there as Blake’s position comes from it.

[caveats[2]]
Auden’s essay of 1935, *New Year Letter* and its note, the letter of 1939 and the foreword on aphorisms were read only in extracts of scanned copies. His words in class in 1941 and in conversation between 1946 and 1948 come from listeners’ notes, Donald Pearce’s and Alan Ansen’s.

[caveats[3]]
That Blake had Chatterton’s ballad in mind rests on the likeness of the wording and on a reviewer’s remark of 2001. No statement by Blake was found.

[caveats[4]]
The stresses marked are one reader’s hearing. The first two syllables of the Devil’s sentence can be stressed either way, and the first word of its second line can take a light stress.
