## The head of the page

[title]
Senta’s ballad

[where]
*The Flying Dutchman*, Act 2, Scene 1, with lines from Acts 1, 2 and 3

[about] (under “The passage”)
Senta is the daughter of Daland, a Norwegian sea captain. At home, among girls who sit spinning, she looks at a picture of a pale man in black and sings them the ballad of the Flying Dutchman, which she has often heard from her nurse, Mary. The Dutchman is a captain damned to sail without end; every seven years he may land to seek a wife. Her father has just met him on the coast and promised him his daughter.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden left no account of this opera alone. He called Nietzsche the best guide to Wagner. Nietzsche, he wrote, saw one theme return in one opera after another: someone wants to be saved. Here, as Nietzsche has it, marriage saves even the Wandering Jew and settles him down. Auden’s own Wagner, in *New Year Letter* (1941), stages a hero who roars for his mother or for death, and Woman, who redeems.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
And Woman, passive as in dreams, / Redeems, redeems, redeems, redeems.

[view.source]
“The Greatest of the Monsters” (1969), in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), pp. 253–54; *New Year Letter* (1941), as printed in *The Collected Poetry of W. H. Auden* (New York: Random House, 1945), p. 308

### Tab “Beat” (beat)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Beat

[plates[0].lenses[0].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
six lines of eight syllables and four beats, the first four with a beat on every second syllable; then a close of six beats, where after the cry two syllables come before each beat

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Wagner wrote the words himself. Four lines keep one measure, eight syllables with a beat on every second one, while they tell of the ship and its master on watch. Then three lines start on a beat: the cry <b>Hui</b>, which imitates air rushing past. Outside the ballad only the Dutchman’s crew cry it, in Act 3. The two lines about the wind end on the call <b>Johohe</b>, marked here as the printed tune has it: two short notes, then a long one. After the last <b>Hui</b> every beat has two syllables before it, and the tune gives each pair two quick notes. That line says he flies like an arrow.

[plates[0].lenses[0].points[0].say] (a numbered remark)
Said aloud, the first two lines can each begin on a stress: the verb of the question, then *Blut*, blood. The measure puts its beat on the second syllable. In the tune printed in an 1895 libretto the first syllable is a short note before the bar line, the highest of its phrase.

[plates[0].lenses[0].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)
Wagner’s own edition of 1871 prints the close as two lines of three beats each: the cry and the arrow, then the three phrases that begin with *ohne*. This libretto runs them into one long line and carries the last two words over.

[plates[0].lenses[0].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage and what its lines say. *Hui*: the DWDS dictionary and Grimm’s; the German libretto has it on nine lines of the ballad, on three of [the chorus of the Dutchman’s crew](#/read/dutchman/act-3:71) in Act 3, and nowhere else. The tune: the melody printed in the Ditson libretto of 1895, p. 16. Wagner’s authorship and line division: *Gesammelte Schriften und Dichtungen*, vol. 1, pp. 23 and 339.

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The first four lines rhyme by turns: <b>an</b> with <b>Mann</b>, <b>Mast</b> with <b>Rast</b>. A handbook of English verse calls four-beat lines rhymed this way closely similar to the ballad stanza, and Wagner heads the song <i>Ballade</i>. The two lines about the wind end on the same call. The last word, <b>Ruh</b>, the rest he is without, has no rhyme. What ties it to <b>Rast</b> is its first letter: <i>ohne Rast und Ruh</i>, without halt or rest, is a set phrase that Goethe and Schiller also use. In 1851 Wagner wrote that end rhyme was a deceit and that for his <i>Siegfried</i> he had turned to verse rhymed on first sounds.

[plates[0].lenses[1].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage. The ballad stanza: Baum, *The Principles of English Versification*, chapter 4; Wagner’s heading *Ballade*: *Gesammelte Schriften und Dichtungen*, vol. 1, p. 339. The set phrase *ohne Rast und Ruh*, in Goethe and Schiller: Grimm’s dictionary, under *rast* and *ruhe*. Wagner on end rhyme and on verse rhymed by first sounds: “Eine Mittheilung an meine Freunde” (1851), in the same edition, vol. 4, pp. 399–400.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Three phrases that begin with <b>ohne</b>, without, close the stanza. <b>Ziel</b> is a marksman’s mark and a voyage’s end: the line likens him to an arrow and takes the mark away. <b>Rast</b> is a halt on a journey. <b>Ruh</b> is stillness, peace and, in Grimm’s dictionary, the rest found in death. In Act 1 the Dutchman says damnation denies him grave and death; in Act 2 he asks Senta whether her faithfulness will bring the long-desired <i>Ruh</i>. Auden sets the Flying Dutchman beside Ishmael, the exile and the Wandering Jew as names of one figure. Of the Romantic view of the sea he writes that no lasting destination is known.

### Tab “English” (compare)

[plates[0].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
English

[plates[0].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
Beside Wagner’s stanza stands the English of a New York libretto that names no translator. Its first four lines keep his eight syllables, his rhyme by turns and the ship’s colors. They give up his question to the hearers and the watch without a halt. At the close his <i>er</i>, he, flies like an arrow; in the English the ship, <i>she</i>, <b>leaps</b> <b>forever</b>. Auden’s objection to opera in translation lies elsewhere. In song, he believed, a listener hears syllables, not words: Wagner sung in English is intolerable, and would be even if the translation were the better poem, because the new syllables do not suit the pitch and pace of the notes.

[plates[0].lenses[3].ties[0].say] (a numbered remark)
Wagner’s first line asks the hearers a question: did you come upon the ship at sea. The English tells it as a fact.

[plates[0].lenses[3].ties[1].say] (a numbered remark)
The ship’s master keeps watch without a halt. The English leaves out the watch and describes his eyes and his face; its *pale* comes from the line above in Wagner.

[plates[0].lenses[3].ties[2].say] (a numbered remark)
*Hui* imitates rushing air. *Huzza* was first a sailor’s shout of high spirits, encouragement or applause.

[plates[0].lenses[3].ties[3].say] (a numbered remark)
Wagner’s word is *er*, he. The ship is *das Schiff*, neuter, so *er* does not point to it; at this place in the second stanza it is the damned man who sails. In the English the ship, *she*, leaps.

[plates[0].lenses[3].ties[4].say] (a numbered remark)
With the two words carried below, these phrases name three things he is without: a mark to reach, a halt, rest. The English has two words for endless time.

### Tab “English” (compare)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
English

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The first five lines are the refrain, the returning part, sung after the first stanza. The last five are sung in its place after the third: two by the girls, three by Senta. The small lines are the libretto’s English. Twice here Wagner names the condition of the Dutchman’s rescue: a woman true <i>bis in den Tod</i>, until death. The English keeps a true woman and both times leaves the death out; its woman is to win his love and share his treasures. In Act 3 the Dutchman says that only a woman true to him until death can free him, and in her last line Senta calls herself true to him until death.

[plates[1].lenses[0].ties[0].say] (a numbered remark)
Wagner: a woman true to him until death. The English: a woman’s heart that does not fail in its mission.

[plates[1].lenses[0].ties[1].say] (a numbered remark)
Wagner turns to the man himself: *du*, you, pale seaman. The English goes on with *he*.

[plates[1].lenses[0].ties[2].say] (a numbered remark)
The girls ask where he will meet the woman who would stay his own, true until death (*treueigen*, a compound from the love poetry of Wagner’s century). The English woman is to win his love and share his treasures.

[plates[1].lenses[0].ties[3].say] (a numbered remark)
*Treu* is troth, faith kept, kin to English *true*. The English has *love*. The third stanza of the ballad keeps the two apart: false love, false troth.

[plates[1].lenses[0].ties[4].say] (a numbered remark)
Senta asks that God’s angel show her to him. In the English she asks the angel to bring him to her.

[plates[1].lenses[0].ties[5].say] (a numbered remark)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Only one thing in the refrain is stated as fact: <b>Erlösung</b>, redemption, a setting free, <b>kann</b>, can, still come to him. ## Passage 3: One rhyme in three acts  (plates[2], id "oath", from o-dutchman/end)

[plates[2].heading]
One rhyme in three acts

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Three places in the opera share one rhyme. Two lines are the Dutchman’s in Act 1, four are Senta’s in Act 2, two are her last words. Each time <b>Tod</b>, death, rhymes with <b>Gebot</b>, command; the libretto has that noun once in each act, always in this rhyme. First the command is damnation’s and forbids him death. He says he has sought death, and in Auden’s lines the Wagner hero roars for it. In Senta’s lines <b>Tod</b> ends the phrase that means until death, the limit of her <b>Treue</b>, troth, and the two words begin alike. After the last <b>Tod</b> the stage direction has her leap into the sea and his ship sink at once.

[plates[2].lenses[0].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage. A count of *Gebot* in the German libretto: three times, once in each act, each time rhymed with *Tod*; the sounds of the two words: the DWDS dictionary. The Dutchman’s search for death: [his monologue in Act 1](#/read/dutchman/act-1:19); Senta’s leap and the sinking of his ship: the [last stage direction](#/read/dutchman/act-3:138). Auden: *The Collected Poetry* (1945), p. 308.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
<b>Gebot</b> is a command, and the word for each of the Ten Commandments. It has a different owner each time. In Act 1 it is damnation’s: no grave and no death. In Act 2 it is troth’s, and Senta says she knows it: troth until death. At the end it is his angel’s. In Act 1 the Dutchman speaks of an angel of God who won him the condition of his rescue, and in Act 3 he states that condition: only a woman true until death can free him from the curse. Her last line begins with <b>Hier</b>, here, and gives the condition back: true to you until death.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In *The Enchafèd Flood* the Flying Dutchman is one of four names Auden gives to a single figure; the others are Ishmael, the exile and the Wandering Jew. That figure is one half of a double-natured hero he finds in a dream told by Wordsworth. The other half is Don Quixote, the dedicated man.

[auden[0].source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), p. 5

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In the same book he lists what is new in the Romantic attitude to the sea. Every man of feeling and honor wants to leave land and city. Man’s true state is the voyage, and the sea is his real situation. What is decisive happens at sea: the choice that counts forever, temptation, fall and redemption. Life on shore is always trivial. Whether there is a lasting destination nobody knows, and a lasting bond can be neither had nor wished for. He does not apply the list to this opera.

[auden[1].source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), pp. 12–13

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In the 1969 essay he calls Wagner’s operas freaks, which have to be listened to differently from most operas. Wagner, he writes, expected his heroes and heroines to be admired, and what he in fact shows is a line of ill-bred neurotics, drawn with the greatest skill. No one else paints what in people is unreasoning, sick, cranky and bent on its own ruin as it really is, with its spell on it. To get the full good of the operas a listener has to find all this in himself and at the same moment hold it at a distance, as what he must overcome.

[auden[2].source]
“The Greatest of the Monsters” (1969), in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), pp. 253 and 255

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In his own prose note to the lines on Wagner in *New Year Letter*, Auden ranks Wagner first among the Romantic artists. He calls the operas masterpieces when they are taken as studies of tribulation, like a psychoanalyst’s case histories, and absurd when they are taken as dramatic plots, because the characters know beforehand that their mistakes are mistakes; his example is the *Ring*. In giving voice to suffering, he writes, Wagner may be the greatest genius there has been, and his genius stops there.

[auden[3].source]
his note to line 1432 of *New Year Letter* (London: Faber, 1941), pp. 143–44; read only in snippets of a scanned copy, with a gap

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In “Notes on Music and Opera” he writes that a librettist’s verses are meant, like a private letter, for the composer and not for the public, and that, as he believes, people listening to song hear syllables, not words. That is his reason for being, in general, against opera sung in translation. In English, he says, Wagner or Strauss sounds intolerable, and a translation that was the better poem would not help, because the new syllables do not suit the pitch and speed of the notes that carry them.

[auden[4].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
In song, poetry is expendable, syllables are not.

[auden[4].source]
“Notes on Music and Opera,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 473

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In the same notes he says that what opera imitates is human willfulness. Music, he writes, is immediate, and nothing merely potential or passive can live beside it; what the great operatic roles share is that each is a state of passion and will. What is sung is so, and for that reason there can be no self-deception in opera. A good libretto plot gives its characters every possible occasion to be carried away, and the theory of music-drama needs a libretto without one sensible moment, which Wagner managed.

[auden[5].source]
“Notes on Music and Opera,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 470–72

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
Nothing Auden said about this opera by itself is on record. The view at the head of the page is put together from his essay of 1969 and from lines of 1941 that describe Wagner’s dramas in general.

[caveats[1]]
The German shown is that of an American libretto and differs in places from the text Wagner printed in 1871. There Senta’s first line of her own reads, in English: let me be the one who redeems you through her troth.

[caveats[2]]
The last return of the refrain, at the end of Scene 2, differs by edition: Wagner’s text gives its last three lines whole, a libretto of 1901 that agrees with the score breaks off before the last word, and the libretto shown here gives Senta the girls’ two lines.

[caveats[3]]
The stresses marked are one reader’s hearing. The first word of each of the first two lines, and *wacht* in the fourth, can fairly be given more weight or less. The tune was read in the melody printed in a libretto of 1895, not in a score.

[caveats[4]]
In the arrow line *er*, he, is taken to be the pale man, as at the same place in the second stanza. Grammar rules out the ship, which is neuter. It does not rule out the wind, which is masculine too.
