## The head of the page

[title]
K. and the Castle

[where]
*The Castle*, chapters 1, 2 and 8, in the English of Willa and Edwin Muir (1930)

[about] (under “The passage”)
A man the book calls K. comes one night to a snowbound village under a castle and says he is the Land Surveyor the Count is expecting. Next morning he sets out on foot for the Castle: the first passage. That evening a messenger hands him a letter from Klamm, a Castle official: the second. In the third, on his fourth day, K. has waited in an inn yard to catch Klamm at his sleigh, has refused to come away, and is left alone as the lights go out.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden wrote that a typical Kafka story turns the heroic quest upside down. The old hero knows what he must do, and others can tell him the way. Kafka’s hero meets no one who knows it, and K. does not know what he should do. In *The Castle* the letter K. wants to be a word, land-surveyor, and so to have a self like everyone else, and he is not allowed one.

[view.source]
“The I Without a Self,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 162–63

### Tab “Clauses” (sentence)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Clauses

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The second sentence says five things of one street: it does not go up to the Castle hill, makes <b>towards</b> it, turns <b>aside</b>, does not lead <b>away</b>, and gets <b>no nearer</b>. Each new clause takes back part of the last, in Kafka’s German too. One of the translators, Edwin Muir, wrote that Kafka loved a sentence that goes forward, turns back on itself and goes forward again. K. goes on only because he expects the road to come round to the Castle. Auden, writing of the way in the stories typical of Kafka, cites Kafka’s aphorism that there is a goal and no way, and that what is called the way is wavering.

### Tab “Stop” (compare)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Stop

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Between the two passages K. walks on through a village that seems to have no end. Then he tears himself from the street into a side lane where the snow is deeper, breaks into a sweat, and stops. The two words for what he did, <b>go on</b>, come back after <b>could not</b>. Kafka’s German has one word in both places, <i>weiter</i>, onward, and calls pulling the feet out <i>schwere Arbeit</i>, work that is heavy or hard. No other imagined world, Auden wrote, is as heavy as Kafka’s, and one step in it takes all one’s strength.

### Tab “Reading” (compare)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Reading

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
K. finds the letter not <b>consistent</b> and sorts it two ways. The <b>address</b> and the mention of his <b>wishes</b> treat him as a free man. <b>Not to lose sight</b> of him, and a <b>superior</b> who is only a village official, treat him as a minor employee. That the Castle might be undecided hardly occurs to him: he reads the contradiction as a choice left to him. Further on he finds a hint that he made the first move, one only <i>an uneasy conscience</i> would sense: uneasy, the text adds, not bad. Auden wrote that K. cannot take instructions that seem contradictory as a sign of others’ guilt, and that they may prove his own.

The book calls its hero <b>K.</b> 1,046 times. Its editor, Max Brod, notes that Kafka seems to have begun with <i>I</i> and then put K. in its place. ### Tab “Turn” (sentence)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Turn

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
One main verb, <b>seemed</b>, carries four clauses, each led by <b>as if</b>. Three say what staying has brought K.: the others have broken with him, he is freer than ever, and no one dares touch him. Then comes <b>but</b>, and the fourth, which a remark between dashes rates at least as strong: nothing is more senseless or hopeless than this. So the freedom and its hopelessness are both what seemed to him. Kafka’s German has its <i>as if</i> three times; the Muirs add one. Auden wrote that Kafka’s hero feels imprisoned and tries to get out, but that prison may be the state he was made for, and freedom the thing that would destroy him.

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[2].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)
anaphora

[plates[2].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)
the same word or words at the head of phrase after phrase

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The sentence ends on three nouns, each with <b>this</b> before it, after <b>nothing more</b> said twice; Kafka’s German has both repetitions. A word repeated at the head of phrase after phrase is called anaphora, and here it lines the three nouns up as a list. Each names again something the first half gave K.: he was <i>freer</i>, he could <i>wait</i>, and nobody dared <i>touch</i> him. <b>Inviolability</b> is the state of what cannot be injured; Kafka’s word, <i>Unverletzlichkeit</i>, is built on the verb that means to wound. So what the first half gave comes back in the same order, and the sentence says nothing is more senseless or more hopeless than these three.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Donald Pearce took the course and made notes after the lectures. According to them, Auden came to Kafka by way of Descartes, asking which of a man’s several selves is the I that thinks. Of modern writers, he said, Kafka best shows the predicaments of mind and metaphysics, above all in *The Castle*. The book is dressed as a satire on bureaucracy; underneath it is a parable, theological and psychological. For the culture of the twentieth century it is what Dante’s poem was for the fourteenth. He told them to read it with the *Divine Comedy* and Melville in mind, and to ask what K.’s quest is for.

[auden[0].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), pp. 134–35

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In the same class Auden put a record on. He said that the second movement of Beethoven’s Fourth Piano Concerto shows best, so far as music can show such a thing, how a man stands to his fate: Ahab to his whale, or K. to his Castle. When the piano defies the orchestra and goes off by itself it is soon lost and hysterical, and it comes back to something like sanity only when it accepts the orchestra again. He ended by asking whether it has found its way back too late.

[auden[1].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), p. 136

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Pearce also sat in on a class of the next semester, in 1942. According to his notes, Auden said that a poet getting in touch with his daemon is like trying to phone a friend whose number one cannot recall and will not look up. Kafka, he said, describes the tie between the village and the Castle in just these terms: a telephone exchange that at times works very well and often fails, or works without reason, with calls that go astray and messages only half made out. He glossed the village as the conscious mind, the Castle as the unconscious, and knew no better analogy for composing a poem.

[auden[2].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), pp. 155–56

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In February 1941, the year of the course, Auden reviewed Kafka in *The New Republic*. Asked which artist stands to the present age much as Dante, Shakespeare and Goethe did to their own, he wrote, one would think first of Kafka. The three big novels use the oldest device in literature, the Quest. The Kafka hero is Faust turned the other way round. For K. it is being exceptional that makes the trouble, and to become exceptional is to become reflective, and so to find that what is necessary looks arbitrary. This hero’s predicament, he wrote, is contemporary man’s.

[auden[3].source]
“The Wandering Jew,” *The New Republic*, 10 February 1941; reprinted in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 110–13

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In 1946 Auden wrote an essay called “K.’s Quest.” Bunyan’s pilgrim, he says there, must gain heaven by deserving it. K. can earn no right to the title of Land Surveyor: all he can do is ask the Castle to permit it. He suffers and fails for the very reason that he is an individual. Since he cannot stop being K., he will never become the Land Surveyor that he wishes to be. Auden calls the contradiction comic: K. is one letter, and Land Surveyor sounds much the grander, yet K. is an individual’s name, and land survey only defines an occupation.

[auden[4].source]
“K.’s Quest,” in *The Kafka Problem*, edited by Angel Flores (1946), pp. 47–52

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In *The Dyer’s Hand* Auden calls Kafka a master, perhaps the greatest, of the pure parable, and says that critics have little of worth to say about parables. A parable’s hero may have a name or be called only K.; the name, the time and the place do not bear on what it means. To find the meaning a reader has to give up standing outside and identify with what he reads, so the meaning differs with every reader. A critic who interprets a parable only shows what it has done to him.

[auden[5].source]
“The I Without a Self,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 159–60

[auden[6].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Max Brod’s life of Kafka, he notes, describes a Czech novel about a village under a castle, in which the castle’s officials are plainly evil. He takes this as a sign that the readers who took the people of Kafka’s Castle for agents of divine grace had it wrong, and he sides with a reading in which its officers are indifferent to good where they are not wicked. Fine as *The Trial* and *The Castle* are, he adds, he thinks Kafka’s best work is in *The Great Wall of China*, a volume written in his last six years.

[auden[6].source]
“The I Without a Self,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 160–61

[auden[7].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In the same essay he sets Kafka’s hero against the heroes of older quests. Their goal is one that everybody wants, and they are confident of reaching it. His goal is his alone. He is sure from the outset that he will fail, and is doomed all the same to enormous efforts. A wrong guess costs him what a wrong choice would, and he must feel as responsible for it. From the beginning he is an I, and his guilt lies in the bare fact that he exists.

[auden[7].source]
“The I Without a Self,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 162–63

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
The English is the Muirs’ translation of 1930, the one Auden’s students read. Where a caption says what Kafka’s German has, the German was read in an edition of 1950 and after. The Muirs worked from the first edition of 1926, which was not compared.

[caveats[1]]
Auden’s sentences about the way, about contradictory instructions, about heaviness and about freedom are said of a typical Kafka story, its hero and its world. He cites none of the three passages shown here. Only his sentence about the letter and the word names *The Castle*.

[caveats[2]]
Auden’s review of 1941, “The Wandering Jew,” and his essay of 1946, “K.’s Quest,” were read here only in search snippets of scanned copies. What he said in class is known from notes a student, Donald Pearce, made afterwards, not from Auden’s own text.
