## The head of the page

[title]
The laws and the city

[where]
*Antigone*: the elders’ song on man, Antigone’s answer, Creon and his son

[about] (under “The passage”)
Two brothers have killed each other fighting over Thebes, and Creon, the new king, orders that one lie unburied on pain of death; their sister Antigone buries him. The first passage ends a song that the chorus, the city’s elders, sing before they know who did it. In the second Antigone, caught, answers the king (two and a half lines are left out at the dots), and in the third Creon quarrels with his son Haemon, who is to marry her. The English is F. Storr’s verse translation of 1912.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
In 1941 Auden made Antigone his instance of the hero who accepts a tragic choice: seeing that she cannot both obey the law and do her duty to her brother, she chooses, and her death is her own will. In 1967 he held that for Sophocles the chief figure is Creon: Antigone’s part is to reveal his hubris, the overconfidence of a man who thinks himself a god, beyond suffering.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
because she realizes that she cannot do both, she is free to choose, chooses, and wills her death

[view.source]
“James Joyce and Richard Wagner,” *Common Sense*, March 1941, in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), p. 116; “The Martyr as Dramatic Hero,” in *Secondary Worlds* (1968), p. 19; on what hubris is, “The Greeks and Us” (1948), in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), p. 19

## Passage 1: The elders end their song on man  (plates[0], id "elders", from antigone/p2)

### Tab “Greek” (word)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Greek

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The elders name two things for a man to honor: <b>the laws of the land</b>, and what Storr calls <b>the Gods of the State</b>, which in the Greek is the gods’ justice, sworn by oath. The man who honors both is <i>hypsipolis</i>, one word made of <i>high</i> and <i>city</i>. Next to it stands its opposite, <i>apolis</i>, <b>cityless</b>: the man whose daring brings him to wrong. The stanza asks for law and justice together. In the second passage Antigone says that Justice made no such laws as Creon’s. Auden’s Antigone owes one duty to her dead brother and one to her city, and must break one.

[plates[0].lenses[0].notes[0].gloss] (set under the word)

[plates[0].lenses[0].notes[0].note] (a note on the word)

[plates[0].lenses[0].notes[1].note] (a note on the word)

### Tab “Beat” (beat)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Beat

[plates[0].lenses[1].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
six beats to the line, mostly three and three, with one or two syllables between the beats

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Each line has six beats, with one or two syllables between them, and four of the six have a pause after the third. A handbook of verse says that lines of more than five beats tend to break up into smaller units. The speeches as a rule go in five beats without rhyme; the song leaves that line, as Sophocles here leaves spoken verse for song. The beat is Storr’s: Auden notes that unrhymed Greek verse, measured by syllable length, and rhymed English verse share nothing but rhythm. Twice Storr sets half against half: <b>the light</b> against <b>counsels of ill</b>, a standing city against a man with none.

[plates[0].lenses[1].points[0].say] (a numbered remark)
Here the phrase runs on past the third beat, and the line has no pause inside it.

[plates[0].lenses[1].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)
*Now* stands once in each half of the line. Said as a contrast, it takes the beat both times; *man*, just before the first, can also be heard stressed.

[plates[0].lenses[1].points[2].say] (a numbered remark)
*City* takes a beat in each half: the city that stands, then *cityless*.

[plates[0].lenses[1].points[3].say] (a numbered remark)
The dictionary stresses this word on its first syllable, and so it takes the line’s first beat. Said lightly, as a run-up to the next word, it leaves the line five beats, and the inner rhyme then falls on the second.

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The rhyme is Storr’s. Greek verse as a rule had none. The line ends rhyme in pairs: <b>skill</b> with <b>ill</b>, <b>State</b> with <b>rate</b>, <b>depart</b> with <b>heart</b>. The lines rhyme midway too: <b>flight</b> with <b>light</b>, <b>land</b> with <b>stand</b>, <b>pride</b> with <b>side</b>. Each pair of long lines is also four short ones rhymed by turns. In Sophocles man’s skill brings him now to evil, now to good. Storr has the other order, and <b>ill</b> comes last, where it rhymes with <b>skill</b>. Storr held that a translation must be English before anything else. Auden thought it almost a rule that the more a version succeeds as English verse, the less it resembles the Greek.

[plates[0].lenses[2].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage. Baum, chapter 5 (Greek verse rejected rhyme) and chapter 4 (a short quatrain printed as a long couplet). The Greek of line 367 and Jebb’s translation of it, for the order of evil and good. Storr’s introduction to his *Sophocles* (1912), p. xii; Auden, *Forewords and Afterwords*, p. 5.

### Tab “Song” (shape)

[plates[0].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
Song

[plates[0].lenses[3].what] (printed over the drawing)
the song on man: its four stanzas, what comes before it and what comes after

[plates[0].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
This stanza is the last of four. The song follows a guard’s report that someone has buried the body; the elders do not know who. Three stanzas count what man has mastered: sea and earth; bird, beast and fish; speech, thought, city life, shelter. For death alone he has found no cure. The fourth turns from his power to its use. In the Greek the last two stanzas each set two opposites side by side: all-inventive and resourceless, high-citied and cityless. Auden wrote that the tragic hero is a warning to the chorus, and the man to imitate is the moderate citizen who keeps the law.

### Tab “Sides” (figure)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Sides

[plates[1].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
antithesis

[plates[1].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
two opposed things set side by side, often in phrases of the same build; here what is the king’s against what is the gods’

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Creon asks whether she dared break <b>the law</b>. Her answer makes the one law two, and the marks sort its terms. On the king’s side: <b>these human laws</b>, <b>a mortal man</b>, <b>a breath</b>. On the gods’ side: <b>Zeus</b>, <b>Justice</b>, and the <b>unwritten laws of Heaven</b>, which she describes by what they are not: unwritten, unchanging, <b>not born today nor yesterday</b>, undying. Sophocles sets the same contraries close together, and the name for that is antithesis. As Auden saw her in 1941, Antigone has to choose between the law and her duty to her brother. The ambiguity, he wrote, belongs to her situation and not to her: she refuses to leave it ambiguous.

### Tab “Knowing” (figure)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Knowing

[plates[1].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)
polyptoton

[plates[1].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)
one root repeated in different forms; here *knew* and *know* in one line, and the verb returns twice below

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Creon has asked whether she knew of his order. Storr gives her the verb three times in one line: <b>knew</b>, <b>knew</b>, <b>know</b>. A word brought back in a changed form is called polyptoton. Sophocles has the verb once in that line and twice below, where the marks stand: no one <b>knoweth</b> where the gods’ laws came from, and <b>I know that I must die</b>. To die sooner she counts as <b>gain</b>. In Auden’s account of 1941 her freedom comes from seeing that she cannot both obey the law and do her duty to her brother: she chooses, and the death that follows is one she wills.

### Tab “Greek” (word)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Greek

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
In Sophocles’ Greek Creon asks if she dared overstep the laws. Two lines earlier, before the passage begins, he called his order a thing proclaimed. She takes up that word, the herald’s, three times here: Zeus did not have this cried; <i>your proclamations</i>, Storr’s <b>a breath</b>, have no such strength; she must die even had he not <b>proclaimed</b> it. Laws she says once, in a line the editors correct: Justice set no such laws among men. She denies that a mortal’s proclamations can override the gods’ usages, <b>unwritten</b> and not liable to fall. For Auden the decree, though perhaps too vindictive, is not plainly evil; Creon’s guilt is hubris toward the gods.

Exchange

[plates[2].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
stichomythia

[plates[2].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
two speakers answer each other a line at a time, often taking up each other’s words

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
After a long speech each, father and son trade single lines, twenty-eight in all; ten are shown. This is stichomythia. In it, the dictionary says, one speaker usually opposes or corrects the other, often by repeating part of his words. The colors mark words taken up: the city (<b>commons</b>, <b>mob</b>, <b>State</b>), ruling, and <b>woman</b>. Sophocles has all three. Auden held that Antigone’s part in the play is to reveal Creon’s hubris toward the gods. The first line here asks about her. By the seventh Creon claims the <b>State is his who rules it</b>. Four lines past the last shown, Haemon tells him that he spurns the gods’ due.

### Tab “City” (word)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
City

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Where Storr has <b>commons</b>, <b>mob</b> and <b>State</b>, Sophocles gives both men one word, <i>polis</i>, or a word built on it: a city, and the body of its citizens. <b>Mob</b>, slang of the 1680s for the rabble, is Storr’s: Creon says <i>the city</i>. <b>Monarch</b> is close: Sophocles has <i>rule alone</i>, the two Greek words behind the English one. <b>Desert</b> is <i>an empty land</i>. The elders sing that the man who honors the laws has a high city, and Creon’s first speech has the same noun for laws: by such laws, or rules, he strengthens the city. His son tells him that a city owned by one man is no city.

### Tab “Play” (shape)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Play

[plates[2].lenses[2].what] (printed over the drawing)
who speaks in the play and for how many lines, and where Antigone leaves it

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
In Storr’s English Creon speaks 339 of the play’s 1,279 lines and Antigone 205. After her last line, 883, come 396 more: the prophet Teiresias warns Creon, he gives way too late, and news comes of her death, his son’s and his wife’s. The messenger says Creon was a man to envy and now all is gone. That is the course Auden gives the hero of Greek tragedy in general: honored and fortunate when the play begins, in exceptional suffering when it ends. In 1967 he said that for Sophocles the play’s chief figure is Creon. Of the single speakers Creon has the most lines, and the last words before the elders end the play.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In a lecture of 1967 Auden takes Antigone as a martyr from before Christianity. Since every society abhors a traitor, he says, Creon’s decree may be thought too vindictive, but it is not plainly evil. What ruins Creon is punishment for his hubris toward the gods and not for how he treats Antigone, which is a sign of that hubris. If she had obeyed, some other course of events would in time have brought it out and brought a like punishment on him.

[auden[0].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
For Sophocles, the principal figure in the tragedy is not Antigone, but Creon.

[auden[0].source]
“The Martyr as Dramatic Hero,” in *Secondary Worlds* (the T. S. Eliot Memorial Lectures of 1967; 1968), pp. 18–19

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In 1948 Auden wrote that the tragic hero of the Greeks is a warning, and that the warning is meant for the chorus, who are ordinary citizens. The hero begins the play honored and fortunate and ends it suffering more than they do, because he has run against a law of right that binds everyone. Usually he could not have avoided it: every course open to him is wrong, and Antigone is one of four examples. Such a position is the sign of another guilt, hubris. Watching him fall, the chorus learns that strength tempts its owner to impiety, and that what makes the gods gods is their justice.

[auden[1].source]
“The Greeks and Us” (the introduction to *The Portable Greek Reader*, 1948), in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), p. 19

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
He goes on to say what each citizen of the democracy ought to aim at being: a moderate man who keeps the law and has no wish to outdo everyone else in strength or fame, and not the Homeric hero.

[auden[2].source]
“The Greeks and Us” (1948), in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), pp. 19–20

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
On reading the Greeks in English, Auden held that Greek literature in translation can no longer be approached as an aesthetic matter, since so much is lost between Greek and English that the loss is all but fatal. Among the obstacles he lists: Greek verse is unrhymed and measured by syllable length; Greek shows by the forms of its words what English shows by their order; Greek has compound epithets in plenty and English has few. Of dialogue in single lines he says that a version in plain modern idiom loses something essential to the Greek manner, which decks plain question and answer out as riddles.

[auden[3].source]
“The Greeks and Us” (1948), in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), pp. 5–7

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
Auden’s reading list sets the *Antigone*, as an alternative to the *Agamemnon*, in the translation of Dudley Fitts and Robert Fitzgerald (1939); the English here is F. Storr’s of 1912, and the beat, the rhyme and the choice of English words are Storr’s.

[caveats[1]]
Auden’s sentences of 1941 and 1967 about the play were read here only in snippets of scanned copies and not in full; the page of the 1941 sentences was worked out from the page headings near them, not seen.

[caveats[2]]
The counts of lines are of Storr’s English as printed here, where five lines split between two speakers count for each; in the Greek, by the usual line numbers, Creon has 358 lines of 1,353 and Antigone 215, and the chorus, with 362, has four more than Creon.

[caveats[3]]
The beats were marked by ear: *man* in the second line shown and the end of *outcast* in the fourth can be heard with more stress, and the fifth has six beats only if *Whoso* is stressed on its first syllable, as the dictionary gives it.

[caveats[4]]
Two of the Greek readings followed here are editors’ corrections of the manuscripts, the verb for *honors* in the song (line 368) and *such laws* in Antigone’s answer (line 452); and in line 369 *the gods’ justice* can also be read, with Jebb, as justice sworn by the gods.
