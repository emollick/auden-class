## The head of the page

[title]
The Fox’s gold, song and sentence

[where]
*Volpone*, Acts 1, 3 and 5

[about] (under “The passage”)
Volpone, a rich Venetian with no heir, pretends to be dying so that men who hope to inherit will bring him gifts, and his servant Mosca lets each think he is the heir. In the first passage Volpone greets his gold. In the second he sings to Celia, whose husband has left her alone with him in hope of the inheritance. In the third a judge closes the trial at which Volpone, Mosca and the three men they cheated have been sentenced, and Volpone steps forward to the audience.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
One remark by Auden on this play was found. In conversation that Alan Ansen noted down in his entry for January 8, 1947, he said he had a special liking for *The Alchemist* and *Bartholomew Fair*, and found *Volpone* too unpleasant. Of Jonson’s comedies as a group he wrote that they are classical where Shakespeare’s are Christian. Christian comedy ends with its people exposed and forgiven, classical comedy with its people exposed and punished.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
In classical comedy the characters are exposed and punished: when the curtain falls, the audience is laughing and those on stage are in tears.

[view.source]
“The Globe,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 177; Alan Ansen, *The Table Talk of W. H. Auden*, edited by Nicholas Jenkins (1991), p. 18

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Volpone addresses his gold in words of Christian worship. It is his <b>Saint</b>. He greets it with <b>Hail</b>, the greeting that begins the prayer to Mary. He asks to kiss it <i>with adoration</i>, and each <b>relick</b> of the hoard besides, which he calls <b>sacred</b> treasure in a <b>blessed</b> room. A <b>shrine</b> was the casket of a saint’s relics. Auden wrote that the basic pattern of Jonson’s comedy of humors is the morality play with one of the seven deadly sins made its hero. A humor was a person’s temperament, his habitual bent. A morality play had qualities such as virtues and vices for its chief persons. The hero of this comedy begins Act 1 by worshipping money.

### Tab “Sentence” (sentence)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Sentence

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The sentence starts with what he is, <b>more glad</b>, and keeps back who is glad. First comes the comparison: the <i>teeming</i> earth sees the sun enter the Ram, as it does in March. Verb and subject come three lines later: <b>Am I</b>. Keeping back the main words holds a hearer in suspense, which throws the emphasis on the words waited for. The earth’s gladness at the longed-for sun is told in full; then the speaker outdoes it. What gladdens him is the gold’s <i>splendour darkening his</i>, and <b>his</b> is the sun’s. Then the gold is likened to a flame at night and to <i>the day struck out of chaos</i>.

### Tab “Beat” (beat)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Beat

[plates[0].lenses[2].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
blank verse: ten syllables and five beats, the line beginning off the beat

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
These lines are blank verse: ten syllables to the line, a beat on every second one, and no pattern of rhyme. One line, ending <b>relick</b>, runs a syllable past the last beat. Volpone gives two greetings of six syllables. In <i>Good morning to the day</i> three unstressed syllables run together before <b>day</b>. <b>Hail the world’s soul, and mine</b> has four stresses: it starts on one, against the measure, and at <b>world’s soul</b> two stand together, which slows the line. He names the gold second and gives it the heavier, slower greeting.

[plates[0].lenses[2].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)

[plates[0].lenses[2].points[2].say] (a numbered remark)

## Passage 2: Volpone’s song to Celia: lines 1 to 10 and 15 to 18  (plates[1], id "song", from volpone/a3s6)

[plates[1].heading]
Volpone’s song to Celia: lines 1 to 10 and 15 to 18

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The song is in short couplets: lines of four beats, rhymed in pairs. The quick return of the rhyme makes each pair a unit, and the short line, so often repeated, gives an effect of hurry. Hurry is what the song urges: love <i>while we can</i>. Twice a pair is split: its second line starts a statement that runs through the next pair. The first is the three lines on <b>suns</b>, light and night, taken from the Latin poet Catullus. The second is the rule that closes the song, where the split pair rhymes <b>steal</b> with <b>reveal</b>, the act with its discovery. One sound picks out four words of the rule: <b>sin</b>, <b>steal</b>, <b>sweet</b>, <b>seen</b>.

The marks on the passage, and a count of the nine rhymed pairs of the whole song in [Act 3, Scene 6](#/read/volpone/a3s6:106): two are split. The short couplet, its hurry, and the repeated first sound: Paull F. Baum, *The Principles of English Versification* (1922), chapters 4 and 5. The lines on suns: Catullus, poem 5, lines 4 to 6, in E. ### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The song is made from a Latin poem, Catullus’s to Lesbia, which opens with a call to live and love. Jonson’s <b>suns</b> that set and rise are Catullus’s <i>soles</i>, his <b>perpetual night</b> is <i>nox perpetua</i>, and <b>rumour</b> is the <i>rumores</i>, the talk of stern old men, which Catullus values at one copper coin. Catullus goes on to count kisses. This song turns to watchers: the four lines left out here ask whether spies and a man’s ears cannot be deceived. The closing rule is not in Catullus’s poem. Latin poets called secret loves thefts, <i>furta</i>, and the rule carries the figure through: <b>steal</b>, <b>thefts</b>, <b>taken</b>, <b>crimes</b>.

Figure

[plates[1].lenses[2].plain] (the figure in plain words)

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The last four lines are an antithesis. On one side is the act: to <b>steal</b> love’s fruits is <b>no sin</b>. On the other is being found out: to <b>reveal</b> the theft, to be <b>taken</b> or <b>seen</b>, these are the <b>crimes</b>. Celia’s husband has argued the same way earlier in the scene, asking who shall know it. Before the scene ends Bonario rushes in to free her, and Volpone cries that he is unmasked and undone. In the last scene he throws off his own disguise. Auden says that classical comedy ends with its people exposed and punished.

## Passage 3: The court’s last words and the Fox’s  (plates[2], id "close", from volpone/a5s8)

[plates[2].heading]
The court’s last words and the Fox’s

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The judge’s speech has no rhyme until its last two lines, <b>feed</b> and <b>bleed</b>. On Shakespeare’s stage rhyme was often used to end a scene, and Acts 2 and 3 of this play end on a rhymed pair. This pair ends the trial, and the court goes out. Then the Fox comes forward and speaks six lines, all in rhymed pairs. Each rhyme joins a word of his case to a word of the audience: <b>laws</b> with <b>applause</b>, <b>due</b> with <b>you</b>, <b>stands</b> with <b>hands</b>. The court has judged him, and in these rhymes he puts his case to the audience, to censure him or to clap.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The Fox uses words of the table and of the court. Applause is a play’s <b>seasoning</b>, and <b>fare</b> meant to feed as well as to get on. A <b>fact</b> was a deed, most often a crime, and to <b>censure</b> could mean to pass sentence: the judge used the word of Mosca’s punishment. In the letter printed before the play Jonson grants that his ending may <i>meet with censure</i> by the strict law of comedy, and answers that the ancients’ comedies do not always end in joy and that a comic poet must imitate justice. Auden calls Jonson’s comedies classical, and says classical comedy ends with its people exposed and punished.

The five persons the court sentences have creatures’ names, which an Italian dictionary of Jonson’s day explains. <i>Volpone</i> is an old fox, <i>Mosca</i> a fly, <i>Voltore</i> a vulture, <i>Corbaccio</i> a filthy great raven, <i>Corvino</i> of a raven’s nature. A medieval book of beasts tells how the fox shams death until birds settle on it, then eats them. Each bird brings the Fox a gift in hope of his estate: a piece of plate, gold coin, a pearl, a diamond. Auden describes what happens in a comedy by Jonson: a rascal with brains cheats a fool with money, and the fool deserves it, since he wants nothing more moral than the rascal does. The court punishes all five.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Writing of the Elizabethan playwrights, Auden calls Jonson the one highbrow among them and says his debt to the morality play is greater than his debt to Latin comedy. Remove the morality play’s hero, Everyman, he says, and make a deadly sin, one of the seven, the hero in his place; let the remaining six band together to gain by it. That gives the plan on which Jonson’s comedies of humors are built.

[auden[0].source]
“The Globe,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 173

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden writes that the first sight of Iago and Roderigo together, in *Othello*, shows the situation of a comedy by Jonson: a clever rascal is cheating a rich fool, and the fool deserves it, since he wants nothing more moral than the rogue does. If *Othello* were a comedy, he adds, Roderigo would find out at last and not dare go to law, since the whole truth would make him look ridiculous or shameful.

[auden[1].source]
“The Joker in the Pack,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 252

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In an essay of 1948 Auden describes a type of comedy, invented by the Greeks, which grew, he says, into several kinds, among them the comedy of humors that Jonson wrote. Its target is the man who breaks the common rule, not believing that it binds him. He collides with others who stand as far outside the law as he does, while the law leaves him alone. He suffers; the audience, who do not see themselves in him, do not. His suffering teaches him to keep the law, out of caution if not out of conscience.

[auden[2].source]
“The Greeks and Us” (1948), in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), p. 30

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
No account of *Volpone* by Auden was found, only one remark made in conversation. What he says here about Jonson’s comedies as a group is set beside these passages by this page; he did not apply it to these lines himself.

[caveats[1]]
His remark that *Volpone* is too unpleasant comes from Alan Ansen’s record of his conversation, not from Auden’s own writing. The book was not seen in full: the words, the page and the date were read in short extracts of a scanned copy.

[caveats[2]]
Some syllables can fairly be stressed another way, among them *Good* in the first line of Act 1, and *no* and *love’s* in the song’s line on sin. The captions rest only on stresses that are not in doubt.

[caveats[3]]
The Oxford dictionary quotes the *shrine* lines among figurative uses of the word for a place of worship; the casket is its older sense. The second senses given for *fare* (to feed) and *mischiefs* (wrongdoers) were in use in Jonson’s time; that he meant both at once is an inference.

[caveats[4]]
The song is shown without its lines 11 to 14. Act and scene numbers are this text’s: the Oxford dictionary cites a line of its Act 1, Scene 1 as Act 1, Scene 3 of Jonson’s folio of 1616, so other editions may number these scenes differently.
