## The head of the page

[title]
Two riddles and an onion

[where]
*Peer Gynt*, Acts 2 and 5

[about] (under “The passage”)
Peer Gynt is a peasant widow’s son in Norway, known for his tall tales. In Act 2 he asks the troll king, the Old Man of the Dovrë, for his daughter and his kingdom, and the Old Man tests him with a riddle. In Act 5 Peer comes home old and shipwrecked, and a Button-Moulder is waiting to melt him down as a man who has never been himself. Solveig, the girl he left in Act 3, has waited in the forest hut all that time.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
In February 1941, the year of the course, Auden wrote that every answer is provisional: Solveig’s settles no more than the earlier ones, and Peer has the Button-Moulder still to meet. In *The Dyer’s Hand* he reads Peer as the poet, for whom almost everything he does is a part to play.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
Throughout Peer Gynt, one question keeps being asked and answered in various ways, namely, Who am I? What is my real self?

[view.source]
“Genius & Apostle,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 436 and 438; “The Wandering Jew,” *The New Republic*, 10 February 1941; reprinted in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 110–13

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[0].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
antithesis

[plates[0].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
two opposed things set side by side, often in phrases of the same build

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The Old Man sets Peer a riddle: what is the <b>difference</b> between a troll and a man. He answers it himself with two sayings, each with a place, the same frame (<b>the saying goes</b>), a name called out and a command. Contrasted things set side by side in matching form are called antithesis. In Ibsen the trolls’ command is the men’s three words with one more after a dash, and that word is the whole difference between the two commands. The translators, William and Charles Archer, also add a <i>to</i> and turn two words round. In his next speech the Old Man calls the added word <b>sundering</b>, dividing.

The Archers’ translation gives up Ibsen’s rhymes and keeps his measure, line for line: four beats, here with one or two syllables between them. ### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
<b>Enough</b> means as much as is needed. The Old Man calls it a <b>sundering</b> word, one that divides, to be cut on Peer’s <b>escutcheon</b>, the shield with his coat of arms. Auden, quoting both sayings in <i>The Enchafèd Flood</i>, says that to be enough to oneself is to have no conscious I set over against the self: one cannot refuse oneself, tell fantasy from fact, or lie. In <i>The Dyer’s Hand</i> he applies the word to the poet, who as a poet manages without other people. In Act 5 the word alone is Peer’s reply; a few lines on he says it again with two names: <i>a hill-troll</i>, <i>an egoist</i>.

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[0].gloss] (set under the word)
as much as is needed

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[0].note] (a note on the word)
From Old English *genog*, sufficient. A dictionary of today’s Norwegian enters the trolls’ phrase as a set expression: to care about no one else.

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[1].gloss] (set under the word)
dividing, setting apart

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[1].note] (a note on the word)
From Old English *sundrian*, to divide, from *sundor*, apart; the word that survives in *asunder*.

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[2].gloss] (set under the word)
shield bearing a coat of arms

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[2].note] (a note on the word)
From Old North French *escuchon*, from Latin *scutum*, a shield; in English since the late 1400s.

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[3].gloss] (set under the word)

[plates[0].lenses[2].notes[3].note] (a note on the word)
Said alone as a cry, meaning that is enough, the word is recorded in English from about 1600. In Ibsen the reply is the motto’s word too, *nok*, which can also mean that a limit has been reached.

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[1].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
extended metaphor

[plates[1].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
one thing spoken of as another, with the likeness carried on part by part

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Peer tells himself he is an <b>onion</b>, not that he is like one. He carries the likeness on: the <b>outermost layer</b> is the <b>shipwrecked man</b>, then the <b>passenger</b>, then the gold-digger. A metaphor carried on like this is an extended metaphor. The marks pair each coat with its man. From <b>swathings</b> on, the onion words stand alone: where Peer looks for a <b>kernel</b>, he names nobody. In Act 4 he throws off a Turkish costume with a line that begins like the first coat’s, <i>there lies the Turk</i>, then <i>here stand I</i>; that part, he says, was <i>only a matter of clothes</i>. For Auden, almost everything Peer does is a part he plays.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
<b>Kaiser</b>, from <i>Caesar</i>, is the German for emperor; the Archers’ translation uses it for Ibsen’s <i>kejser</i>. In Act 1 Peer tells his mother he <i>will be a king, a kaiser</i>, and Auden says the only empire safe from everyone else is one’s own consciousness. Just before the passage Peer composes his epitaph as Kaiser of the beasts; the passage begins by taking the title back. One coat is an <b>ego</b>, Latin for I: Ibsen writes <i>jeget</i>, the I. Peer looks for a <b>kernel</b>, the seed or core, and finds <b>swathings</b>, wrappings, in Ibsen simply layers. The scene ends with his cry, on hearing Solveig sing, that here was his <i>Kaiserdom</i>.

emperor; from Latin Caesar

[plates[1].lenses[2].notes[0].note] (a note on the word)
The German title, taken early from Latin *Caesar*. In the Cairo madhouse Peer is crowned with straw as Kaiser of the self.

[plates[1].lenses[2].notes[1].gloss] (set under the word)

[plates[1].lenses[2].notes[1].note] (a note on the word)
Ibsen’s word is *jeget*, the I: the pronoun made into a noun. *Ego* is the Latin pronoun, used in English philosophy for the self as the one who thinks and acts.

### Tab “Beat” (beat)

[plates[1].lenses[3].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)

[plates[1].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
This is Ibsen’s measure for most of the play, kept in the Archers’ translation without his rhymes: four beats, the syllables between them free. William Archer took it to be designed for variety and suppleness in dialogue, and found it fit for moving speech as well as comic. Each of the three coats shown takes two lines. The last line shown is split by a stage direction, as in Ibsen: two beats, Peer throws the pieces away, two beats more.

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[2].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
anaphora

[plates[2].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
the same words at the head of line after line, or of phrase after phrase

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Peer begins two lines with the same three words, <b>Where was I</b>. Solveig answers in three phrases, all built on two words, <b>in my</b>. Starting line after line, or phrase after phrase, with the same words is called anaphora. His <i>where</i> asks for a place; her <i>in my</i> gives three, each something of hers. Peer starts back, calls these <i>juggling words</i>, and tells her that she is herself the mother of the man who is there. She says she is. Auden counts her line among the answers to the play’s question, and says Ibsen does not decide whether that faith is well founded.

[plates[2].lenses[0].parts[2].label] (in the key)

Solveig and Peer share the first line, two beats each. Peer’s two questions then fill the measure, four beats each; the voice adds a stress on <b>God’s</b>, between beats. Solveig’s answer has ten syllables and three stresses, on <b>faith</b>, <b>hope</b> and <b>love</b>; a fourth beat would have to fall on a small word. The translators’ note says they gave up the measure of this line because padding would spoil its simplicity. In Ibsen the line ends on his word for love, rhyming with the last word of Peer’s half-line.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Peer asks where he was <b>as myself</b>. The men’s saying in Act 2 was <i>be thyself</i>, and Ibsen has one word in both, <i>selv</i>, self. Peer then asks where he was with God’s <b>sigil</b> on his brow: a seal, sign or mark, in Ibsen <i>Stempel</i>, a stamp. The Button-Moulder has told him that to be oneself is to show the Master’s intention like a signboard. Auden quotes those lines as the Button-Moulder’s comment on the men’s saying. Solveig answers with three nouns, the three Paul names in 1 Corinthians, in his order: faith, hope, charity. By the translators’ note, Ibsen’s word for <b>love</b> also means charity in the biblical sense.

[plates[2].lenses[2].notes[0].note] (a note on the word)
In Ibsen one word, *selv*, stands in both sayings of Act 2 and in this question. Auden remarks that the English word can stand for the one who is aware or for the one he is aware of.

[plates[2].lenses[2].notes[1].note] (a note on the word)

The Button-Moulder’s voice comes into Solveig’s lullaby with two lines. The second begins to say what will be seen at the last cross-road, <b>And then we’ll see whether</b>, and stops: <b>I say no more</b>. Breaking off in mid-sentence is called aposiopesis. Three times he has let Peer go on to the next cross-road; the third time he also breaks off, at <i>but then</i>. Solveig then sings louder; her two lines return in the opposite order, with <b>dream</b> added. Auden wrote in 1941 that a staging in which Peer dies here gets the play wrong: Solveig’s answer settles no more than the others, and Peer has the Button-Moulder still to meet.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In an essay of 1941 Auden makes the trolls’ saying the motto of a closed society and the human saying the motto of the adult member of an open one. In *The Dyer’s Hand* he calls the trolls’ motto the motto of the animals. He gives the human saying as a command to be true to oneself. That is the wording of R. Farquharson Sharp’s Everyman translation, the edition named on his reading list for the course, in which the two commands differ by one word. The Archers’ translation, printed here, has *be thyself*. The Button-Moulder’s lines he quotes in 1962 are in the Archers’ words.

[auden[0].source]
“Criticism in a Mass Society,” in *The Intent of the Critic*, edited by Donald A. Stauffer (1941), pp. 130–31; “Balaam and His Ass” and “Genius & Apostle,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 110 and 439; *Peer Gynt*, translated by R. Farquharson Sharp, Everyman’s Library (1921), Act 2, scene 6

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In *The Enchafèd Flood* (1950) Auden quotes the troll king’s riddle and its answer, in the Everyman wording, and calls it a catechism. The prospect of putting off human nature and being a troll, he says, draws every man, and each keeps something back, as Peer does. He also quotes the Button-Moulder’s answer about being oneself beside Father Mapple’s sermon in *Moby-Dick*, and says the two say the same thing. Near the end he has the Button-Moulder wait for the artist at the next cross-roads, to ask whether he has become himself.

[auden[1].source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), pp. 85–86, 116–17 and 149

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Three times in *The Dyer’s Hand* Auden tells another scene from the troll hall. The troll king proposes a small operation on Peer’s eye that would make a true troll of him, and Peer refuses. He will gladly call a cow a lovely girl, but he will not be altered until he cannot tell which is which. Auden calls that keeping his humanity, which includes the power to lie. In “Genius & Apostle” the scene shows the poet, who pretends for the fun of it and knows his made-up worlds are made up.

[auden[2].source]
“Balaam and His Ass,” “The Prince’s Dog” and “Genius & Apostle,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 110, 194 and 438

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In *The Dyer’s Hand* Auden says that Solveig’s answer is Peer’s own belief said back to him, and that the price of being a poet may yet be the Button-Moulder’s casting-ladle. So far, he adds, Peer has been lucky in the women behind him. He takes the bond of mother and child in the play as a parable of a love that time does not touch and that nothing either one does can change. In 1946 he set the ending beside that of Goethe’s *Faust*, found Faust’s rescue unconvincing, and called Ibsen the more honest of the two, since *Peer Gynt* ends with the Button-Moulder warning Peer of another meeting.

[auden[3].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
Ibsen leaves in doubt the question whether this faith is justified or not.

[auden[3].source]
“Genius & Apostle,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 440–41; “K.’s Quest,” in *The Kafka Problem*, edited by Angel Flores (1946), pp. 47–52

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
Auden’s review of 1941, “The Wandering Jew,” and his essay of 1946, “K.’s Quest,” were read here only in the passages that a search of scanned copies brings up. The sentences this page reports from them were seen; the pages around them were not.

[caveats[1]]
The English verse is William and Charles Archer’s. Auden set his class the Everyman translation by R. Farquharson Sharp, which words these passages differently: its saying for men is to be true to oneself, and that is the form Auden quotes.

[caveats[2]]
What is said of Ibsen’s Norwegian rests on the first-edition text in the University of Oslo’s edition and on a dictionary of today’s Norwegian. The Norwegian is described, not printed, and matching Ibsen’s spellings of 1867 to today’s dictionary words is this page’s own step.

[caveats[3]]
The stresses marked are one reader’s hearing of a free measure of four beats. Several lines can fairly be heard another way, and Solveig’s answer can be given a fourth beat by leaning on a small word.

[caveats[4]]
Peer names no man for two of the nine coats. That the coat like a crown is the straw crown of the madhouse, and the black-streaked coat the trade in slaves, idols and missionaries, is inferred from what he says of each and from its place in the order.
