## The head of the page

[title]
No more Isolde

[where]
*Tristan und Isolde*, Act 2, Scenes 1 and 2, and the end of Act 3

[about] (under “The passage”)
Tristan is bringing the Irish princess Isolde by ship to Cornwall, to marry his uncle, King Mark. She orders a death potion for the two of them, and her maid Brangäne pours a love potion in its place. In Act 2 Isolde waits for Tristan at night in a garden, and they sing together until Mark surprises them at dawn. In Act 3 Tristan, wounded by the king’s man Melot, dies as Isolde reaches him, and she sings over his body.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Summing up de Rougemont in 1941, Auden calls the Tristan legend, which Wagner’s opera crowns, the chief form of a myth in which perfection comes only by death: for the lovers, love lasts in time only while something bars its consummation, and only death can make them one. Speaking for himself, he adds that their tie holds no possibilities, fixed as it is to a thing already done, the potion they drank.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
something predestined, for which they have no moral responsibility

[view.source]
“Eros and Agape,” *The Nation*, 28 June 1941, a review of Denis de Rougemont’s *Love in the Western World*; in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 138 and 140

### Tab “Verbs” (sentence)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Verbs

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Brangäne has blamed herself for Isolde’s shame. Isolde’s answer begins <i>Dein Werk?</i>, your work? In its last fourteen lines short brackets mark the verb of each clause, ten (one in two words), and long ones show whose they are. Two are Isolde’s, both past: she <b>nahm</b>, took, death’s work in hand, and <b>ward</b>, became, another’s own. Seven are <b>Frau Minne</b>’s, Lady Love’s, from past to what may come: has taken away, took, grasped, turns, ends, may choose, may lead. The tenth orders the maid to let her obey. Auden wrote in 1941 that the lovers’ tie holds no possibilities: it is fixed to a thing already done, the potion they drank.

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
<b>Werk</b>, work, and <b>Hand</b> end the two lines of Isolde’s own act. Six lines later the same two words end the lines in which Lady Love has the work in her hand. In between, <b>Hand</b> rhymes with <b>entwandt</b>, taken away, and <b>Pfand</b>, pledge. The dotted letters are alliteration, the device of Wagner’s <i>Ring</i> poems: one first sound joins <b>Minne</b>, Love, to <b>meiner Macht</b>, my power, which she takes the work from. Auden set this speech’s opening words over his pages on these lovers and their servants, and wrote there that the exchange of potions binds the two from outside, not by their own decisions.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The small glosses give the plain sense. <b>Minne</b> is the love of the medieval poets, who address it as a lady, <b>Frau Minne</b>. Isolde speaks of her in words of debt and lordship: taken <b>in Pfand</b>, as the pledge a creditor holds; become <b>zu eigen</b>, her own, a word once used of bondmen; owing <b>Gehorsam</b>, obedience, whose adjective arose to render the Latin for a Christian’s obedience to God. Earlier in the speech Love is a queen, with life and death her subjects. The myth, Auden wrote in 1948, could only arise where people are taught that to give oneself to God is a free and absolute commitment.

### Tab “Crossing” (figure)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Crossing

[plates[1].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
chiasmus

[plates[1].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
a crossing: two things are named, then named again in the opposite order; here pronoun and name, then name and pronoun

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
This German text gives Isolde the first three lines and Tristan the next three; in the vocal score he answers each as it ends. She calls him <b>Du</b>, you, by her own name, takes his name for <b>ich</b>, I, and drops her own: <b>nicht mehr</b>, no more. He does the same. Pronoun and name change places from line to line, a crossing, with no verb between them and no <i>und</i>, the “and” Isolde earlier calls a sweet little word. Each addresses the other as the singer’s self. Auden told the 1941 class, a student recalled, that romantic lovers are narcissists, each finding in the other the proof of being exceptional.

### Tab “Grammar” (sentence)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Grammar

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
In the last eight lines every word is marked by its kind: sixteen words, and not one verb that tells who does a thing or when. Four verbs stand as nouns, with capitals: naming, parting, knowing, catching fire. A verb in that form shows no person and no tense. The lines set phrase beside phrase, and <b>endlos ewig</b>, endlessly, eternally, is two words of like meaning. Auden noted in 1969 that in <i>Tristan</i> Wagner strings together phrases set side by side and words of like meaning as his music needs them, and in 1962 that the passion of the two has nothing before or after it but itself.

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
In this German text each name rhymes only with itself; Isolde’s lines and Tristan’s share one rhyme, <b>ich</b>, I. The libretto gives the last eight lines to both: four on one rhyme, then <b>ein-bewußt</b>, aware as one, rhymed with <b>Brust</b>, breast, and <b>Liebeslust</b>, love’s delight. ### Tab “Sentence” (sentence)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Sentence

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Isolde’s last questions hang on <b>Soll ich</b>, shall I. <i>Sollen</i> is the verb for what another’s will requires; in a question put to oneself it tells of indecision. This is her last verb that says who acts, and <b>mich</b>, myself, three lines on, is her last word for herself. After that line there is no pronoun and no question mark: three phrases beginning with <b>in</b>, two bare verbs, drown and sink, then <b>unbewußt</b>, unconscious, and a cry. Auden wrote in 1941 that when Eros, the will of every creature to realize itself, tries to live only in what might be, the end, if not sickness, is the physical world breaking in and destroying consciousness; his next sentence is about Tristan.

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Three rhyme words lose a consonant at each step: <b>Schwall</b>, a surge as of water, <b>Schall</b>, sound, <b>All</b>, the universe. The last two lines have the rhyme that closed the first lines given to both lovers in Act 1 and their duet in Act 2: there, you alone known to me; then, aware as one; here, <b>unbewußt</b>, unaware: the same word with <i>un-</i> before it, the German for unconscious. The last line is theirs too, less the word for love: <b>höchste Lust</b>, highest delight. Each one’s passion, Auden wrote, aims past the other at Nirvana, a unity before anything was many.

### Tab “Beat” (beat)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Beat

[plates[2].lenses[2].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
short lines of one or two beats: four syllables in the questions, then two lines of six, one of five, one of four and four of three

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The rule under each line is its measure; the dots are the spoken stresses. The four lines of questions have four syllables each, like twenty-one of the twenty-two lines before them. After the last question the measure changes: two lines of six syllables, the only ones in the song, each beginning off the beat; then one of five, one of four and four of three. The two long lines name the surge and the sound; three of the shortest are single words: drown, sink, unconscious. The line a composer writes for the voice, Auden held, is decided by the syllables of the words. With Chester Kallman he wrote that Wagner insisted on a text that matched his musical ideas exactly.

[plates[2].lenses[2].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)

### Tab “English” (compare)

[plates[2].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
English

[plates[2].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
Under each German line stands the English that H. and F. Corder fitted to the vocal score. It keeps each line’s syllable count and the scheme of the rhymes, and changes what is said. Isolde asks whether to breathe herself away; the English Isolde draws the breezes in. <b>Unbewußt</b>, unconscious, is left out: where it stood the singer is drinking in a kiss. Wagner’s last eight lines name no kiss and no person. Auden held that in song a listener hears syllables, not words, and that Wagner in English is intolerable, and would be even if the English were better verse than the German, since the new syllables do not fit the notes.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[0].say] (a numbered remark)
*Verhauchen* is an elevated, veiled word for breathing out one’s life: Isolde asks whether to breathe herself away. In the English she wins the breezes to her own breathing.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[1].say] (a numbered remark)
A *Schwall* is a great mass, above all of water, that breaks over one like a wave. The English has *breezes*.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[2].say] (a numbered remark)
*All* is the universe. In medieval German the word was said of God, and later of a beloved person. The English has a *whirlwind*.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[3].say] (a numbered remark)
*Unbewußt*, unconscious, has no word in the English. In its place the singer is drinking *in a kiss*.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[4].say] (a numbered remark)
*Lust* is desire and its pleasure in one word. *Bliss* keeps the pleasure, and *kiss* in the line before rhymes with it.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
The 1941 class, as the student Donald Pearce recalled it, was set *Romeo and Juliet*, the librettos of *Carmen* and *Tristan und Isolde*, and de Rougemont’s new book. Auden made two points, much on de Rougemont’s lines: that romantic lovers are narcissists, and that their love needs a barrier, at best one that cannot be overcome, so that only death can unite them. What such lovers love, he said, is death. Then he played the love duet on a phonograph.

[auden[0].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), pp. 137–39

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden’s review of 1941, summing up de Rougemont, sets the legend of Don Juan beside that of Tristan as its opposite, a legend that says yes to the flesh and no to the spirit and lives for the present moment alone. To Tristan time is an ill to be borne and to Don Juan an ill to be smashed, so Auden names the one a suicide and the other a murderer. Neither knows the one he loves as a person. Opposed to both alike, he writes, is what Christianity teaches about Agape; a page later he calls it Eros changed by grace.

[auden[1].source]
“Eros and Agape,” *The Nation*, 28 June 1941; in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 138–39

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Later in the same review Auden speaks for himself. Eros, for him the basic will of every creature to realize itself, must in man make the possible actual by deciding, and runs from that task in two ways. Hiding in the actual, it gets, unless madness comes, the opposite of what it sought: possibilities break in and destroy the actual, and Don Juan’s affairs stay possibilities never made real. Hiding in the possible, it finds, unless sickness comes, the physical world breaking in upon consciousness: Tristan’s tie to Isolde, fixed to a thing already done, holds no possibilities. He asks what can free man so that Eros dares to decide.

[auden[2].source]
“Eros and Agape,” *The Nation*, 28 June 1941; in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 139–40

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In a lecture of September 1940 Auden explains why Wagner is popular: ordinary people afraid for their jobs do feel held by a fate beyond their control and, like Tristan, are glad of night and its dreams after a dull day’s work. Suffering, he goes on, is what Wagner’s art could voice, and in Tristan and Isolde it is the pain of self-love. His best passages, he adds, are for one voice alone. In a review of June 1941 he writes that sex was a torment to Wagner, and that nothing is less a matter of biology, or more a matter of the mind, than Tristan and Isolde’s love.

[auden[3].source]
“Mimesis and Allegory,” a lecture given at Columbia University on 14 September 1940 and printed in *English Institute Annual 1940* (1941), and “Ambiguous Answers,” *The New Republic*, 23 June 1941, a review of Jacques Barzun’s *Darwin, Marx, Wagner*; in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 83 and 136

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In 1944, setting out Kierkegaard’s three ways of judging, Auden takes these lovers as his case. In betraying Mark the two feel guilt, yet thwarted desire would have hurt them more had they kept their vows. The aesthetic view wants all three to suffer, because that makes the situation interesting. The ethical view would have them keep faith and would gladly swap the love potion for a strong sedative, since it cares only how they act. The religious view has a blessing for them only to the degree that their guilt is truly suffered, and blesses Mark most, whose suffering is the more innocent and who, belatedly, forgives.

[auden[4].source]
“A Preface to Kierkegaard,” *The New Republic*, 15 May 1944, a review of *Either/Or*; in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 215–16

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In 1948 Auden calls the story one of two modern erotic myths with no parallel in Greek literature; the other is Don Juan. The potion, where the story has one, only makes the two see that they already love, and take the fact as fated and beyond recall. Each holds the other as the highest good, and still their bond is a torment: desire is only the sign of a longing to become one, which bodies forbid, so that the end they seek is to die embracing. The potion and the obstacle that cannot be removed guard them against change.

[auden[5].source]
“The Greeks and Us” (1948), in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), pp. 23–25

[auden[6].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In *The Dyer’s Hand* Auden calls it lucky for an understanding of the myth that Wagner made an opera of it: a stout tenor and soprano cannot be taken for a pair drawn together by good looks. The two are mythical figures, never met in life, and each is so indifferent to who the other is that, he says, they might as well have been paired by lot. Elsewhere in the book he counts them among the great operatic roles, each a state of passion and will, and holds that a music-drama needs a libretto with nothing sensible said in it, which is hard to bring off, though Wagner did.

[auden[6].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
their passion is not for each other but for something they hope to obtain by means of each other

[auden[6].source]
“Balaam and His Ass,” pp. 120–22, and “Notes on Music and Opera,” pp. 470 and 472, in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962)

[auden[7].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Reviewing a life of Wagner in 1969, Auden agrees with its author that Wagner’s affair with Mathilde Wesendonk came out of this opera and did not cause it. A close reader of its words and music, he says, becomes aware of a homosexual triangle of Mark, Melot and Tristan: when the lovers are caught, Mark’s long speech passes over Isolde and is spoken to Tristan alone. He also sets the opera against the theory Wagner published in 1851, which ruled out duets, repeated words, strict meters and even rhyme: in *Tristan* the duet comes back and the orchestra drowns the words.

[auden[7].source]
“The Greatest of the Monsters,” *The New Yorker*, 4 January 1969, a review of Robert Gutman’s *Richard Wagner: The Man, His Mind, and His Music*; in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), pp. 247–48 and 251–52

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
The view that opens the page is mostly Auden’s 1941 summary of de Rougemont’s thesis; its last sentence, and his essays of 1948 and 1962, are in his own voice. Apart from the duet and the words set over his essay, no remark of his on these passages was found.

[caveats[1]]
What Auden said in class in 1941 comes from an account that a student, Donald Pearce, wrote long after. It is not Auden’s own text, and what he said of the duet concerns the music, which this page does not show.

[caveats[2]]
In the vocal score Tristan answers each of Isolde’s lines with pronoun and name the other way around, so the two share her name at a line’s end, not the word for I. Wagner’s poem as printed in 1873 and the English libretto have four lines of names, for both.

[caveats[3]]
The stresses marked are one reader’s hearing of lines written to be sung. The first word of the first line and the word for myself in the fourth can fairly be given more weight or less.

[caveats[4]]
Auden’s pieces of 1940, 1941 and 1944 were read only in search results of a scanned copy, with page numbers taken from the nearest running heads.
