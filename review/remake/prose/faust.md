## The head of the page

[title]
The changing measures of Faust

[where]
*Faust*, Part I: the Prologue in Heaven, the first Study scene and Gretchen’s room

[about] (under “The passage”)
In Heaven three archangels end their hymn to the Lord’s creation, and Mephistopheles, the devil of the play, speaks next. In the second passage he has come into the study of the scholar Faust, first as a poodle and then as a traveling scholar, and Faust asks who he is. In the third, Gretchen, the girl Faust has courted with the devil’s help, sits alone at her spinning wheel. Faust has kissed her and has since gone off into the forest. The English printed here calls her Margaret.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden thought *Faust* rich in poetry and in wise sayings but not exciting as drama. He likened it to a variety show: the scenes are of interest one by one and have no real continuity. No translation, he wrote, can show how many styles of poetry Goethe commanded. Auden’s own age, he held, worships possibility, and the Devil of such an age comes as Mephisto, that is, as an actor, to whom no name belongs.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
Goethe’s amazing command of every style of poetry, from the coarse to the witty to the lyrical to the sublime

[view.source]
“Balaam and His Ass,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 115 and 118; “Italian Journey,” in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), p. 131

### Tab “Beat” (beat)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Beat

[plates[0].lenses[0].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
four beats to the line for the archangels; five beats, then six, for Mephistopheles; the line beginning off the beat throughout

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The first four lines end the archangels’ hymn: four beats a line, as in the twenty-four before. With Mephistopheles the measure changes. Here he has six lines of five beats, then two of six that break in the middle; the rest of his speech mixes four, five and six. The Lord answers him in the same mixed lines, which are the verse of most of the play. In his first line here Mephistopheles says he cannot make <b>hohe Worte</b>, high words: fine phrases like the archangels’, as an edition of 1892 explains them.

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The letters at the line ends mark the rhymes. The hymn rhymes by turns: <i>Stärke</i> with <i>Werke</i>, <i>mag</i> with <i>Tag</i>. Mephistopheles keeps that order while he excuses himself. When he comes to his subject, how men torment themselves, he changes to pairs and stays in pairs to the end of his speech. A rhyme that returns at once makes its two lines one unit, able to hold one complete idea. His second pair is one sentence, and it says what he thinks of man. Its rhyme, <b>Schlag</b>, stamp or kind, with <b>Tag</b>, is the rhyme the hymn ended on, and <b>Tag</b>, day, is the hymn’s last word.

[plates[0].lenses[1].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage, and the lines themselves. The rest of Mephistopheles’ speech, which keeps to pairs: [the Prologue in Heaven](#/read/faust/prologue:11). That a rhyme returning at once makes its two lines one unit: Paull F. Baum, *The Principles of English Versification*, chapter 4, on the short couplet. *Schlag*: the *Digitales Wörterbuch der deutschen Sprache*.

### Tab “Parody” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Parody

[plates[0].lenses[2].name] (the name of the figure)
parody

[plates[0].lenses[2].plain] (the figure in plain words)
the form and wording of a dignified piece closely imitated and made ridiculous by an unfitting subject

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The hymn ends on <b>wie am ersten Tag</b>, as on the first day. The Lord’s <b>hohen Werke</b>, high works, are <b>herrlich</b>, glorious, as on that day. The archangel Raphael’s stanza, the hymn’s first, ended on the same line, so the hymn says it twice. Mephistopheles takes the words up. He says he cannot make <b>hohe Worte</b>, high words, and that man is <b>wunderlich</b>, odd, as on the first day. He keeps the line’s end and changes the adjective. That is parody. An edition of 1892 notes that his line parodies the hymn’s. When he ends, the Lord asks whether he always comes only to accuse.

### Tab “Beat” (beat)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Beat

[plates[1].lenses[0].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
rhymed lines of six, five and four beats, the line beginning off the beat

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Faust’s line of three names has six beats and breaks in the middle. His question and Mephistopheles’ first words make a second such line, and the next line, the riddle, is a third. This is the alexandrine, the long line of French verse. Schiller wrote to Goethe in 1799 that it divides into two equal halves and rhymes in pairs, and that this puts everything in the French plays under the rule of opposition. Faust asks what the riddle means, and the answer begins in four beats: <b>der Geist, der stets verneint</b>, the spirit who always says no. Six of the answer’s seven lines have four beats.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[0].say] (a numbered remark)
Faust speaks. Six beats, and the line breaks after the first of the three names.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)
Faust’s question is half a line. Mephistopheles’ first words, just below, are the other half: six beats between the two speakers.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[2].say] (a numbered remark)
The riddle: six beats, breaking after *will*, wills.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[3].say] (a numbered remark)
Faust again, in five beats. *Gemeint*, meant, waits for its rhyme in the answer: *verneint*.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[4].say] (a numbered remark)
Mephistopheles answers in four beats.

[plates[1].lenses[0].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)

### Tab “Antithesis” (figure)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Antithesis

[plates[1].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)
antithesis

[plates[1].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)
two opposed things set side by side, often in phrases of the same build

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The riddle’s line has two halves built alike: <b>stets</b>, always, then a noun, then a verb. <b>Das Böse</b>, evil, stands against <b>das Gute</b>, good, and <b>will</b>, wills, against <b>schafft</b>, works. Contraries set close in matching words are an antithesis. It sets what the power wants against what it brings about; Faust calls it a riddle. In the Prologue the Lord used this verb of the companion he gives man, who must, as devil, <i>schaffen</i>. Auden quoted those lines in 1941 and wrote that the Devil brings about the loss of his own wager. Later, quoting the riddle, he could hardly see any good or evil that Mephisto does to Faust.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Faust has asked Mephistopheles his name and got no answer. He cites three names for devils. Two put Bible names into German: <b>Fliegengott</b>, god of flies, is Beelzebub, and <b>Verderber</b>, destroyer, is Apollyon. <b>Lügner</b>, liar, is what John’s Gospel calls the devil. The answer takes none of them. It gives <b>ein Teil</b>, a part, then a description: <b>der Geist, der stets verneint</b>, the spirit who always says no. In <i>New Year Letter</i> (1941) Auden names the Devil by this line in English, and his note gives the German. He later wrote that a spirit which rejects everything actual must hate creation; here Mephistopheles says that all that comes to be, <b>entsteht</b>, deserves to perish.

### Tab “Beat” (beat)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Beat

[plates[2].lenses[0].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
two beats to the line, in lines of three to six syllables

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Every line here can be said with two beats, and so can every line of the scene, forty in all. Goethe’s direction does not say that she sings; Schubert made a song of the scene in 1814. A line holds one thing of Faust’s: his walk, his figure, his smile, his eyes. One item runs over: <b>seiner Rede Zauberfluß</b>, the magic flow of his talk, is cut in two, and its second line is a single word. After <b>sein Kuß</b>, his kiss, the sentence stops without a verb, and the scene’s first stanza comes back word for word, as it did once before.

[plates[2].lenses[0].points[0].say] (a numbered remark)

[plates[2].lenses[0].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)

[plates[2].lenses[0].points[2].say] (a numbered remark)

[plates[2].lenses[0].points[3].say] (a numbered remark)

[plates[2].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Seven things of Faust’s are named in eight lines. Five begin with <b>sein</b>, his, at the head of the line; two have it after <i>und</i>, and. A word repeated at the head of line after line is anaphora; it makes the items a list, here without a verb. The list goes from his walk, figure, smile and eyes to his talk and then to touch: <b>Händedruck</b>, the pressure of his hand, and <b>Kuß</b>, the kiss. There it stops, and the returning stanza begins <b>Meine</b>, <b>Mein</b>, <b>Ich</b>: my, my, I.

### Tab “English” (compare)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
English

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Taylor keeps the two beats in every line and the rhyme of each stanza’s second and fourth lines. What he gives up is the repeated word. Goethe’s <i>sein</i> heads five of the eight lines; Taylor’s <b>His</b> heads two, and then <i>The</i>, <i>And</i>, <i>Of</i> and <i>In</i> take its place. <b>Size</b> stands for <i>Gestalt</i>, a person’s figure, and <b>the bliss</b> is not in the German; both appear to be there for the rhymes with <i>eyes</i> and <i>kiss</i>. A reader who knows <i>Faust</i> only in translation, Auden wrote, will probably think Goethe too intellectual and short of passion.

[plates[2].lenses[2].ties[0].say] (a numbered remark)
Taylor keeps *His* at the head of the first two lines.

[plates[2].lenses[2].ties[1].say] (a numbered remark)
From the third line on, *his* moves inside the line, and *The*, *And*, *Of* or *In* stands first.

[plates[2].lenses[2].ties[2].say] (a numbered remark)
*Gestalt* is a person’s figure or build. Taylor has *size*, which rhymes with *eyes*.

[plates[2].lenses[2].ties[3].say] (a numbered remark)
*Zauberfluß*: magic flow. Taylor turns the two lines round, the flow first and the talk after.

[plates[2].lenses[2].ties[4].say] (a numbered remark)
*Händedruck*: the pressure of a hand. Before it Taylor has *the bliss*, which is not in the German and gives him the rhyme for *kiss*.

[plates[2].lenses[2].ties[5].say] (a numbered remark)
The kiss ends the list in both.

The two versions as they stand, line under line. *Gestalt*: the *Digitales Wörterbuch der deutschen Sprache*. Auden: *Forewords and Afterwords*, p. 131.

[plates[2].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
Auden likened <i>Faust</i> to a variety show: one scene after another, each of interest in itself. The verse keeps changing too. Part I opens in ottava rima, the eight-line stanza of the Italian epics. The archangels’ hymn has four beats. Faust’s first speech opens in Knittelvers, the four-beat rhymed line that ruled German verse in the sixteenth century, when the first book about Faust was printed. Most of the play is in rhymed lines of changing length. Gretchen sings a song in three beats; Faust, alone in the forest, speaks unrhymed lines of five; Gretchen at her wheel has two beats; and one scene is prose.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In February 1941 Auden reviewed three books of Kafka and turned the review into a history of the hero who goes on a quest. With *Faust*, he wrote, something new comes into the story that *Pilgrim’s Progress* did not have: irony. He quoted the Lord’s lines about the companion who must work as a devil, and concluded that the Devil himself brings about the loss of his wager. Faust and Peer Gynt, he added, are exceptional for being morally worse than ordinary people; God and men favor them because they take what is arbitrary in themselves for what is necessary.

[auden[0].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
Faust is saved because he is never content, and he is never content because Mephistopheles will never let him rest

[auden[0].source]
“The Wandering Jew,” *The New Republic*, 10 February 1941; reprinted in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 110–13

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden could well imagine every actor wanting the part of Mephisto, who never fails to entertain, while whoever plays Faust goes unnoticed as long as Mephisto is on stage. He blamed the play’s faults as drama on the myth itself and held that Goethe did not lack talent as a dramatist. Faust’s story, as he read it, is about a man who will not be anyone and wants only to turn into someone else, and Mephisto makes visible what is possible and not actual. In an ideal production, he said, identical twins would play the two.

[auden[1].source]
“Balaam and His Ass,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 115–16

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Mephisto calls himself a part of the part that was once everything. Auden read this as a refusal of everything finite: a spirit that turns down whatever is actual can only hate creation. Of the riddle shown here he said he could hardly tell what good or what evil Mephisto does to Faust. With his help Faust may do others a great deal of harm, but nothing Faust does alters Faust. He lets Mephisto entertain him, and these shows change his character as little as watching the play changes its audience.

[auden[2].source]
“Balaam and His Ass,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 117

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden held that Faust escapes damnation and yet cannot sensibly be called saved: the angels who carry him to Heaven speak of him as still a pupa, and a creature in that state cannot be judged. In 1946 he found the ending of the play unsatisfying as art: the reader does not believe that Faust is saved, and neither, he thought, does Goethe. In 1948 he wrote that Marlowe, though less clever than Goethe, had the sounder sense, since his Faustus ends in hell.

[auden[3].source]
“Balaam and His Ass,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 117; “K.’s Quest,” in *The Kafka Problem*, edited by Angel Flores (1946), p. 49; “Squares and Oblongs,” in *Poets at Work* (1948), reprinted in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 349–50

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In class in December 1941, lecturing on *The Tempest*, Auden said that Prospero’s giving up of his magic for ordinary life belongs to a very old theme, and that Faust gives up something like it. Faust lets go of wide learning and power so that he can be of direct use to other people, in his case by joining in the building of dikes. The reference is to the end of Part II. This comes from notes a student made after the lecture.

[auden[4].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), p. 150

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In *New Year Letter*, the long poem he published in 1941, Auden names the Devil by Mephistopheles’ line, put into English, and a note gives the German and cites *Faust*, Part I, Scene 3. A little later the poem addresses Mephistopheles as one who has been cheated and only thinks he is having his own way. Another note quotes, in German, the Lord’s lines about the companion who must work as a devil.

[auden[5].source]
*New Year Letter* (London: Faber, 1941), lines 384 and 400, with the notes to lines 384 and 395

[auden[6].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Writing of the Romantic hero and his lack of any lasting interest in other people, Auden set Faust beside Ibsen’s Peer Gynt. Each comes across a great many people, and what matters about both, he said, is that they go on and the others stay where they were.

[auden[6].source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), p. 107

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
Auden names no passages for the styles he lists, and does not point to the names and costumes of Mephistopheles; his variety show is a remark about the scenes. The passages and these matchings are this page’s choice, as is setting Schiller’s remark on French plays beside the riddle.

[caveats[1]]
The stresses marked are one reader’s hearing of the German. Another reader could fairly mark some syllables otherwise, above all the first words of lines, and some of Gretchen’s lines can be heard with three beats.

[caveats[2]]
An editor of 1892 reads the riddle as the devil’s own opinion: in doing what men call bad he does what he thinks good, and admits no higher power that turns it to good. He finds no special effect meant in the six-beat lines of the Prelude at the Theatre.

[caveats[3]]
Auden’s article of 1941, his notes to *New Year Letter* and his essays of 1946 and 1948 were read here only in snippets of scanned copies. What he said in class comes from notes a student, Donald Pearce, made after the lectures.

[caveats[4]]
The course’s reading list names the Everyman edition, which is Albert Latham’s translation, and “Omit” is written by hand against the title. The English here is Bayard Taylor’s. The German is a modern-spelling text and not the spelling of 1808.
