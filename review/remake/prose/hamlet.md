## The head of the page

[title]
Three speeches of Hamlet

[where]
*Hamlet*, Act 1, Scene 2; Act 3, Scene 1; Act 5, Scene 2

[about] (under “The passage”)
Hamlet, prince of Denmark, has come home from university after his father’s death and found his mother married to his uncle, who is now king. In the first passage he answers her before the court. His father’s ghost then tells him that the uncle was the murderer, and in the second passage, two acts later, the uncle still reigns and Hamlet is talking to himself. In the third, the King has rigged a fencing match to kill him, and his friend Horatio has just said he will put it off.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden wrote that Hamlet has no faith in God or in himself, so he can say who he is only through other people: the man whose uncle murdered his father and is married to his mother. He wishes to be what the hero of a Greek tragedy is, someone made by his situation. That is why he cannot act: he can only “act,” which Auden glosses as playing at possibilities.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
Hence his inability to act, for he can only “act,” i.e., play at possibilities.

[view.source]
“Genius & Apostle,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 451–52

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
After the first line, one sentence runs for six lines and a half. It opens <b>’Tis not</b>, so a denial is coming. Then it lists, a line to each, the cloak, the black suits, the sigh, the tears, the face, then all the rest, and four times <b>nor</b> brings in the next. What they cannot do comes only in the last five words: <b>That can denote me truly</b>. A verb kept to the end holds the sense open until every sign of mourning has been named. Two lines later Hamlet says what he does have and gives it no noun: <b>that within</b>.

### Tab “Naming” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Naming

[plates[0].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)

[plates[0].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)
the long way round: a description put in the place of a name

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Three lines are cut to one pattern: an adjective, a noun, then the breath, the eye or the face it belongs to. Hamlet does not say sigh, tears or sad face. He says <b>suspiration</b>, a word found in Shakespeare only here; a <b>river in the eye</b>; and the <b>haviour</b>, the bearing, of the <b>visage</b>. Rhetoric calls this periphrasis, the long way round. Each phrase names a sign by where it shows. Three lines on, the speech says what the signs are: <i>actions that a man might play</i>. In Act 2 Hamlet describes a player in tears by much the same signs: his <i>visage</i>, <i>tears in his eyes</i>, <i>a broken voice</i>.

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The Queen has asked why, if death is common, it <i>seems</i> so particular with Hamlet. The verb could mean <i>appears</i>; he takes it as <i>is so in appearance only</i>. Three of his words for outward grief are also playhouse words. <b>Actions</b> are the gestures that go with speech; in Act 3 he tells the players to <i>suit the action to the word</i>. To <b>play</b> is to act a part. A <b>show</b> is a display, and also a stage spectacle, like the players’ dumb show, a scene acted without words. <b>Trappings</b> and <b>suits</b> are things put on. Auden’s account of Hamlet turns on a playhouse word with two senses: he cannot act, he can only “act.”

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[0].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[0].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
Hamlet speaks blank verse, the unrhymed five-beat line, and for nine lines the line-ends do not rhyme. The last two do: <b>show</b>, <b>woe</b>. The quick return of a rhyme makes two lines felt as one closed unit. On Shakespeare’s stage a rhymed pair often marked a scene’s end: ten of this play’s twenty scenes end on one. The players’ play in Act 3 rhymes in pairs. Here a rhymed pair closes a speech in mid-scene, and the King answers. <i>Seem</i> comes three times, <i>show</i> twice, last as the rhyme. Hamlet has set himself apart from <i>actions that a man might play</i>. Auden, lecturing on the play in 1947, said that Hamlet is an actor and acts theatrically.

### Tab “Beat” (beat)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)

[plates[1].lenses[0].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
ten syllables and five beats, the line beginning off the beat, without rhyme; some lines run one unstressed syllable past the last beat

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The play’s verse line has ten syllables, every second one a beat, the last included. Five of the ten lines, the first four and the eighth, add an unstressed eleventh: a weak ending, which tends to give the line a falling rhythm, from stressed to unstressed. The question fills four such lines and stops inside the fifth, on another unstressed syllable, <i>them</i>. The sixth, eighth, ninth and tenth also stop inside the line. Auden said in 1947 that in <i>Hamlet</i> Shakespeare tries out the stop inside the line to make a voice between passion and prose. At <b>ay</b> a stress stands between two beats; stresses side by side slow the voice before <i>there’s the rub</i>.

### Tab “Verbs” (sentence)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Verbs

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Twelve times in ten lines <i>to</i> is followed by a verb: <b>to be</b>, <b>to die</b>, <b>to sleep</b>. This is the infinitive, the form tied to no person: it names an act without its doer. Only six verbs have a subject: five forms of <i>is</i>, and <b>we end</b>. In thirty-three lines to himself Hamlet never says <i>I</i>, <i>me</i> or <i>my</i>; his six other long speeches to himself each say them at least seven times. In his 1947 lecture Auden said that this speech can be lifted out of the play and out of the character. He wrote that Hamlet cannot act, only play at possibilities. The thirty-three lines end on <i>lose the name of action</i>.

### Tab “Chain” (figure)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Chain

[plates[1].lenses[2].name] (the name of the figure)
anadiplosis

[plates[1].lenses[2].plain] (the figure in plain words)
a doubling back: the words that end one clause begin the next

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Twice Hamlet says <b>to die, to sleep</b>. The first time he adds <i>No more</i>, and the sentence goes on to what a sleep would end. The second time the line stops on <i>sleep</i>, and its last two words begin the next line. Rhetoric calls this anadiplosis: what ends one clause begins the next. The repeated word becomes the start of a further step. He has called death a sleep, and from sleep the next step is dreaming, <b>perchance to dream</b>. He says at once what that is: <b>the rub</b>. The next lines give the reason: the dreams that may come <i>must give us pause</i>.

[plates[1].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
The question is asked in the words of a fight: <i>slings</i>, <i>arrows</i>, <i>take arms</i>, <i>opposing</i>. <b>Shocks</b> belongs with them: a shock was a violent collision, as of armies. <b>Outrageous</b> meant violent; it comes from the Latin for <i>beyond</i> and has no root in <i>rage</i>. Dying gets other words: <i>sleep</i>, <i>end</i>, and <b>consummation</b>, from the Latin for summing up and finishing. It is to be wished for <b>devoutly</b>, with devotion. The word that stops the wish is a term from the game of bowls, as Auden told his class in 1947. A <b>rub</b> is whatever slows the bowl or turns it from its course. The speech turns there, from the wish to the dread of what may come after death.

### Tab “Crossing” (figure)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Crossing

[plates[2].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
antimetabole

[plates[2].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
the same words said again in the opposite order

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Three clauses begin with <i>if</i>. The first sets <b>now</b> against <b>not to come</b>. The second says the same two things in the opposite order: <b>not to come</b>, then <b>now</b>. The third changes one term, <i>not now</i>, and ends <b>it will come</b>. Whichever case holds, it comes. The verb of the second passage, <i>be</i>, is here again, and now it has a time and a subject, <i>it</i>, which is never named. Auden wrote of the character as a whole that Hamlet wishes to be what the hero of a Greek tragedy is, someone made by his situation. In these clauses what is coming is the subject of every verb; the next sentence has a noun for its subject, <b>readiness</b>.

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Horatio has told Hamlet to obey his misgiving. <b>Augury</b> was the Roman art of telling what is to come from signs, most often the flight of birds. Hamlet <b>defies</b> it: rejects it. His next sentence names a bird. Calvin’s chapter on it infers from the sparrow that the flight of birds is ruled by God’s counsel. Hamlet refuses the art that reads birds for warnings, and goes from the bird whose fall is in God’s care to the three ifs.

In the first four acts Hamlet has seven long speeches to himself, of twelve to fifty-eight lines, all in verse. Auden wrote that the audience hears such a speech as spoken to them. By the count drawn here, about half of what he says to others is verse.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In 1941, the year of the Michigan course, Auden talked about Shakespeare one evening at home with Charles Miller, a student in the class who shared his house. A director casting Hamlet, he said, might as well go into the street and take whoever comes first, because the part needs no actor. It is enough to say the speeches aloud: in them Hamlet is instructing himself, and arguing with himself, about how to act the parts he has decided to play.

[auden[0].source]
Charles H. Miller, *Auden: An American Friendship* (1983), p. 61

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden wrote that a whole personality, with what it discloses and what it hides, can hardly be shown through one character. The soliloquy tries to get round this, but it is a convention: it puts what is in fact a dialogue into the shape of one person’s speech. In Hamlet’s soliloquies the voice that is supposed to speak to itself is heard as speaking to the audience, who then suspect that he lets them hear only what he judges fit for them and keeps back the rest.

[auden[1].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
a single voice which is supposed to be addressed to himself but, in fact, is heard as addressed to us

[auden[1].source]
“Balaam and His Ass,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 110

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In *The Enchafèd Flood* Auden says that Shakespeare had a new idea of revenge and made of the Hamlet he inherited the earliest hero of the romantic kind. It is his situation that makes this Hamlet a hero: his mother’s adultery with the uncle who killed his father. Until then he was an ordinary, agreeable young man. So he does not simply avenge his father and have done with it. In secret he holds the situation dear and cannot bring himself to end it, because he would not know who he was without it.

[auden[2].source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), p. 110

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In his 1947 lecture on the play Auden cited T. S. Eliot’s verdict that it fails as a work of art, and said that Hamlet, the one character who does not act, is not well fitted into it. He doubted that anyone had played the part without looking ridiculous. It should be given, he said, to someone brought in from the street, with professionals in every other part, because Hamlet is an actor, and nobody can act himself. He called Hamlet bored at bottom, and said that is why he behaves theatrically.

[auden[3].source]
*Lectures on Shakespeare*, reconstructed and edited by Arthur Kirsch (2000), pp. 159 and 164

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In the same lecture Auden said that the soliloquies of *Hamlet*, like those of other plays Shakespeare wrote in those years, can be detached from the character and from the play; in his earlier and later plays they are more closely worked in. The speech of the second passage was his clear example. He also said that Hamlet is given verse when he is alone, in the soliloquies, and with others when passion is high, as with his mother, and that with other people he otherwise mostly speaks prose.

[auden[4].source]
*Lectures on Shakespeare*, reconstructed and edited by Arthur Kirsch (2000), pp. 159–60

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Also in that lecture Auden said that in *Hamlet* Shakespeare tries out the caesura, the stop inside the verse line, to get a voice between the passionate and the prosaic. He said too that Shakespeare’s paired adjectives change in this play: an earlier pair says one thing twice, and the pairs in *Hamlet* join an abstract word to a concrete one.

[auden[5].source]
*Lectures on Shakespeare*, reconstructed and edited by Arthur Kirsch (2000), p. 160

[auden[6].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In the spring of 1947 the Saturday class that went with Auden’s New York lectures on Shakespeare turned to language: close comment on single words and phrases of two plays, *Hamlet* and *The Tempest*. The final examination that term, as a student’s journal reports it, asked what single words in the two plays mean and had the students punctuate one of Hamlet’s prose speeches.

[auden[6].source]
*Lectures on Shakespeare*, reconstructed and edited by Arthur Kirsch (2000), Appendix I, pp. 321–40, and Appendix II, p. 341

[auden[7].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Alan Ansen’s notes of that class keep what Auden said on the speech of the second passage: *rub* is a bowler’s term, for a ball turned aside when it grazes another; editors have proposed changes to *sea of troubles*; and *to say*, two lines on, does not follow grammatically. The notes on *Hamlet* open with a general remark: Hamlet’s good relations with people all go bad, except the one with Horatio.

[auden[7].source]
*Lectures on Shakespeare*, reconstructed and edited by Arthur Kirsch (2000), Appendix I, pp. 324 and 327

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
Auden’s 1947 lecture on *Hamlet* is not his own text. Arthur Kirsch rebuilt it from notes made by people who heard it. It was read here only in snippets of scanned copies of the book, and so were the notes of his 1947 class.

[caveats[1]]
The remark of 1941 comes from a memoir that Charles Miller printed in 1983, forty-two years later. The memoir was read here only in snippets.

[caveats[2]]
The stresses marked on the second passage are one reader’s hearing of the lines. Its first line can take a stress on *that* or on *is*, and a few other syllables could fairly be marked otherwise. The pauses marked follow the punctuation of the text printed here.

[caveats[3]]
The text printed here reads *shows of grief* in the first passage, where the early editions called quartos read *shapes*. The counts of verse, prose and rhymed scene-endings follow this text, which sets a few short prose lines as verse.

[caveats[4]]
Auden’s sentence that Hamlet has no faith in God is about the character as a whole. No remark of his was found on the first or the third passage, and in the third Hamlet speaks of providence.
