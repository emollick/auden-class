## The head of the page

[title]
The heart and the reason

[where]
*Pensées*, fragments 277 and 278, 347 and 348, and 233

[about] (under “The passage”)
Pascal died in 1662 and left notes, many of them for a defense of Christianity; they were printed eight years later as his *Pensées*, or thoughts. W. F. Trotter’s English, printed here, follows an edition that numbers over nine hundred notes and sorts them by subject into fourteen sections; that order is an editor’s, not Pascal’s. Pascal writes in his own voice, at times to an unbeliever addressed as *you*. The third passage answers a speaker who has just said the right thing is not to bet at all.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
In 1939 Auden called Pascal by far the noblest and most tempting of the dualists, who in his account split what exists in two, good and evil; Pascal’s kind sets a supernatural world against this one. Auden saw a divided man, corrupt in heart and sound in intellect, and held that his reason saved him, not the heart he credited: reason told him how corrupt his heart was.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
Like most of us he exalted the faculty he lacked over the faculty he possessed, the heart over the reason

[view.source]
*The Prolific and the Devourer* (written 1939; Ecco Press, 1994), pp. 31 and 46–48

### Tab “Reasons” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Reasons

[plates[0].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
antanaclasis

[plates[0].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
the same word said again with a different meaning

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The first sentence uses one word twice, <b>reasons</b> and then <b>reason</b>, in two senses. The plural means grounds or motives; the singular names the power by which a person knows and judges, and it returns three times below. Littré’s French dictionary lists the two senses apart and quotes this sentence under motive. The play on the word is Pascal’s: <i>raisons</i>, <i>raison</i>. It gives the heart reasons in the very word that names the power said not to know them. In the manuscript the clause about reason is an addition above the line, and the play came with it. Auden’s judgment was that Pascal set the heart above the reason.

### Tab “French” (word)

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Trotter, the translator, writes <b>feel</b>, <b>experiences</b> and <b>felt</b>. Behind <b>feel</b> is <i>sait</i>, knows. Behind <b>experiences</b> is <i>sent</i>, from <i>sentir</i>, a verb that means to feel and also to perceive. Behind <b>felt</b> is <i>sensible</i>, able to be perceived, and Littré’s dictionary quotes this phrase under that sense. In fragment 282 Pascal uses <i>sentir</i> for the heart’s knowledge of first principles, where Trotter writes <i>intuitive knowledge</i>, and says that truth is known by the heart as well as by the reason. So the heart here knows as well as loves. Auden’s sentence on Pascal calls both heart and reason faculties.

### Tab “Against” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)

[plates[0].lenses[2].name] (the name of the figure)

[plates[0].lenses[2].plain] (the figure in plain words)

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The second fragment, 278, says one thing twice, each time by a contrast: the <b>heart</b>, and not <b>the reason</b>. The second statement is a definition, <b>This, then, is faith</b>, and like the first it ends on the reason that is shut out. Between the two, <b>heart</b> and <b>God</b> change places. In the French edition used here the fragment is one sentence, and <i>then</i> is Trotter’s word. The next fragment says what is denied: faith is God’s gift and not a gift of reasoning. Auden’s verdict uses the same two nouns the other way round: Pascal believed his heart had saved him, and Auden gives the credit to his reason.

[plates[1].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)

[plates[1].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
<b>Man</b> is called <b>a reed</b>, and the next six words say why: nothing in nature is weaker. Calling one thing by another’s name is metaphor. After the semicolon the reed returns with one word added, <b>thinking</b>, so one image holds both things the paragraph argues: first the weakness, then the thought. The editor of the French text finds a memory of Montaigne in the <b>vapour</b> and the <b>drop of water</b>, and quotes him on man overthrown by a contrary wind or a morning mist. A note of Pascal’s headed <i>Montaigne</i> begins by calling his faults great. Auden wrote that Pascal was to him what Montaigne had been to Pascal.

### Tab “Verbs” (sentence)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Verbs

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The verbs of force belong to the universe, or to a <b>vapour</b> and a <b>drop of water</b>: <b>arm</b>, <b>crush</b>, <b>kill</b>, <b>killed</b>. Four times their object is <b>him</b>. Man’s own verbs are few: he is, he dies, he <b>knows</b>. The last clause gives the universe the same verb with <b>nothing</b> after it, and that verb is the whole reason given for <b>more noble</b>, which the editor of the French text glosses as of a higher kind. The next paragraph concludes with <b>then</b>: all dignity is in thought. That is Pascal’s word; Auden’s word for the faculty Pascal possessed is reason, and he held that it, not the heart, saved him.

### Tab “Crossing” (figure)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Crossing

[plates[1].lenses[2].name] (the name of the figure)
chiasmus

[plates[1].lenses[2].plain] (the figure in plain words)
a crossing: two things are named, then named again in the opposite order

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The last sentence has two halves, each opening with <i>by</i>. In the first <b>the universe</b> acts on <b>me</b>; in the second <b>I</b> act on <b>the world</b>. The two come back in crossed order, which is chiasmus. In Pascal’s French one verb serves both halves, <i>comprend</i> and <i>comprends</i>. It means to contain and to understand, and the French editor notes that Pascal sets the literal sense against the figurative: what holds the speaker in space, he holds in thought. The heart’s reasons turn on one word in the same way. Trotter needs two verbs, <b>encompasses</b> and <b>comprehend</b>. In the manuscript <b>By space</b> is an addition, which gave the halves matching openings.

## Passage 3: Fragment 233: the wager  (plates[2], id "wager", from pascal/s03)

Fragment 233: the wager

### Tab “Voice” (sentence)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Voice

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The passage is one side of a conversation: the other speaker has refused to bet. Three statements rule that out: <b>you must wager</b>, <b>not optional</b>, <b>You are embarked</b>. Then come two questions, four invitations with <b>let us</b>, and one point called settled, in a sentence Pascal added above the line. <i>You</i> or <i>your</i> comes eighteen times in Trotter’s English and fifteen in the French, <i>I</i> not once, so the choosing stays with the listener. One of Pascal’s notes is headed <i>Order by dialogues</i>. Auden granted such accounts of choice their interest as drama and asked if they are true: his decisive choices hardly seemed serious when he made them.

### Tab “Pairs” (figure)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Pairs

[plates[2].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)
parallelism

[plates[2].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)
clauses of the same build, set side by side

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Three clauses are built alike, which is parallelism: <b>two things to lose</b>, <b>two things to stake</b>, <b>two things to shun</b>. They head four pairs, each divided the same way. One side reads <b>the true</b>, <b>reason</b>, <b>knowledge</b>, <b>error</b>. The other reads <b>the good</b>, <b>will</b>, <b>happiness</b>, <b>misery</b>. The division lets the argument settle reason’s side in one sentence and turn to happiness. In the manuscript the first and last pairs are additions above the line. Auden’s objection to the word <i>wager</i> is that it makes faith sound like prudent calculation. Later in the paragraph Pascal’s word for the player who will not stake one life to win three is <i>imprudent</i>.

### Tab “Scales” (figure)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Scales

[plates[2].lenses[2].name] (the name of the figure)
antithesis

[plates[2].lenses[2].plain] (the figure in plain words)
two opposed things set side by side, often in phrases of the same build

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Pascal’s verb is to weigh: <b>weigh the gain and the loss</b>. Two sentences on, two halves of six words differ only in <b>gain</b> and <b>lose</b>, <b>all</b> and <b>nothing</b>. Setting opposites in matching phrases is antithesis. It makes the case for one bet, that God is: everything to gain, nothing to lose. In the manuscript a struck-out start went straight to the case of losing, and the words for weighing and <b>If you gain, you</b> were added above the line. Auden objected in 1939 that to let another world sway life in this one is in effect to suppose two Gods, and the prudent man will bet on the second.

[plates[2].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
The words belong to a voyage and a game. <b>Embarked</b> is <i>embarqué</i>, said of a man at sea who cannot leave the ship. Behind <b>in wagering</b> is <i>en prenant croix</i>, taking the side of the coin that Trotter has called heads. The reckoning goes by a rule the next fragment names, <i>the doctrine of chance</i>. ## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In an essay on Kierkegaard, Auden says that however good the argument, faith is still a choice, and nobody can make it for someone else. He finds Pascal’s word and Kierkegaard’s both inadequate, and allows each some value. The wager reminds people that everywhere else in life they act on faith all the time, and willingly, so that religion cannot fairly be expected to differ.

[auden[0].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
Pascal’s ‘wager’ and Kierkegaard’s ‘leap’ are neither of them quite adequate descriptions, for the one suggests prudent calculation and the other perverse arbitrariness.

[auden[0].source]
“Søren Kierkegaard,” in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), p. 178

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In 1939 his objection went further. Whether a supernatural world exists he leaves open. But to hold with Pascal that it ought to change how life is lived here is, he wrote, to split the chain of causes in two and in effect to set up two Gods, one ruling each world. The second God’s punishments never end, so prudence backs him against the first. Auden calls this the sin against the Holy Ghost, the denial that truth is one.

[auden[1].source]
*The Prolific and the Devourer* (written 1939; Ecco Press, 1994), pp. 46–47

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Among his notes on existentialism Auden grants that accounts of choice such as Pascal’s wager and Kierkegaard’s leap have their interest as drama, and asks if they hold true. Of the few decisive choices in his own life he says that he had little idea, when he made them, of how much hung on them. He is thankful for it: had he known the danger, he would not have taken the step.

[auden[2].source]
“Hic et Ille,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 103

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In the Michigan class of 1941 Auden used Pascal to describe the two chief characters of *Othello*. According to a student’s notes, he said that Othello is exactly one of Pascal’s types, a man who meets life’s problems with nothing but feeling and a handful of moral rules. Iago has the mind Pascal calls geometrical: he settles everything by reason, and reasons faultlessly, though he neither sees the principles nor cares about them. The *Pensées* as printed here open with this contrast, of the mathematical mind with the intuitive.

[auden[3].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), pp. 141 and 143

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In *New Year Letter*, the long poem Auden published in 1941, a few months before the course, the Devil purrs agreement when people contradict one of his lies too emphatically. He wonders aloud if the serpent in Eden was not, very nearly, the spirit of geometry, calls it foolish to look for salvation in logic, and asks when people will throw intelligence away and live by an order of the heart. Auden’s note to the first of these lines quotes Pascal in French: the mind has its order, by principle and demonstration, and the heart has another. Two more of his notes to the poem quote the *Pensées*.

[auden[4].source]
*New Year Letter* (London: Faber, 1941), lines 483–527 and Auden’s note to line 497

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Reviewing Kierkegaard’s journals, Auden puts Pascal with Nietzsche, Simone Weil and Kierkegaard among the writers who are hard to judge fairly. A first reading overwhelms: no one has spoken in such a voice or seen so sharply. On rereading, doubts set in. Each of them presses one side of the truth so hard that the rest is lost, and the reader’s first enthusiasm can turn into a dislike just as excessive. The more one reads such writers, he adds, the plainer it is that their life of feeling is badly out of order.

[auden[5].source]
“A Knight of Doleful Countenance,” in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), pp. 182–83

[auden[6].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Edward Mendelson, in his preface to *The Prolific and the Devourer*, writes that Pascal was one of its models: Auden was reading him by June 1939, had written a poem on him by August, and that month called the book his *pensées*. Its second part opens by saying that, like Pascal, Auden starts from an observation and an assumption: that people are unhappy more often than happy, and that this is unnatural. He adds that Pascal overstated the unhappiness. The poem of that summer is “Pascal.”

[auden[6].source]
*The Prolific and the Devourer* (written 1939; Ecco Press, 1994), p. 29, and Edward Mendelson’s preface to it, p. viii; “Pascal,” in *The Collected Poetry of W. H. Auden* (New York: Random House, 1945), pp. 86–89

[auden[7].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In an essay of 1956 Auden wrote that among the writers on theology he knew at school and university, only Pascal was readable and shook his self-satisfaction. Looking back, he found something fake in the writing, a romantic taste for unhappiness that may have been what drew him; but Pascal at least did not sound like a clergyman, and would not let him put God out of mind when he wanted to. In talk that Alan Ansen wrote down in February 1947, Auden names the greatest aphorists, Pascal first, counts him among the untypical French writers he likes, and says he has no liking for Montaigne.

[auden[7].source]
Essay in *Modern Canterbury Pilgrims*, edited by the Dean of New York (James A. Pike) (London: A. R. Mowbray, 1956), pp. 36–37; Alan Ansen, *The Table Talk of W. H. Auden* (London: Faber, 1991), p. 25

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
*The Prolific and the Devourer*, the book that gives this page its point of view, was read only in snippets of a scanned copy, and so were *New Year Letter*, the essay of 1956 and Ansen’s *Table Talk*. Wording and page numbers were not seen on a full page.

[caveats[1]]
What Auden said in class in 1941 is known from notes that a student, Donald Pearce, wrote down after each lecture, and his talk of 1947 from notes kept by Alan Ansen. Neither is Auden’s own text.

[caveats[2]]
The English is W. F. Trotter’s, and its sentence breaks and some of its words (*feel*, *then*, *atom*, *the world*, and three of the eighteen uses of *you*) are his. In fragment 278 the French verb *sentir* can be taken simply as to feel.

[caveats[3]]
Pascal’s additions above the line and the order in which he filled one manuscript page are taken from an editor’s notes and a modern transcription, not the manuscript. The order is the modern editors’ judgment, and they count the two fragments as separate notes, not part of the wager.

[caveats[4]]
The printed order and the numbers of the fragments are an editor’s. Fragments 347 and 348 stand on different pages of the manuscript, and modern editors think 277 was written after 278. The paragraph on dignity and thought is known only from early copies of Pascal’s papers.
