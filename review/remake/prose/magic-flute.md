## The head of the page

[title]
The trials

[where]
*The Magic Flute*, Act 2: the questions before the trials, the Queen of the Night’s aria, and the end of the opera

[about] (under “The passage”)
Tamino, a prince, and Papageno, a bird-catcher, are at the priest Sarastro’s temple, where Tamino hopes to win Pamina, daughter of the Queen of the Night. In the first passage, which is spoken, two priests question them before their trials; Tamino has just said he seeks friendship and love. In the second the Queen has shown Pamina a dagger, told her to kill Sarastro and forbidden her to answer. In the third Papageno and his bride sing of the children they want, and a chorus of priests ends the opera.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden calls the opera overoptimistic. Tamino stands for the spiritual and gains wisdom and Pamina because he has the courage to stake his life in the trials. Papageno stands for the natural and is given Papagena because he has the humility to refuse the risk. The opera holds, he says, that nature and spirit can live together in man if each keeps to itself and the natural may refuse interference.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
he has had the humility to refuse to risk his life even if the refusal will mean that he must remain single

[view.source]
“Balaam and His Ass,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 134–35

### Tab “Answers” (compare)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Answers

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Matching numbers mark where the second exchange answers the first. The priests set one test in the same words: <b>erkämpfen</b>, to win by fighting, and <b>Tod</b>, death, as the risk. Here Tamino is asked three questions and answers each in one word. Papageno gives his reasons, asks what the trial is, and at the word for death says he stays single. Auden and Chester Kallman, who put the opera into English, write that this exchange is Papageno’s trial, handled so lightly in the original that audiences can miss it. He passes, they say: his last answer shows his humility, and for it he is rewarded. Tamino, Auden says, is rewarded for the courage to stake his life.

### Tab “Wishes” (sentence)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Wishes

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Tamino’s answer to the warning is one sentence in two matched halves. Each names a thing he wants and calls it a prize: wisdom’s teaching is to be his <b>Sieg</b>, victory, and Pamina his <b>Lohn</b>, reward. The verb <b>sey</b>, be, is said once for both, a figure called zeugma. Auden says Tamino faces the ordeal knowing what lies beyond it: wisdom and the princess. Papageno begins with two refusals and puts his wish last, under an <i>if</i>. The priest answers <b>nie</b>, never, with a condition of his own, which Auden and Kallman call a threat. The opera does not carry it out: Papageno promises only silence and breaks it, never enters fire or water, and is given Papagena.

### Tab “Beat” (beat)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Beat

[plates[1].lenses[0].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
five beats, the line beginning off the beat, in lines of eleven and ten syllables by turns

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The German has five beats to the line. Without rhyme, the line of five beats is the classic line of German verse drama since Lessing’s <i>Nathan der Weise</i> of 1779. Most of this libretto’s verse has four beats. The Queen has ten lines of five, these and two in Act 1; a chorus of priests, two armed men and three boys sing all the others. Auden and Chester Kallman, writing on the translation of opera, hold that the Queen, a supernatural being, cannot speak like a courtesan such as Verdi’s Violetta. The last line, to the gods, is broken twice and can be heard to begin on two stresses.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[0].say] (a numbered remark)
This line begins on a stressed word, *Tod*, death, where the measure has no beat.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)
As Mozart set them, the fifth and sixth lines become four short ones: the phrase meaning for ever is sung three times, once each with the words for cast off, forsaken and shattered. Auden and Kallman remark of Mozart in general that when a text is too short for his music he has its lines sung again.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[2].say] (a numbered remark)
The line turns from Pamina to the gods. Two dashes break it, and its first word, a command, can be heard as a stress before the beat. The libretto of 1791 prints *Rache*, vengeance, and *Götter*, gods, apart; as Mozart set them they are one word, *Rachegötter*, gods of vengeance.

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Each set of four lines rhymes by turns, on two syllables in the odd lines and on one in the even, the usual way with this line in German. The two-syllable rhymes pair the Queen’s <b>Herzen</b>, heart, with Sarastro’s <b>Todesschmerzen</b>, pains of death, and the daughter <b>verlassen</b>, forsaken, with Sarastro <b>erblassen</b>, turning pale, a noble word for dying. The first set closes on <b>nimmermehr</b>, nevermore, and the second on <b>Schwur</b>, oath, which rhymes with <b>Natur</b>: she swears that nature’s bonds shall be shattered if Sarastro does not die by Pamina’s hand.

### Tab “Threat” (figure)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Threat

[plates[1].lenses[2].name] (the name of the figure)
chiasmus

[plates[1].lenses[2].plain] (the figure in plain words)
a crossing: two things are named, then named again in the opposite order; here condition and penalty, then penalty and condition

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Five lines hold one threat, said twice in opposite order. The third line sets the condition, that Sarastro die at Pamina’s hand, and the fourth the penalty: she is a daughter no more. The fifth and sixth say the penalty again, and the seventh returns to the condition in four of the third line’s words, <i>nicht durch dich Sarastro</i>, not through you Sarastro. Condition, penalty, penalty, condition: a chiasmus, which shuts the penalty between two statements of the condition. Afterward Pamina asks the gods what to do; later she says her mother’s curse pursues her. Auden and Kallman list that curse among the sufferings she must learn to bear.

### Tab “English” (compare)

[plates[1].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
English

[plates[1].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
The English libretto names no translator. It keeps the five beats, two lines a syllable short, and at first the German word order: <i>of hell the vengeance</i>. It has none of the two-syllable rhymes: its first four lines do not rhyme, and its last four rhyme on one syllable. Auden and Chester Kallman, who translated the opera themselves, write that such rhymes are frequent in German and mostly comic in English, and that a solemn scene does better with no rhyme than with a laughable one. The English order differs too: as printed, the line set here under the seventh stands fifth, so that each condition comes before its penalty.

### Tab “List” (figure)

[plates[2].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)

[plates[2].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
After a line that begins <b>Erst</b>, first, three begin <b>Dann</b>, then. One word at the head of line after line is called anaphora; it makes the lines a list, here of children. Each line ends in a name, his and hers by turns; the names differ by one letter, and each parent sings the lines that end in his or her own. The first two lines have the word for little, the next two <b>wieder</b>, again. A few lines on, in words Mozart did not set, the libretto has the parents, with the little ones at play round them, rejoice in their own image. Auden, in 1943, calls Papagena Papageno’s double, and says the two multiply at one with nature.

### Tab “Caliban” (compare)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Caliban

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Beside the duet are the three lines of Caliban that Auden prints after it. Prospero has charged Caliban with trying to rape his daughter. Caliban wishes it done: he would have peopled the island with Calibans. Auden finds one attitude in both: the pair are humble and innocent in it, Caliban guilty and defiant. The numbers mark what is shared and what is not. In both, children are to bear a parent’s name. The duet’s children are a gift hoped for from the gods, they come one at a time, and every second one bears the mother’s name. Caliban says <b>I</b>, and all his would bear his own.

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
He sets the opera beside *The Tempest*. (As a dictionary has it, the Manichees held matter to be evil; Pelagius denied original sin.) After the lines of the chorus and the duet he prints Caliban’s answer to Prospero. [auden[0].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
If *The Tempest* is overpessimistic and manichean, *The Magic Flute* is overoptimistic and pelagian.

[auden[0].source]

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
A lecture of January 1943 opens with the questions of the first passage, in an English version. Tamino’s moral, he says there, is the familiar one: he is blessed for having staked everything, failure included. The surprise is that Papageno gets his girl too, because he is frankly and selfishly himself and would sooner give her up than die. Papagena, his double, is given him, and the two multiply at one with nature. But a Papageno who has been woken, he adds, cannot stay as he is: he must follow Tamino onward or sink to a Monostatos or a Caliban.

[auden[1].source]
“Vocation and Society” (1943), in *Auden Studies 3: “In Solitude, for Company”* (1995), pp. 15–16 and 18

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
An essay of 1946 calls the story a parable about education with two heroes, a romantic one and a comic one. Tamino stakes his own life, and the princess with it, on the mere chance of marrying her. Papageno is in love with no one woman; a girl is all he wants. To be sure of his life he accepts staying single. He is rewarded all the same, and the pair expect to live on, not as themselves, in children without number. Tamino’s heroism, he says, is passion; Papageno’s is the humility to admit he has none.

[auden[2].source]
“Mozart and the Middlebrow” (1946), in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 290–91

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Lecturing on *The Tempest* in 1947, he says the play and the opera run parallel and ask the same thing, what education is. He pairs the persons: Sarastro with Prospero, the Queen of the Night with Sycorax, Monostatos with Caliban, Tamino and Pamina with Ferdinand and Miranda. Papageno, who refuses to be tested at the risk of death, still wins the prize, because he too will pay, in his own way: by staying single, or by taking an old woman for his wife.

[auden[3].source]
*Lectures on Shakespeare*, reconstructed and edited by Arthur Kirsch (2000), the lecture on *The Tempest*, p. 300

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In the preface to their English version, he and Chester Kallman find two themes in the story. One is a change in how Night stands to Day, instinct to reason: where the Queen and Sarastro were at war, Pamina and Tamino marry. The other is education: how someone finds his calling and what that asks of him. The story has two heroes, one needing courage to venture, one humility to stay at home; Papageno passes his trial when he chooses to stay single. The original blurs this, they say, keeping him on stage for laughs and having him vow a silence the audience knows he will break.

[auden[4].source]
W. H. Auden and Chester Kallman, Preface to *The Magic Flute* (1956), in *Libretti and Other Dramatic Writings by W. H. Auden, 1939–1973* (1993), pp. 129–32

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
He and Kallman also wrote about the work of translating opera. *The Magic Flute*, they note, is made of separate musical numbers, and the talk between them is spoken. A libretto has two rhythms, the one its verse has when read aloud and the one the music gives it, and a translator must keep the second. Rhymes of two syllables, frequent in German, are mostly comic in English. And the style must suit the kind of opera and the kind of character.

[auden[5].source]
W. H. Auden and Chester Kallman, “Translating Opera Libretti,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 484–91

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
The preface of 1956 is by Auden and Chester Kallman together. That preface, the lecture of 1943 and the essay of 1946 were read here only in snippets of scanned copies, not in full.

[caveats[1]]
Auden’s lecture of 1947 on *The Tempest* was put together by its editor from notes that students made, so its words may not be his; it too was read only in snippets.

[caveats[2]]
Singers sing the aria as Mozart set it: words of the fifth and sixth lines come again, and *Rache* and *Götter* are one word. The lines shown are those of the libretto printed in 1791, so the record on this page will not match them word for word.

[caveats[3]]
The English libretto printed here leaves out the spoken scene of the first passage, so the English under its phrases is this page’s own. Under the aria its lines are set in the German order; in that libretto the line shown seventh stands fifth.

[caveats[4]]
The stresses marked in the aria are one reader’s: in three lines the fourth beat falls on a light word, and the last line’s first word can be heard lighter. The count of five-beat lines leaves out an irregular line of the two armed men and the duet’s stammered opening.
