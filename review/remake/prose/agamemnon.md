## The head of the page

[title]
The yoke, the cloth and the net

[where]
*Agamemnon*: the first chorus, the king’s return, the murder

[about] (under “The passage”)
Agamemnon, king of Argos, has been ten years at the war with Troy. In the first passage the chorus of old men sing of how it began: the fleet lay windbound at Aulis, a seer said the goddess Artemis asked for the life of the king’s daughter, and the king spoke. In the second he has just come home, and his wife Clytemnestra has ordered purple cloths spread between his chariot and the door. In the third she stands over his body and tells the old men what she did.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Agamemnon is Auden’s first example of the Greek tragic situation. Either course is wrong: kill his daughter or fail his army. He could not have avoided the wrong, yet being placed so is the sign of a guilt the gods hold against him: hubris, the self-confidence of a man who believes he is a god, beyond suffering. Sometimes an act shows it: Agamemnon treading the purple carpet.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
The typical Greek tragic situation is one in which whatever the hero does must be wrong

[view.source]
“The Greeks and Us” (the introduction to *The Portable Greek Reader*, 1948), in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), p. 19

## Passage 1: The king at Aulis, as the elders sing it  (plates[0], id "yoke", from agamemnon/p1)

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[0].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
anaphora

[plates[0].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
the same word at the head of clause after clause

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The king names two acts and gives one word to both: <b>Ill</b> to disobey, and <b>ill</b> to kill his child. A word repeated at the head of clauses that follow one another is an anaphora. This one is Aeschylus’s: his Greek begins both clauses with <i>bareia</i>, heavy. Neither act is called the lesser evil. Four lines on, the translator, E. D. A. Morshead, has <b>’Twixt woe and woe</b>, where the Greek asks which of the two is free of evil. This choice is Auden’s first example of the Greek tragic situation, in which either course is wrong: kill his daughter or fail the army he leads.

### Tab “Metaphor” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Metaphor

[plates[0].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)
metaphor

[plates[0].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)
one thing spoken of as another

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The elders tell what he did in one picture. A yoke is the harness that fastens a pair of oxen. Calling one thing by another’s name is a metaphor: here the compulsion of Fate is a yoke. The yoke is <b>Fate’s</b> and it is <b>compelling</b>. Yet the subject is <b>he</b> and the verb is <b>took</b>. Both are in Aeschylus: his king puts on the yoke-strap of Necessity, and the verb is one used of getting into clothes or armor. Auden wrote that the drama of a play of the Greek kind is in what the situation forces the hero to do, not in what he freely chooses.

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The rhyme is the translator’s. Aeschylus’s song is not rhymed. The four lines that name the two acts rhyme by turns: <i>disobey</i> with <i>slay</i>, <i>pride</i> with <i>side</i>. Then <b>dwell</b> ends a line of three beats, and its rhyme waits. Two couplets follow, pairs of rhymed lines. A handbook of verse calls the couplet the simplest group of lines that rhyme holds together, and each of these holds one of the king’s reasons: he dare not desert, and the army’s demand is rightful. Only then does <b>well</b> answer <b>dwell</b>, in the speech’s other line of three beats. So the rhyme left open at the line of the two woes is closed by the prayer that the deed be well.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[0].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[0].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
<b>Recreant</b> is an old word for one who gives up a fight, and Aeschylus’s word is <i>liponaus</i>, a man who deserts the fleet. <b>Rightfully</b> stands for <i>themis</i>, what custom lays down as right. So both reasons the king gives concern the fleet: to spare the child would be desertion, and the army’s demand is right. Auden describes sparing her as a failure of the king’s duty to the army. <b>Fate’s</b> stands for <i>ananke</i>: force, constraint, necessity. In 1945 Auden called Greek tragedy the tragedy of necessity: what its spectator feels is pity that it had to be so.

## Passage 2: At the palace door: thirteen lines  (plates[1], id "cloth", from agamemnon/p4)

At the palace door: thirteen lines

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[1].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
stichomythia

[plates[1].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
two speakers answer each other a line at a time, often taking up each other’s words

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Queen and king speak one line each for thirteen lines, and she has the first and the last. Talk in single alternating lines is called stichomythia, and the translator keeps Aeschylus’s count. A line takes up what the last one said: <i>unsay</i> is met by <i>I have said</i>, <i>human blame</i> by the <i>murmur of a crowd</i>. Just before, the king refused the cloths as an honor for gods, a pride he fears. In these lines the gods are named once, in her first question. Then the talk is of Priam, of other people, of who wins. After it he walks, the act that for Auden shows his hubris: the self-confidence of a man who believes he is a god.

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Sentence

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Each line is a whole sentence, marked by its kind. In English the queen’s seven are four orders, two questions and one statement. The king’s six are five statements, the first led in by <i>Know</i>, and one question. In his speech just before, the orders are his: <i>Strew not this purple</i>, and <i>bid thee honour me as man</i>. In these lines they are hers, and her last opens on a single word, <b>Yield</b>. His reply ends by saying that his will bows down to hers. Three of her orders are in the Greek, the first and the last among them. Her question about the gods and her order about envy are statements in H. W. Smyth’s Greek text.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The king calls the talk a war, which is no woman’s part. She answers in his terms: happy victors may well yield in it. He asks if she craves a triumph. In her last line <b>Yield</b> stands for a Greek verb that means be persuaded, or obey. <b>Of thy grace</b> stands for one word, <i>hekon</i>: of your own free will. Her <b>happy victors</b> are in the Greek the fortunate. So her argument is his own good fortune, and what she asks is that he give way freely. Auden wrote that it takes exceptional good fortune to be tempted into hubris.

## Passage 3: The queen over the body  (plates[2], id "net", from agamemnon/p6)

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[2].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
metaphor

[plates[2].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
one thing spoken of as another; here a robe called a net, after a simile that says it is like one

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The first two lines say she so worked that he could neither flee nor ward off his doom. The next three give the means three ways. First a simile, a likeness said outright with <i>as</i>: a <b>trammel</b>, a fishing net, hemming in a shoal. Then a metaphor, the thing called by another’s name: she trapped him with <b>toils</b>, a hunter’s nets. Only the last line gives the plain noun, a <b>robe</b>. Aeschylus keeps it back in the same way: his Greek names the net first, adds <i>as for fish</i>, and names the garment last.

### Tab “Play” (shape)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Play

[plates[2].lenses[1].what] (printed over the drawing)
where a net is named in the play, who names it, and who is caught

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
This translation names a net at nine places, eight of them in the Greek. The first two are thanks for victory: the elders praise the Night that flung a <i>mesh</i> over Troy, and the king says the <i>meshes</i> closed on it <i>by our hands</i>. Each later net is said of the king or his captive Cassandra. One catches no one: rumor, the queen says, gave him more wounds than a net has holes. Cassandra sees one before the murder. Here the queen tells how she used hers; last, the elders mourn their king, wrapped in a <i>spider-web</i>. Auden wrote that the tragic hero starts out glorious and fortunate and ends by suffering more than the chorus that watches him fall.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Two of the translator’s words need their old senses. A <b>trammel</b> was a fishing net before it was any hindrance. <b>Toils</b> are nets for taking prey, and the word comes from the French for cloth, <i>toile</i>. The last line keeps two words of Aeschylus. <b>Abundance</b> stands for <i>ploutos</i>, wealth, and <b>robe</b> for <i>heima</i>, a garment. Both are used at the palace door. The king was loath to waste the wealth of the house, and <i>heima</i> was the word for the cloths he walked on. Auden names the walk as the act that shows the king’s hubris. The robe, named in the same two words, is what holds him for the blow.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In “The Greeks and Us” Auden also wrote that the tragic hero is a warning, and that the warning is meant for the chorus, the ordinary citizens. At the start of the play the hero has glory and good fortune; by its end he suffers more than they do, because he has run against a law of right that holds for everyone. Watching him fall, the chorus learns that a strong man’s strength tempts him into impiety, which the gods punish. The gods, he wrote, are gods because they are perfectly just, and their strength is the tool of their justice.

[auden[0].source]
“The Greeks and Us,” in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), p. 19

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
He added that this world is foreign to a modern reader, who takes a man’s acts to be partly his own free choice and partly his circumstances, and so cannot follow a world where the situation a man is in is enough to make him guilty. The Greek hero, he wrote, is punished for his hubris by being made to sin without knowing it or against his will. His suffering comes on him from outside, as a punishment sent by the gods.

[auden[1].source]
“The Greeks and Us,” in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), pp. 20–21

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In *The Dyer’s Hand* Auden wrote that a play of the Greek kind is about an uncommon person who meets an uncommon fate, and that its drama is in what the situation forces that person to do. The chorus may sometimes warn the hero, but he cannot heed them, because a hero of this kind cannot become other than he is.

[auden[2].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
In a Greek tragedy everything that could have been otherwise has already happened before the play begins.

[auden[2].source]
“The Globe,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 173–74

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In 1945 Auden set the Greek tragic hero beside the Christian one. Greek tragedy, he wrote, is the tragedy of necessity: the spectator pities what could not have been otherwise. Agamemnon is again one of his examples, a hero who must choose between two evils. The hero’s flaw, he wrote, does not produce the tragic situation. The gods send the situation to punish the flaw.

[auden[3].source]

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
The verse and the rhyme are E. D. A. Morshead’s, of 1881. Auden’s list for the course named Louis MacNeice’s translation of 1936, which is in copyright and is not shown here.

[caveats[1]]
No Greek is printed here. What is said of the Greek rests on Herbert Weir Smyth’s text of 1926. Morshead worked from an earlier text and may have read it differently: in the thirteen single lines he has a question and an order where Smyth prints statements.

[caveats[2]]
Auden names Agamemnon for the choice at Aulis and the walk on the carpet. His other sentences used here are about Greek tragedy in general; the page applies them to these lines. His essay of 1945 was read only in snippets of a scanned copy.

[caveats[3]]
The count of nets depends on what is counted. Nine places are counted in Morshead’s English. At one of them the Greek has no net, at one no one is caught, and places that name a robe, a trap or an ambush but no net are left out.

[caveats[4]]
In the translator’s rhymes *took* and *yoke* agree only loosely, and *mind* and *wind* fully only in an older pronunciation of *wind*.
