## The head of the page

[title]
A single line of verse

[where]
*The Journal of My Other Self* *(The Notebooks of Malte Laurids Brigge)*, Part One, pages 18 to 20 of the edition of 1930; and one poem, “Der Panther”

[about] (under “The passage”)
Malte Laurids Brigge, twenty-eight and unknown, keeps these notebooks in a small room in Paris. He has just written that he is learning to see and ought to begin work, and that verses written young come to little, since verses are experiences. Then he says what a single line of verse requires. The third passage is a poem of 1902 or 1903, from the Paris stay the novel draws on; Rilke later called it the first result, in the Jardin des Plantes, of being schooled to work before nature.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Nothing by Auden that names this book has been found. In 1936 he praised the part of it that the first two passages here come from, as the critic Herbert Read quoted it: a fine passage, where Rilke counts out the mass of things seen and heard that one poem needs. That, he went on, was exactly his objection to Shelley, who seemed to him to have attended to nothing but ideas.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
sensory experiences that should go to the making of a single poem

[view.source]
“Psychology and Criticism,” *New Verse*, no. 20 (April–May 1936); in *The English Auden*, edited by Edward Mendelson (1977), p. 357

## Passage 1: One sentence, eleven things  (plates[0], id "count", from rilke/p1-02)

[plates[0].heading]
One sentence, eleven things

[plates[0].label] (names the first of two versions)
The English of 1930

[plates[0].pair.label] (names the second version)
Rilke’s German

### Tab “Sentence” (sentence)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Sentence

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
This is one sentence of 126 words in the English. It makes its assertion at once: subject and verb are done in the first eight words. The next 104 are a list. <b>To</b> stands at the head of eleven things to return to, as <i>an</i> does in Rilke’s German. The sentence could end after any of them. Handbooks of composition call such a sentence loose, and nothing in its grammar says when the list must stop. It stops at the dash, and all this is then called not enough. Auden’s verb for the passage is <i>enumerates</i>: Rilke counts out the things seen and heard that one poem needs.

[plates[0].lenses[0].spans[0].label] (in the key)
subject and verb

[plates[0].lenses[0].spans[1].label] (in the key)
the eleven things

[plates[0].lenses[0].spans[12].label] (in the key)
not enough

[plates[0].lenses[0].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage, and a count of its words in the English printed here, with the pair joined by the dash counted as two words. The loose sentence: T. Clifford Allbutt, *Notes on the Composition of Scientific Papers* (1904), p. 64. The German is the same sentence in Rilke’s text. Auden’s verb: “Psychology and Criticism” (1936), in *The English Auden*, p. 357.

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[0].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)
climax

[plates[0].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)
a ladder: words or phrases set in rising order, each step greater than the one before

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The last five things in the list rise in order, each with more space in it than the one before: quiet <b>rooms</b>, mornings by the <b>sea</b>, the sea itself, <b>oceans</b>, nights that flew with all the <b>stars</b>. Such a series is a climax, Greek for ladder. Through the first four steps the English phrases shrink as the things grow, from seven words to one. Then the last step is the longest, thirteen words, and the dash stops the list at its widest. So the <i>all this</i> that the sentence then calls not enough has just taken in the stars.

### Tab “German” (compare)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
German

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The build of the sentence is Rilke’s. One verb, <i>zurückdenken</i>, to think back, carries the list, and each of the eleven things begins with <b>an</b>, as it begins with <b>to</b> in the English. The English says <i>sea</i> twice and then <b>oceans</b>. Rilke says <i>Meer</i>, sea, three times: by the sea, the sea as a whole, seas. Three steps of the climb shown under Figure are taken on that one noun: the word stays, and what it covers grows. After the dash comes <i>noch nicht genug</i>, not yet enough, the first of three <i>not yet</i> in the German.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[0].say] (a numbered remark)
The German has one verb here, *zurückdenken*, to think back.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[1].say] (a numbered remark)
The German *an* does the work of the English *to*. It heads each of the eleven things.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[2].say] (a numbered remark)
*Meer* is sea. The word comes three times, and this is the first.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[3].say] (a numbered remark)
The German says *das Meer überhaupt*, the sea as a whole.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[4].say] (a numbered remark)
*Meere* is seas: the same noun, now plural. The English changes the word.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[5].say] (a numbered remark)
The German is *noch nicht genug*, not yet enough. It is the first of three times the paragraph says *noch nicht*.

[plates[0].lenses[2].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The two passages as they stand together, and a count of *an* and *to* in them. The German words: the *Digitales Wörterbuch der deutschen Sprache* under *zurückdenken*, *überhaupt* and *noch*. The second and third *not yet*: Rilke, *Sämtliche Werke*, vol. 6, p. 724, as given at zeno.org.

## Passage 2: Not until  (plates[1], id "turn", from rilke/p1-02)

[plates[1].heading]
Not until

The English of 1930

[plates[1].pair.label] (names the second version)
Rilke’s German: the last sentence

### Tab “Sentence” (sentence)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Sentence

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Three shorter sentences come first. The fourth, of forty-eight words, begins with its condition, <b>Only when</b>, and runs twenty-two words before the dash and <b>only then</b>. Handbooks call this order periodic. The long sentence of the first passage could end in eleven places; this one cannot end before its point, <b>the first word of a poem</b>. From <i>a single verse</i> to this sentence the paragraph says <i>must</i> eight times. This sentence has no <i>must</i> and no <i>one</i>. Its subjects are the memories, <i>it</i>, and the word; the writer is there only as <i>us</i> and <i>ourselves</i>. Earlier sentences say what one must do; this one says what can then happen.

[plates[1].lenses[0].spans[4].label] (in the key)
the main clause

[plates[1].lenses[0].spans[5].label] (in the key)
the point

[plates[1].lenses[0].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)

The periodic sentence: Charles Sears Baldwin, *Composition, Oral and Written* (1909), p. 122, and T. Clifford Allbutt, *Notes on the Composition of Scientific Papers* (1904), p. 64.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Rilke’s German is printed for the last sentence. Before it the German says <i>noch nicht</i>, not yet, three times, the third time of the memories themselves: they <i>are not yet it</i>. Then <b>Only</b>, which is <i>erst</i>, not until, answers twice. The English of 1930 turns that third sentence around: there the memories are what <b>matter</b>, and the third step is gone. The list began for the sake of a single <i>Vers</i>, a line of verse, and it ends when the first word of a <i>Vers</i> <b>arises</b>: <i>aufsteht</i>, stands up. The English says <i>verse</i> there and <b>poem</b> here.

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[0].gloss] (set under the word)
*noch nicht*: not yet

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[0].note] (a note on the word)
This is the second of three in the German: to think of all this is not yet enough, to have memories is not yet enough, and the memories themselves are not yet it.

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[1].gloss] (set under the word)
German: are not yet it

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[1].note] (a note on the word)
Rilke’s words are *selbst sind es noch nicht*, the memories themselves are not yet it, and the English of 1930 says the reverse.

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[2].gloss] (set under the word)
*Erst wenn*: not until

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[2].note] (a note on the word)
*Erst* says that a thing does not come sooner than the time named, and it comes again where the English has *only then*: *erst dann*, not until then.

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[3].gloss] (set under the word)
*Blick*: a look

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[3].note] (a note on the word)
In Grimm’s German dictionary the first sense of *Blick* is a flash of light and a later one the light of the eye; it is the first noun of “Der Panther,” the third passage here.

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[4].gloss] (set under the word)
*Gebärde*: bearing, gesture

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[4].note] (a note on the word)
Rilke uses the same noun, *Gebärde*, near the start of the list for what small flowers make when they open; Grimm gives its first sense as a person’s whole bearing.

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[5].gloss] (set under the word)
*Vers*: a line of verse

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[5].note] (a note on the word)
The German list begins *Um eines Verses willen*, for the sake of one line, and ends on *das erste Wort eines Verses*, the first word of a line; Grimm derives *Vers* from Latin *versus*, the turn of the plow, a furrow, a line.

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[6].gloss] (set under the word)
*aufsteht*: stands up

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[6].note] (a note on the word)
Grimm gives the German verb for getting up from bed or from a seat, and says that in old narrative it is often followed by going, or by speaking. Here going follows: the first word of a line stands up and then goes forth, *ausgeht*.

[plates[1].lenses[1].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
Rilke’s German: the last sentence as printed here, and the sentences before it in *Sämtliche Werke*, vol. 6, p. 724, as given at zeno.org. Grimm’s *Deutsches Wörterbuch* under *Vers*, *aufstehen*, *ausgehen*, *Blick* and *Geberde*; the *Digitales Wörterbuch der deutschen Sprache* under *erst* and *noch*. The English of [the whole paragraph](#/read/rilke/p1-02:0), for *verse* and *poem*.

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[1].lenses[2].name] (the name of the figure)
metaphor

[plates[1].lenses[2].plain] (the figure in plain words)
one thing spoken of as another

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The memories are to turn into three things, and each belongs to a body: <b>blood</b>, a <b>glance</b>, a <b>gesture</b>. This is a metaphor: a thing of the mind is spoken of as the body. The list began, before the first passage here, with what one must <i>see</i> and with the <i>gestures</i> of flowers. It ends with memories that have become one’s own glance and gesture and can no longer be told apart from oneself. Auden wrote in 1939, reviewing Rilke’s poems, that a poet always has the problem of saying abstract ideas concretely, and that the symbols Rilke thinks in are physical, not intellectual. This prose sentence, too, puts <i>memories</i> in physical terms.

[plates[1].lenses[2].parts[0].label] (in the key)
of the mind

[plates[1].lenses[2].parts[1].label] (in the key)
of the body

[plates[1].lenses[2].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage, and the words *see* and *gestures* earlier in [the same paragraph](#/read/rilke/p1-02:0). Metaphor as Gideon O. Burton’s *Silva Rhetoricae* defines it. Auden: “Rilke in English,” *The New Republic*, 6 September 1939, p. 135.

## Passage 3: In the Jardin des Plantes, Paris  (plates[2], id "panther", from rilke-de/panther)

[plates[2].heading]
In the Jardin des Plantes, Paris

[plates[2].label] (names the first of two versions)
Rilke, 1902 or 1903

[plates[2].pair.label] (names the second version)
Jessie Lemont, 1918

### Tab “Subjects” (sentence)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Subjects

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Only the title names the panther. The poem has it twice, both times in the first stanza: in <i>Sein Blick</i>, his gaze, and in <i>Ihm</i>, to him. It is never the subject of a verb. Each stanza takes as its first subject something seen of it: the gaze, <b>Blick</b>; the gait, <b>Gang</b>; the pupil’s curtain, <b>Vorhang der Pupille</b>. The last subject is <b>ein Bild</b>, an image that goes in. Rilke later called the poem the first result of his schooling under Rodin, to work before nature like a painter or sculptor, and wrote that one can see this in it. In the notebook Malte writes that he is learning to see.

[plates[2].lenses[0].spans[0].label] (in the key)
the animal: his, to him

[plates[2].lenses[0].spans[1].label] (in the key)
the first subject of each stanza

[plates[2].lenses[0].spans[5].label] (in the key)
the last subject: an image

[plates[2].lenses[0].spans[6].label] (in the key)
its verb: goes in

[plates[2].lenses[0].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the poem. German Wikipedia, “Der Panther,” on the three stanzas as gaze, gait and eye. Rilke’s letter of 17 March 1926, in *Briefe aus Muzot* (1935), read as Kih-Seong Kuh quotes it, *Die Tiersymbolik bei Rainer Maria Rilke* (1967), p. 60. Malte on learning to see: [an early entry](#/read/rilke/p1-01:8) and [the paragraph on verses](#/read/rilke/p1-02:0).

### Tab “Beat” (beat)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Beat

[plates[2].lenses[1].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
five beats, the line beginning off the beat, in lines of eleven and ten syllables by turns; the last line has four beats

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The rule under each line is the measure and the dots are the speaking voice. For eleven lines the measure does not change. Two things happen once only, both in the last stanza, which begins <i>Nur manchmal</i>, only sometimes. Its second line has the poem’s one full stop inside a line; after it, <i>Dann</i>, then, an image goes in. The last line has four beats. It says the image <i>ceases to be</i>, and a study of animal symbolism in Rilke assumes that the poet cut the line short to strengthen that statement.

[plates[2].lenses[1].points[0].say] (a numbered remark)
The one full stop inside a line. It parts the opening of the eye from what follows it.

[plates[2].lenses[1].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)
Eight syllables and four beats, after eleven lines of five beats.

[plates[2].lenses[1].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the poem, with the syllables and beats counted line by line. What the last stanza says. The reading of the short last line: Kih-Seong Kuh, *Die Tiersymbolik bei Rainer Maria Rilke* (1967), p. 64, which German Wikipedia, “Der Panther,” follows.

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The rhymes go by turns, a b a b. In the first stanza <b>Stäbe</b>, bars, ends the first line, comes back inside the third with its rhyme <i>gäbe</i> right behind it, and returns as <i>Stäben</i> in the fourth, both times after <i>tausend</i>, a thousand. Repeating a word stresses it, and the word here is bars: to him there seem to be a thousand of them, and no world behind.

[plates[2].lenses[2].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the poem, and what its first stanza says. Repeating one word for emphasis: Gideon O. Burton’s *Silva Rhetoricae*, under *ploce*. The sense of the German words: the *Digitales Wörterbuch der deutschen Sprache* under *Stab*, and the English versions printed in English Wikipedia, “The Panther (poem).”

### Tab “English” (compare)

[plates[2].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
English

[plates[2].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
Lemont’s English of 1918 keeps the rhyme by turns and five beats to the line. It changes what some lines say. In the German the bars go past; in the English the glance passes by them. Twice the German says outright that something is not there, and the English gives a noun with adjectives. The gaze <i>holds nothing any more</i>; the English has a <i>dazed and vacant stare</i>. Behind the bars is <i>no world</i>; the English has <i>the empty air</i>. At the end one image <i>ceases to be</i>, in the short line; the English has a <i>flood of visions</i> that <i>sink and die</i>, in a line of full length.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[0].say] (a numbered remark)
Word for word: from the going past of the bars. In the German the bars are what goes past. In the English the glance passes by the bars.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[1].say] (a numbered remark)
*Blick* is a look, a gaze. The notebook has the same word where its English says *glance*: the memories are to turn to *Blick und Gebärde*, glance and gesture.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[2].say] (a numbered remark)
The German has a verb: the gaze holds nothing any more. The English gives the stare two adjectives.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[3].say] (a numbered remark)
*Keine Welt* is no world. The English has *the empty air*.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[4].say] (a numbered remark)
*Ein Bild* is an image, in the singular, and it goes in. The English has a *flood of visions*, and they arise.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[5].say] (a numbered remark)
The German says that it ceases to be, in a line one beat short. The English visions sink and die, and the line keeps its five beats.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Reviewing the *Duino Elegies* in English in 1939, Auden wrote that a poet always has the problem of saying abstract ideas concretely, and that hardly any poet between the seventeenth century and Rilke had found a new way to do it. Shakespeare, he says, saw what is not human in human terms. Rilke goes the other way and sees people in terms of things, which he calls *Dinge*; and that habit of thought, as Rilke had said himself, is more a child’s than an adult’s. All of this is said of the poems. The review does not name this book.

[auden[0].source]
“Rilke in English,” *The New Republic*, 6 September 1939, pp. 135–36; in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 25–27

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In *New Year Letter*, published in 1941, the year of the course, the dead masters sit in judgment on the poet. One couplet names Rilke among them: things bless him, it says, using his German word, *die Dinge*, and it calls him loneliness’s Santa Claus. Later in the poem the Devil hands the poet a volume of Rilke and quotes the Eighth Elegy to him.

[auden[1].source]
*New Year Letter* (1941), lines 215–16 and 549–56

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Donald Pearce, who took the course, made his notes from memory after a lecture. Where his notes on the *Henry IV* lecture stop, he says that more followed, on Rilke’s *Elegies* as far as he recalls, and that he meant to get it down the same night and lost it. No account of a class on this book has been found.

[auden[2].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), p. 148

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden’s later view was cooler. In his inaugural lecture at Oxford in 1956, printed in *The Dyer’s Hand*, he said that Rilke was still a great poet in his judgment, and one he could no longer read. Elsewhere in that book he names Mallarmé and Rilke as the leading heretics of one kind: the poet who credits art with a magical use of its own, until he takes himself for a god who makes his own universe and counts the visible world as nothing. He grants that both had genius, and says that the last impression their work leaves is of falseness and unreality.

[auden[3].source]
“Making, Knowing and Judging” and “The Poet & the City,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 51 and 76

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
Nothing by Auden on this book by name has been found. His sentence of 1936 is about one part of it, which Herbert Read had quoted, and was read here only in snippets of a scanned copy. What he wrote of Rilke from 1939 on is about the poems.

[caveats[1]]
The English of 1930 turns one sentence around: Rilke wrote that the memories themselves are not yet it. His German for that sentence is given from the collected works and is not printed with the English.

[caveats[2]]
*Vers* is glossed here as a line of verse, its first sense in the dictionaries. In common use it can also mean a stanza, and the English of 1930 says *verse* in one place and *poem* in the other. *Aufsteht*, glossed as stands up, in Grimm also means to arise.

[caveats[3]]
The stresses marked in “Der Panther” are one reader’s hearing of the lines. A few unstressed syllables could fairly be marked otherwise, and nothing said about the poem depends on them. The glosses of its German follow the dictionaries named and published English versions.

[caveats[4]]
That Rilke cut the last line of “Der Panther” short to strengthen its statement is an assumption, and the study of 1967 that says so gives it as one. The poem’s date is not certain, and Rilke’s letter of 1926 about it was read as that study quotes it.
