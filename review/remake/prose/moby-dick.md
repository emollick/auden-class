## The head of the page

[title]
Ishmael and Ahab

[where]
*Moby-Dick*, Chapters 1, 37 and 128

[about] (under “The passage”)
Ishmael tells the story of a whaling voyage on the Pequod of Nantucket, whose captain, Ahab, has lost a leg to a white sperm whale, Moby Dick, and makes the crew swear to hunt it. In the first passage, from the opening paragraph, Ishmael says why he goes to sea. The second gives the opening and the close of Ahab’s speech alone in his cabin on the evening of the oath. In the third, near the end, another captain asks Ahab’s help to find a lost boat.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
For Auden this is a tragedy of possibility: it could have gone otherwise. Ahab starts where a Greek hero finishes, in great misfortune, which tempts him and gives him a choice. He chooses wrongly and goes on doing so. Only his last refusal, of the Rachel’s plea for help, makes his end necessary. In defiance, Ahab says that fate acts and not he.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
The defiant man and the obedient man use the same words “It is not I but Fate,” but their meaning is opposite.

[view.source]
“The Christian Tragic Hero,” *The New York Times Book Review*, 16 December 1945, pp. 1 and 21; *The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), pp. 136–37 and 140 (the quotation is on p. 137)

### Tab “Sentence” (sentence)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Sentence

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The first sentence here has 87 words. Four clauses headed by <b>whenever</b> come first, each longer than the last. Then comes the main clause, which could stand alone: <b>I account it high time</b>. Kept to the end, it holds the sense open. Auden wrote that Ishmael goes to sea to escape spleen and his own lack of power, and has consciousness and no will. In the first and third clauses Ishmael says <i>I find myself</i>, a phrase for what one does without meaning to. In the fourth his low spirits get the upper hand <i>of me</i>, and it takes a principle to <i>prevent me</i>. The main verb, <i>account</i>, means consider.

[plates[0].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
All four clauses are headed by the same word, <b>whenever</b> (the fourth after <i>and especially</i>). A word repeated at the head of clause after clause is anaphora, and it makes the clauses one list. The list runs from his face to his soul, to coffins and funerals, to people’s hats in the street, and one <b>then</b> answers all four. Auden prints part of this sentence beside Coleridge and Baudelaire as a description of how it feels to be a solitary who cannot go the crowd’s way. <i>Whenever</i> means every time that, so each item is one that comes back; the sentence before calls going to sea <i>a way I have</i>.

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
<b>Hypos</b> is short for <i>hypochondria</i> and meant low spirits, fits of melancholy. <b>Involuntarily</b> is built on the Latin word for <i>will</i>, with <i>in-</i>, meaning not; it goes with what Ishmael does, at coffin warehouses and funerals. <b>Deliberately</b> comes from the Latin for weighing well, as on scales, and <b>methodically</b> from a Greek word for a following after, built on the word for a road. These two, the words of purpose and method, go only with the acts a principle must prevent: stepping into the street, knocking off hats. Auden’s image for the condition this sentence describes is Dürer’s <i>Melancholia</i>, who knows how to build her city and lacks the will.

### Tab “Beat” (beat)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Beat

[plates[1].lenses[0].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
the foot of blank verse: an off-beat, then a beat

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Ahab speaks alone, in prose, under a stage direction. The rule under the words repeats the foot of blank verse, the verse of Shakespeare’s plays: an off-beat, then a beat. The voice follows it through the first paragraph’s eighteen feet, with a light stress off the beat at <i>pale</i> and a foot, <i>let them</i>, that can be heard turned round. From <i>is laid</i> to <i>I rush</i> it follows twenty more, with one foot turned at <i>Over</i> and one beat left unstressed in <i>unerringly</i>. Chapter 16 says a whaleman of great force learns a <i>lofty language</i> and is <i>formed for noble tragedies</i>. For Auden, Ahab dies unrepenting, like Shakespeare’s tragic heroes.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[0].say] (a numbered remark)
Four feet to the first stop, then five, six, one and two. Only the second phrase has the five feet of a line of blank verse.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)
A poet’s contraction of *wherever*. It saves a syllable, and the beat stays even.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[2].say] (a numbered remark)
Two stresses, then a stop. Two more fall together, on *fixed* and the first syllable of *purpose*. They slow the voice, and here it leaves the rule.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[3].say] (a numbered remark)
The last sentence starts on a stress, against the rule, and ends in step with it.

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[1].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)
metaphor

[plates[1].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)
one thing spoken of as another

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Ahab has dared the gods to <b>swerve</b> him. His answer is a picture: the path to his purpose is laid with <b>iron rails</b>, and his soul is <b>grooved to run</b> on them. Speaking of one thing as another is a metaphor. At the start he says <i>I</i> for the ship, which leaves a <b>wake</b>, a <b>track</b> the waves swell to cover; at the end his way is iron. Neither verb, <i>is laid</i> or <i>is grooved</i>, names a doer. Earlier the verbs are his: <i>I’ve willed</i>, <i>I’ll do</i>. Auden quotes this sentence where he says that defiance and obedience both say it is fate, not I, and mean opposite things.

<b>Envious</b> comes from the Latin for looking with malice, and once meant spiteful. In the chapter before, Ahab says he sees <i>inscrutable malice</i> in the whale; here he gives the waves a word of ill will. ## Passage 3: The Rachel, in Chapter 128  (plates[2], id "rachel", from moby-dick/c128)

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[2].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
antimetabole

[plates[2].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
the same words said again in the opposite order

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Captain Gardiner, whose boy is lost in a boat, says he <i>will not go</i> until Ahab agrees, and asks him to <i>do</i> as he would be done by. That is the Golden Rule of the Gospels. Ahab’s reply has the two verbs in the opposite order: he <i>will not do</i> it, and he must <i>go</i>. Words repeated in the opposite order are called antimetabole, or more loosely chiasmus, a crossing; this one runs from one speaker to the other. With <i>go</i> Ahab puts <b>must</b> where Gardiner had <i>will</i>, and Gardiner had already told him <i>you must</i>. For Auden, Ahab’s end becomes necessary only with the answer he gives here.

### Tab “Sentence” (sentence)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Sentence

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Each of Gardiner’s two sentences here has a clause hung on it. He will not go <i>till</i> Ahab says yes. Ahab is to do <i>as</i> he would be done by <i>in the like case</i>: he too has a boy. The narrator says Ahab’s voice <i>moulded every word</i> of the answer. Its four sentences here are short, and none has such a clause. The last joins a blessing, a wish and a statement with <i>and</i> and <i>but</i>, and ends <b>I must go</b>. The next paragraph calls the refusal <i>unconditional</i>. Auden says that in refusing Gardiner, Ahab counterfeits the Gospel text that a disciple must hate father, mother, wife and children.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
<b>Avast</b> is a ship’s order to stop, and <b>good-bye</b> is a contraction of <i>God be with ye</i>. Ahab says it twice, then <b>God bless ye</b>. His next wish has no God in it: <i>may I forgive myself</i>. In his paragraph on Ahab, Auden writes of a religious hero whose god is his own will, set in defiance. The chapter’s last sentence takes its words from Jeremiah, where Rachel weeps for her children and refuses comfort <i>because they were not</i>. Matthew quotes that verse for the children Herod killed. For Auden the Rachel stands for people caught up in the mystery of existence who neither understood nor chose it, as those children were in Christ’s birth.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden takes Melville’s own word, *isolato*, for Ishmael’s kind: a man who keeps to a land of his own. He sets part of the sentence shown in the first passage beside lines of Coleridge’s “Dejection” and Baudelaire’s “Spleen,” as reports of how it feels to be a solitary who cannot go the way of the crowd. In a city where tradition no longer walls anyone in, he says, no direction is closed to Ishmael and nothing in him makes any one direction necessary, so he has to do something drastic and goes to sea.

[auden[0].source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), pp. 34–36

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden’s essay of 1945 calls the book four things at once: an epic, a tragedy and a quest, each heroic, and a religious quest told as allegory. It is also, he adds, an American novel of its century. The essay ends with Ishmael. He is no character, Auden says, since a character is someone who wills and acts, and Ishmael has consciousness without will: he knows and does nothing. Not having started to live, he cannot die, and the book leaves him an orphan who has yet to make his first choice. Ahab, unrepenting like Shakespeare’s tragic heroes, goes to a death that was not necessary and that he chose.

[auden[1].source]
“The Christian Tragic Hero,” *The New York Times Book Review*, 16 December 1945, pp. 1 and 21

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden reads the nine ships the Pequod meets as so many kinds of people and societies, each standing differently toward the mystery of existence: the old, who may have known it and cannot pass it on; those who know and do not tell; those who make an idol of it or have lost their wits to it; the lazy and the greedy, who will never notice it; those who meet it with plain sense and endurance; the lucky and light-minded, who say there is none; those caught up in it who neither understood nor chose it; and those it has left grieving.

[auden[2].source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), pp. 60–61

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
On the page where he comes to the Rachel, Auden writes that Ahab is glad of pain, the typhoon for one, and takes a calm, happy day for a temptation. He calls this a false copy of the way saints accept suffering and mistrust pleasure.

[auden[3].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
His whole life, in fact, is one of taking up defiantly a cross he is not required to take up.

[auden[3].source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), p. 140

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In class in the fall of 1941 Auden named Ahab and the White Whale, or Kafka’s K. and the Castle, as examples of how a single person stands to his fate, which was the subject of the course, and then played the second movement of Beethoven’s Fourth Piano Concerto. A student wrote down what he asked the class to hear: the piano sets itself against the orchestra, its fate, and strikes out alone; before long it has lost its way; it steadies only when it takes the orchestra back. He asked whether that return is in time.

[auden[4].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), pp. 135–36

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
A student who shared Auden’s house remembered him saying many times that the book is really a poem of the epic kind, and that this is how to read it. In a note to *New Year Letter*, published in 1941, Auden called American writing the writing of solitary people and said that most American books could open the way this one does, with *Call me Ishmael*. Melville’s sea and whale, he wrote there, are no more real than a drawing-room in Henry James; he called most American novels parables.

[auden[5].source]
Charles H. Miller, *Auden: An American Friendship* (1983), p. 135; W. H. Auden, *New Year Letter* (1941), note to line 1566, pp. 152–53

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
Auden’s 1945 essay “The Christian Tragic Hero,” his note in *New Year Letter* and Charles Miller’s memoir were read here only in extracts from scanned copies, not from end to end. His words in class are known from Donald Pearce, a student who wrote them down after the class.

[caveats[1]]
The second passage is prose, and its marks show one way of saying it: *pale*, *let them*, *Swerve me*, *through* and the last syllable of *unerringly* can fairly be stressed otherwise, and *envious* can be said as two syllables or three.

[caveats[2]]
The crossing of *go* and *do* in the third passage runs from one speaker to the other, with other sentences between; the handbooks define antimetabole and chiasmus within one sentence or in clauses that follow each other.

[caveats[3]]
The count of twelve chapters with stage directions takes only directions set on a line of their own: asides in brackets inside a paragraph are not counted, nor is the title of Chapter 29, which is worded as a stage direction.

[caveats[4]]
Auden’s sentence about a hero whose god is his own will begins with *if* and is said of the religious hero in general. It stands in his paragraph on Ahab, and the page sets it beside Ahab’s wish to forgive himself.
