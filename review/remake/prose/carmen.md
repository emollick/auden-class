## The head of the page

[title]
Carmen’s liberty

[where]
*Carmen*, Act 1, Scene 5; Act 2, Scenes 5 and 6; Act 3, Scene 2 (the scene numbers of the French libretto)

[about] (under “The passage”)
In a square in Seville the young men ask Carmen, a gypsy, when she will love them. She answers with a song, then throws a flower at the one man still at his work, the soldier Don José. He goes to prison for her, and the night he comes out she says that if he loved her he would follow her to the mountains; he refuses, then draws his sword on an officer and has to go. In Act 3, among smugglers, she turns up death in the cards.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
In *The Enchafèd Flood* Auden describes the wilderness of the Romantics, sea or desert, as the place with no community, where a person is free alike of the evils and of the duties of life among others. Byron’s ocean is one example, Captain Nemo’s sea another, and Carmen’s mountains a third. Such places are marked by the absence of limits. Because they are free, he adds, they are lonely.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
And so Carmen tempts Don José to leave the fertile plain for the barren lawless mountains.

[view.source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), pp. 15–17

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
French verse is measured by the number of syllables. Here the line has eight, which a history of French verse gives as the line of lyric pieces and lighter poetry: Carmen is answering the young men with a song. The endings go by turns: a mute <i>e</i>, which the count leaves out and a singer sounds, then a full syllable. One line of the refrain is printed with a word twice, and has ten. Auden says a libretto’s verses are addressed to the composer. By Winton Dean’s account the composer wrote these himself, and the tune is adapted from a song by Sebastián Yradier with the same plan, verse in the minor and refrain in the major.

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[0].lenses[2].name] (the name of the figure)
anadiplosis

[plates[0].lenses[2].plain] (the figure in plain words)
a doubling back: the words that end one clause begin the next; here they come back after a second *si*

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The refrain ends on two sentences built alike: <i>si</i>, if, a condition, then a result. Both conditions are in the present; in Act 2 she will say what would happen if. The result of the first, that she loves, comes back after the second <i>si</i> as its condition, the link rhetoric calls anadiplosis, and from it follows a warning. So the warning is for the one who does not love her. The pronoun is <i>tu</i>, one person, though many men asked. One man, José, has gone on working; after the song she goes straight to him, and left alone he puts it down to his paying her no attention. Auden’s example of a lifelong one-sided romance is José and Carmen.

### Tab “Count” (beat)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Count

[plates[2].lenses[0].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
a line of twelve counted syllables, cut after the sixth, then a line of six; the last two lines have eight and twelve

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
French verse is measured by the number of syllables. A final <i>e</i> counts before a consonant, is dropped before a vowel, and is left out at a line’s end. The two women beside Carmen have just told their fortunes in lines of eight, with one line of ten in their refrain. Eight is the measure of her first song and, by a history of French verse, of lyric pieces and lighter poetry. Carmen has read death in four lines of her own. What she sings next is in another measure: a line of twelve, resting after its sixth syllable, then a line of six. The line of twelve has been the standard line of French tragedy since the sixteenth century.

[plates[2].lenses[0].points[0].say] (a numbered remark)

[plates[2].lenses[0].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Each stanza is four lines, rhymed by turns. Its long lines end on a syllable with a mute <i>e</i>, the ending French verse calls feminine: <b>amères</b>, bitter, <b>sincères</b>, sincere. Its short lines end on a full syllable, the masculine ending. French poets have alternated the two since the sixteenth century, and Ronsard gave the reason: verse so made is fitter for music. In singing, the mute <i>e</i> is heard like any other vowel. In the third stanza the short lines end on <b>sort</b>, fate, and <b>mort</b>, death. The last two lines rhyme as a pair: <b>qu’importe</b>, what does it matter, and <b>forte</b>, strong. Their rhyme keeps the sound <i>or</i> of <b>sort</b> and <b>mort</b>.

[plates[2].lenses[1].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage. L. E. Kastner, *A History of French Versification*, pp. 64–65, for the alternation of the two endings, Ronsard’s reason for it, and the mute *e* in singing. Littré under *amer*, *sincère*, *sort*, *mort*, *importer* and *fort*.

### Tab “Build” (sentence)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Build

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The second and third stanzas, marked here, are built alike: <i>si</i>, if, then a command, then what the card will do. If your page is lucky, shuffle and cut without fear. But if you must die, begin again twenty times. They differ at the line ends. In the second stanza the card and its verb share a line. In the third, each long line ends on its subject, <i>the dread word</i>, <i>the pitiless card</i>, and the verb waits for the short line. French classical critics, a history of French verse reports, held that a run-over hurries the reader past the rhyme into the next line, where a stronger pause then falls. Here it falls after <b>sort</b>, fate, and <b>mort</b>, death.

The English is from a libretto sold in New York about 1910. It follows the French line by line, without the rhyme or the count. The French says <i>tu</i>, you, in every stanza; the English begins with <i>I</i>. <b>Sort</b>, fate, becomes <i>heaven</i>. The card’s own words, <b>la mort</b>, death, become <i>thy doom</i>. <b>La plus forte</b>, the strongest, becomes <i>strong</i>. In a lecture on <i>Richard III</i>, pieced together from listeners’ notes, Auden said that people of very strong will are inclined to a belief in fate and in signs, and his example was Carmen reading the cards. Both are in the French: what fate has written the card will say, and Carmen is the strongest.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[0].say] (a numbered remark)
The French says *tu*, you, in every stanza. The English says *I* in this one line, and *thou*, *thy* and *thee* after it.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[1].say] (a numbered remark)
*Mêler* and *couper* are the card player’s words: to shuffle the pack, then to cut it in two. The English says *throw* and *play*.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[2].say] (a numbered remark)
*Sort* is fate. In Littré it is also, among the ancients, a supposed way of learning the future by throwing dice, and the Latin word meant at first tallies of wood thrown on the ground. The English says *heaven*.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[3].say] (a numbered remark)
The French is a command: *begin again twenty times*. In its place the English has a clause that gives the cards a will.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[4].say] (a numbered remark)
The French sentence ends on the card’s own words, set in quotation marks: *la mort*, death. The English says *thy doom* here, and *death* in the line she sings next.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[5].say] (a numbered remark)
*Braver* is to face a person or a thing as a brave man does: to defy.

[plates[2].lenses[3].ties[6].say] (a numbered remark)
*La plus forte* is the strongest. The English says *strong*.

The marks on the two versions. Littré under *sort*, *mêler*, *couper*, *braver*, *mort* and *fort*. Auden on strong will and the belief in fate and signs: *Lectures on Shakespeare*, edited by Arthur Kirsch, p. 21, in the lecture on *Richard III*.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In a talk at Swarthmore College in January 1943 Auden sets the old schoolmaster, who flogged, against the progressive educator, whom he calls a Carmen wrapped in cellophane: one who sings of a land where nobody suffers and nothing is required of anyone, and there is only the self and its expression. He then quotes the four lines on liberty that close Act 2, and adds that this affair, as the little Don Josés soon find out, begins as Hans Andersen and ends as Grimm.

[auden[0].source]
“Vocation and Society” (1943), in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), p. 179

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In his New York lecture on *Richard III*, as an editor has put it together from the notes of people who heard it, Auden says that the more a man masters other people’s wills, the more what he did not intend and cannot control comes to matter to him. He gives Carmen reading the cards as his example of the rule quoted here.

[auden[1].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
Very strong-willed people are apt to believe in fate and signs

[auden[1].source]
*Lectures on Shakespeare*, reconstructed and edited by Arthur Kirsch (2000), p. 21

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Writing of Tristan and of Don Giovanni, Auden says that to idolize one real person romantically for a whole life is possible, and does happen, on one condition: the love must be on one side only, with one of the two playing the Cruel Fair. His example is Don José and Carmen. An idol, he explains, is what a person makes answerable for his own existence, and it is an idol no longer once it makes a claim on him in return.

[auden[2].source]
“Balaam and His Ass,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 121

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In a piece of 1947 on the mythical types of woman, Auden’s second type is the Infernal Venus. She lures men to ruin and is against order of every kind. She stirs men and leaves them unhappy, and as a rule she ends badly. Her classic case, he writes, is Carmen, and he follows the sentence with a passage of Mérimée’s story.

[auden[3].source]
“The Mythical Sex” (1947), in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), p. 328

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In a piece written for *Vogue* in 1948 Auden says that opera is about willed feeling: there could be none unless people, besides having emotions, insisted on having them, whatever trouble that gives themselves and others. Every great character of opera, he says, is of this kind, and Carmen is one of the six he names.

[auden[4].source]
“Opera Addict” (1948), in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), p. 401

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Of the words of an opera Auden says that the verses a librettist writes are addressed to the composer and not to the public, and that their work is done once they have given him a melody. In song, he holds, a listener hears syllables and not words, and it is the syllables that decide what vocal line a composer writes. For that reason he is not, as a rule, in favor of performing operas in translation.

[auden[5].source]
“Notes on Music and Opera,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), p. 473

[auden[6].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Donald Pearce, who took the course of 1941, remembers that when he asked to join it Auden told him there would be opera scores and libretti, and named *Carmen* first. That semester Auden lectured often on love, and the class was set the libretti of *Tristan und Isolde* and *Carmen*, with *Romeo and Juliet* and Denis de Rougemont’s *Love in the Western World*. On neither page does Pearce report anything Auden said of the opera itself.

[auden[6].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), pp. 133 and 137

[auden[7].says] (a paraphrase of him)
By Alan Ansen’s record of his talk in the late 1940s, Auden thought *Carmen* a very great work.

[auden[7].source]
Alan Ansen, *The Table Talk of W. H. Auden*, edited by Nicholas Jenkins (1990), p. 18

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
The English is from a libretto sold in New York about 1910, with no translator named. In the first song it rhymes every second line and seems made for singing; in the other two passages it keeps the order of the French without rhyme. It is not a close translation.

[caveats[1]]
Auden’s remark on Carmen and the cards comes from a lecture pieced together by an editor from listeners’ notes. It was read here only in snippets of a scanned copy, as were his pieces of 1943, 1947 and 1948, Ansen’s record of his talk and Winton Dean’s book on Bizet.

[caveats[2]]
The marks show the count of syllables and the accents the rule fixes, on the sixth syllable of a long line and the last of every line. French verse leaves the other stresses free, and they are not marked.

[caveats[3]]
The second passage sets together three places in Act 2: five lines of Carmen’s to José, two that she sings a little later, and four that everyone sings as the act ends. Auden quotes one line more of her speech than the French text printed here has.

[caveats[4]]
The first passage is the first of the song’s two verses and its refrain; the French text prints the refrain’s second line with one word twice, then once; no score was consulted. By Winton Dean’s account its words are Bizet’s own, and he also reworked those of the card solo.
