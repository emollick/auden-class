## The head of the page

[title]
Three voices in Hell

[where]
*Inferno*, Cantos 3, 5 and 26

[about] (under “The passage”)
Dante tells the poem as his own journey. Lost in a dark wood halfway through his life, he is led down through Hell by the shade of the Roman poet Virgil. At the entrance he reads the writing over the gate. In the second circle Francesca, killed with her lover by her husband, tells him that love led them to one death. Far lower, the Greek hero Ulysses, speaking from inside a flame, tells how a short speech sent his old crew on a last voyage.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden cited the writing over Hell’s gate when he explained the wrath of God. That phrase, he wrote, describes no mood in God. It tells what God is like to a person if he twists or denies his tie to God. So Dante, he said, wrote over Hell that Power, Wisdom and Love made it. He added, crediting Landor, that the people of the *Inferno* have no wish to leave.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
the way in which I experience God if I distort or deny my relation to him

[view.source]
Introduction to *The Living Thoughts of Kierkegaard* (1952), reprinted as “Søren Kierkegaard” in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), p. 176

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Letters mark the rhymes. In each group of three the first and third lines rhyme, and the middle one sets the rhyme of the next. Dante devised this chain, <i>terza rima</i>, for the poem and built it on the number three, which his prose ties to the Trinity. To an Italian, Auden wrote, parting what Dante says from this form is unthinkable. Here the second rhyme joins <b>dolore</b>, pain, <b>fattore</b>, maker, and <b>amore</b>, love: what the gate leads to, and who made it. The chain keeps every group open to the next: when the writing ends, <b>duro</b>, <i>I last</i>, has no rhyme yet, and the next three supply <b>oscuro</b>, dark, and <b>duro</b> again.

[plates[0].lenses[0].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The rhyme letters marked on the passage, and what its lines say; its last open sound, at *porta*, returns in [the next three lines](#/read/dante-italian/inf-03:4). The *Enciclopedia Dantesca* under *terzina*: Dante devised the chain, built it on three, and kept each group open to the next; it quotes his *Vita Nuova* on three and the Trinity. Baum, *Principles of English Versification*, chapter 4. Auden: *Prose*, vol. II, p. 324.

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[0].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)
anaphora

[plates[0].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)
the same words at the head of line after line

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Three lines begin with the same words, <b>Per me si va</b>: <i>through me is the way</i>. Beginning line after line alike is called anaphora, and it belongs to the poem’s grave style of pronouncement. Each line changes only where the way leads: into the sorrowing city, into eternal pain, among the lost people. The <i>me</i> is a gate. Medieval city gates carried verse inscriptions, and this writing is made in their likeness. In the writing only the ninth line turns to anyone: <b>voi ch’intrate</b>, you who enter.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The fifth and sixth lines name the gate’s maker as the three persons of God, each by its established attribute: <b>podestate</b>, power, the Father; <b>sapïenza</b>, wisdom, the Son; <b>primo amore</b>, first love, the Spirit. Auden quoted them to explain the wrath of God: what God is like to a person if he twists or denies his tie to God. On the gate <b>duro</b> is a verb, <i>I last</i>, from the Latin for <i>hard</i>. Four lines later the traveler calls the writing’s sense <b>duro</b>, hard. The two rhyme. A rhyme of one form in two senses sets them against each other: the gate lasts, and its meaning is hard to him.

### Tab “Compare” (compare)

[plates[0].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
Compare

[plates[0].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
Longfellow’s version of 1867 gives each of Dante’s lines one English line, in nearly the same word order. It does not rhyme. In translating Dante, he wrote in his journal, something must be given up, and it must be the rhyme, to keep what he valued more: fidelity. Auden in 1947 weighed an English Dante in prose against one in Dante’s rhyme. English has few rhymes, he wrote, so even the best attempt to keep the form is driven to a falsely poetic choice and order of words. Either way, he said, something is lost. Here the chain is absent, and Dante’s one word <b>duro</b> becomes <b>last</b> and <b>hard</b>.

[plates[0].lenses[3].ties[1].say] (a numbered remark)
*Dole* is an old word for grief, from the same root. In his note Longfellow hears a funeral bell tolling in the two Italian line ends.

### Tab “Sentence” (sentence)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Sentence

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Francesca makes three sentences on one frame, each with <b>Amor</b>, Love, as its subject. The first two add a law in the present and an act in the past: love seized <b>costui</b>, this man; love seized <b>mi</b>, me. The third is one line: love led <b>noi</b>, us, to one death. One use of such repeating, a handbook of 1593 says, is to put the cause before each effect. In her eight lines here no verb has <i>I</i> or <i>we</i> as subject. Twice she adds what still holds, with <b>ancor</b>, still; the second time it is the love. Explaining the wrath of God, Auden repeated a remark he credited to Landor: the people of the <i>Inferno</i> have no wish to leave.

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)

[plates[1].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)
polyptoton

[plates[1].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)
one root repeated in different forms

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
One root comes three times in a single line, each time in a new form: <b>Amor</b>, the noun, love; <b>amato</b>, one who is loved; <b>amar</b>, to love. The figure is called polyptoton. A handbook of 1593 says that words derived from one another please the ear and make the mind consider how near akin the things are. The line is Francesca’s second law: love spares no one who is loved from loving. Later in the poem Virgil says nearly the same with a condition added: love kindled by virtue has always kindled love, once its flame showed (<i>Purgatorio</i> 22, lines 10 to 12).

### Tab “Measure” (beat)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Measure

[plates[1].lenses[2].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
eleven syllables: the last accent always on the tenth, and in these lines another on the sixth

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Dante’s line goes by count: eleven syllables, the last accent always on the tenth. A vowel ending one word usually runs into a vowel beginning the next, and the two count as one syllable. The three lines that begin with <b>Amor</b>, Love, are marked: the rule under each shows the count, and the dots show the voice’s stresses. In the second and third the stresses fall on every second syllable. The first keeps that run to the sixth, then stresses the seventh, right beside it. A stress so placed often brings out its word. The word is <b>ratto</b>, at once: in her first law love takes hold at once.

[plates[1].lenses[2].points[0].say] (a numbered remark)
Stresses meet on the sixth and seventh syllables. The second of them falls on *ratto*, at once.

[plates[1].lenses[2].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)
Seven written syllables count as five: twice a final vowel runs into the vowel that follows.

[plates[1].lenses[2].points[2].say] (a numbered remark)
Here the two vowels stay apart, which is regular after a word like *noi*. The accent on the sixth syllable falls on *noi*, us.

[plates[1].lenses[2].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks drawn on the three lines follow the stresses of the Italian words. The rules of the line are from the *Enciclopedia Dantesca* under *endecasillabo*, *sinalefe* and *dialefe*: eleven syllables, the tenth accented; vowels that meet run together; a stress set beside a main accent often brings out its word. *Ratto* as *at once*: the same work under *ratto*, which cites this line.

### Tab “Antithesis” (figure)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Antithesis

[plates[2].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
antithesis

[plates[2].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
two opposed things set side by side, often in phrases of the same build

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The first three lines end the speech Ulysses made to his old crew at the marks Hercules set so that no one should go farther. Its last two lines set two ways of living against each other, hinged on <b>non</b>, <i>not</i>, and <b>ma</b>, <i>but</i>: <b>viver come bruti</b>, to live like brutes, and <b>seguir virtute e canoscenza</b>, to follow virtue and knowledge. An antithesis can make a thing clear by its contrary. Auden, who quoted the same three lines, said that in this speech Ulysses is again the counselor of evil, and that his words are like the Serpent’s to Eve. The Serpent promised Eve knowledge, and <b>canoscenza</b>, knowledge, is the last word of the speech.

### Tab “Metaphor” (figure)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Metaphor

[plates[2].lenses[1].name] (the name of the figure)
metaphor

[plates[2].lenses[1].plain] (the figure in plain words)
one thing spoken of as another

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
One thing is called by the name of another: <b>remi</b>, oars, are made <b>ali</b>, wings, and the voyage is a <b>volo</b>, a flight. Virgil had called the wings of Daedalus an oarage of wings, and Dante turns the phrase around. Elsewhere in the poem wings and flight are a regular figure for the soul’s movement, as in its rising to God. Between the wings and the flight stands <b>folle</b>, mad. So the flight is at once the ship’s swift course and a going astray. <b>Folle</b> is the speaker’s own word, said from inside the flame. For Dante, Auden said, what Ulysses did was at bottom Adam’s original sin.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
<b>Semenza</b> is <i>seed</i>: the crew are told to consider the stock they come from. <b>Virtute</b> comes from the Latin for <i>manliness</i>, which also meant courage and worth. The word comes once more in the canto, in the poet’s own voice near its start: he reins in his wit so that it does not run where virtue does not guide it. Those lines, Auden wrote, perhaps mean that Dante himself felt the temptation of Ulysses, a lust of curiosity. Ulysses calls his words an <b>orazion</b>, a term of rhetoric for a set speech, and says it made the men so <b>aguti</b>, keen, that he could hardly have held them back.

Where folle, mad, comes in the poem’s hundred cantos (34 of Inferno, 33 of Purgatorio, 33 of Paradiso), and where it is said of a journey

[plates[2].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
In the poem’s hundred cantos <b>folle</b>, mad, comes fourteen times. Four times it is said of a journey. The first of all is the traveler’s: he is not Aeneas and not Paul, he says, and fears his own going may be mad (canto 2). Devils call his road mad (canto 8), and Ulysses says it of his flight (canto 26). The last comes as the traveler tells what he saw from the stars: the mad crossing of Ulysses (canto 94, <i>Paradiso</i> 27). So the poem sets one word on both journeys, first and last in the traveler’s own voice.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In an unfinished book of 1939, Auden said everyone meets a few people he can only feel to be damned, usually intelligent, sensitive and well placed. Hell’s gates, he wrote, stand wide open. The lost may go when they wish; but that would mean admitting there is another life outside, and they fear to, because they would then have to live it. All this they know, and the knowledge is Hell’s flame. Many people, perhaps most, stand with the good pagans of the *Inferno*, desiring without hope; but Limbo, their place, belongs, he said, at the bottom of Purgatory, not at the top of Hell, where Dante’s poem puts it.

[auden[0].source]
“The Prolific and the Devourer,” an unfinished book written mostly in 1939; in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), Appendix I, p. 428

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In 1946 Auden reviewed C. S. Lewis’s *The Great Divorce*. Its teaching aim, he wrote, is to set right a mistaken idea of what Christians hold about Hell and to make plain the words Dante saw over the gate; he quoted the gate’s fourth, fifth and sixth lines in Carlyle’s prose translation. The mistake was to liken God’s law to human law, with an eternal penalty imposed on people without their consent. On doctrine he had two objections, and one touched Dante: whatever Dante did, he said, theology lets no writer show a historical person as a lost soul. Nobody may believe that even of Hitler, still less hope it.

[auden[1].source]
“Red Lizards and White Stallions,” *The Saturday Review of Literature*, April 1946; in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 292–95

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In a talk of January 1943 Auden drew on the canto of the gate. A student of literature in whom nothing happens, he said, finds it out. In a student of physics nothing may happen either, but his skill gets him a good post in industry, so he may live out his life and never learn that he has no passion: that he is one of the trimmers of the *Inferno*, the souls just inside the gate, who earned neither blame nor praise. Auden then gave Virgil’s words about them in the Carlyle and Wicksteed prose of the Modern Library, the edition on his list for the course.

[auden[2].source]
“Vocation and Society,” an essay based on a talk of January 1943, in *‘In Solitude, for Company’: W. H. Auden after 1940*, edited by Katherine Bucknell and Nicholas Jenkins (1995), pp. 25–26; also in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), p. 180

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden spoke of Francesca at the end of his New York lecture on *Romeo and Juliet*, given on 6 November 1946 and rebuilt from students’ notes. Romeo and Juliet, he said, do not tell romance from love. He cited Kierkegaard: romantic love can be shown in a single moment, and married love cannot, since a good husband is one every day. Then he said that the tale of Paolo and Francesca holds a warning to be heeded, and gave the scene in the Carlyle and Wicksteed prose, her three sentences on Love included, down to the last words of the canto.

[auden[3].source]
Lecture on *Romeo and Juliet*, 6 November 1946, in *Lectures on Shakespeare*, reconstructed and edited by Arthur Kirsch (2000), pp. 51–52

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Writing on *The Tempest*, Auden said the play shows Nature and Spirit in conflict, lays the fault on Nature and clears Spirit. Dante, he said, holds just the reverse. He quoted three lines of *Purgatorio* 17 in Italian, which say that natural love never errs and that the other love can err by choosing a bad object, or by too little force or too much. In 1941, the year of the course, he had gone to the same canto twice, both times to its lines on love as the seed of all virtue and of each act that earns punishment.

[auden[4].source]
“Balaam and His Ass,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 130–31; the notes to *New Year Letter* (1941); “Eros and Agape,” *The Nation*, 28 June 1941

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden noted that the last voyage of Ulysses seems to be Dante’s invention. Ulysses is damned as a giver of evil counsel. Auden quoted, in Carlyle’s prose, Dante on holding his talent in check, Ulysses on his ardor to know the world, and the close of the speech to the crew. There, he said, Ulysses is again what he was at Troy. He acts like the sea hero of the Romantics, which in Tennyson he becomes. For Dante the deed was worse than blameworthy: at bottom it was Adam’s original sin. Dante’s words on his talent, he added, may mean that Dante felt the same temptation: a lust of curiosity.

[auden[5].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
the Evil Counsellor whose words echo the words of the Serpent to Eve

[auden[5].source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), pp. 9–10

[auden[6].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In 1947 Auden reviewed *The Portable Dante*, whose *Comedy* is Binyon’s translation, in Dante’s rhyme. He judged that version the best so far and set a passage of it beside Longfellow’s and Cary’s, which he counted among the best known. To an Italian, he wrote, parting what Dante says from the form is unthinkable. A reader of English is troubled by the falsely poetic wording and word order that a language poor in rhymes forces on even the best attempt to keep the form. The best English imitation of Dante, he noted, is a passage of Eliot’s in unrhymed three-line groups. Prose or verse, he said, something is lost.

[auden[6].source]
“The Essence of Dante,” *The New York Times Book Review*, 29 June 1947; in *Prose*, vol. II (2002), pp. 322–25

[auden[7].says] (a paraphrase of him)
By Donald Pearce’s account, the final examination of the 1941 course was to write out cantos of the *Comedy* from memory, in the Carlyle and Wicksteed prose version. The list began with *Inferno* 3 and 5, the cantos of the first two passages here. Auden asked for seven cantos and came down to five when the class protested. Other witnesses give six.

[auden[7].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), p. 153

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
What Auden said of Francesca comes from a 1946 lecture rebuilt from students’ notes; he said her story holds a warning and did not say what it is. The remark he credited to Landor has not been found in Landor’s writings, and in 1938 Auden was unsure whose it was.

[caveats[1]]
Six things of Auden’s were read only in extracts from scanned copies: a book of 1939, a note of 1941, a talk of 1943, a lecture of 1946 and two reviews. In 1939 he wrote of Hell’s gates and did not name Dante’s gate.

[caveats[2]]
In the first of Francesca’s lines here the voice may lean harder on *gentil* or on *ratto*; that the meeting stresses bring out *ratto* is this page’s own use of a general rule. In the sixth the subject, read here as Love, is by another reading Paolo.

[caveats[3]]
On the speech of Ulysses the commentaries divide. Some read it as a last piece of false counsel, as Auden did. Others, Bosco and Reggio among them, hear in it what Dante himself held: that to want knowledge is natural to every man.

[caveats[4]]
Under *Poem*, the count of fourteen takes the adjective *folle* alone; the noun *follia* comes twice more in the poem. Auden’s list for the course named an English prose translation; the passages are shown here in Dante’s Italian, with Longfellow’s verse beside it.
