## The head of the page

[title]
The last canto

[where]
*Paradiso*, Canto 33

[about] (under “The passage”)
Dante has risen to the last heaven, one of pure light, where the blessed form a white rose. Beatrice, who led him up, is back in her seat among them, and Saint Bernard, devoted to the Virgin Mary, stands beside him. The canto opens with Bernard’s prayer to her that Dante may be given the strength to see God; the prayer is granted, and the rest is what he saw. He is, he says, like a man after a dream: the feeling stays and the sight is almost gone.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
For Auden, Dante is the greatest writer of dream literature: his images never run dry, and his exact structure of logic and mathematics fixes even the number of lines. For Dante, he wrote, the vision of one beloved person’s glory is not the foot of a long ladder: a single step leads from it to God, and in the last vision that love is transformed, not destroyed.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
there is only one step to take, from the personal creature who can love and be loved to the personal Creator who is Love

[view.source]
“George Macdonald” (1954) and the Introduction to *The Protestant Mystics* (1964), both in *Forewords and Afterwords* (1973), pp. 269 and 68

### Tab “Figure” (figure)

[plates[0].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Figure

[plates[0].lenses[0].name] (the name of the figure)
paradox

[plates[0].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)
a statement that contradicts itself on the surface and is still meant as true

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The prayer opens with three pairs of terms, and each pair is one name for Mary: <b>Vergine Madre</b>, virgin mother; <b>figlia del tuo figlio</b>, daughter of your son; <b>umile e alta</b>, lowly and high. By the measure of nature the two terms of each pair contradict each other. A statement that contradicts itself and is true all the same is a paradox. Pairs like these were already a formula of the Church’s public prayer. The next three lines state the event they point to: she raised human nature so high that its maker did not disdain to become its creature. In the faith the poem holds, each pair is fact.

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Lines 1 and 3 rhyme. The line between them ends on <b>creatura</b>, creature, and lines 4 and 6 answer it with <b>natura</b>, nature, and <b>fattura</b>, the thing made. Line 5 ends on <b>fattore</b>, maker, answered by <b>amore</b>, love, and <b>fiore</b>, flower. This is terza rima, the chained rhyme Dante was the first to use for a long poem. <i>Fattore</i> and <i>fattura</i> come from one verb, to make. They end the lines that say the maker of human nature did not disdain to become its making. Maker then rhymes with love: line 7 says that in her womb love was lit again.

[plates[0].lenses[1].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage, and lines 4 to 7. Terza rima and Dante’s first use of it: the Treccani *Vocabolario* under *terzina*, and the *Encyclopaedia Britannica* under *terza rima*. *Fattore* and *fattura*: the *Vocabolario*, whose entry for *fattura* cites these lines, and the *Enciclopedia Dantesca* under *fattura*.

### Tab “English” (compare)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
English

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Beside Dante’s lines is the English prose of Wicksteed, printed in 1899 facing the Italian. A student recalls that Auden’s class had to write out cantos of the <i>Comedy</i> from memory in this prose, this canto last. Auden wrote that the sound of a poem, with its rhythm, rhymes and puns, cannot be translated, and that what does not rest on the words themselves, such as an image, can be, to a degree. The three pairs cross into English whole, and <i>Maker</i> and <i>making</i> keep one root as <i>fattore</i> and <i>fattura</i> do. The rhyme is gone, and so is the one letter that parts <i>figlia</i> from <i>figlio</i>.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[0].say] (a numbered remark)
The first pair crosses word for word.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[1].say] (a numbered remark)
The sense is whole. In Italian, daughter and son are one word with two endings, *figlia* and *figlio*. English has two unlike words.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[2].say] (a numbered remark)
The third pair is kept: *lowly* and *uplifted*.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[3].say] (a numbered remark)
In the verse *creatura* ends a line and sets a rhyme that lines 4 and 6 must meet. In prose the word sets nothing.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[4].say] (a numbered remark)
*Maker* for *fattore*.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[5].say] (a numbered remark)
*Making* for *fattura*. Wicksteed keeps one root for the two words, as Dante does.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[6].say] (a numbered remark)
*Farsi* is *fare*, to make, turned on oneself: to make oneself, and so to become. It is the verb inside *fattore* and *fattura*. The English word *become* loses it.

[plates[0].lenses[2].ties[7].say] (a numbered remark)

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)

So the things of the universe are spoken of as the loose quires of a book, and in the depth of the light Dante sees them bound, with love, in one <b>volume</b>. This is the first thing he tells of what he saw.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Lines 85 to 87 are in a bookbinder’s words: <b>legato</b>, bound; <b>volume</b>; <b>si squaderna</b>, lies apart in quires. Lines 88 to 90 say it again in the terms of the schools of his day. <b>Sustanze</b> are things that exist in themselves, and <b>accidenti</b> the qualities that exist in them. <b>Conflati</b> is from a Latin verb for melting metals together. The sentence ends on <b>un semplice lume</b>, which is read two ways: the thing he speaks of is one light without parts, or what he says of it is no more than a glimmer. At line 55 he has said that from there on his sight was greater than speech shows.

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[0].gloss] (set under the word)
is held deep within

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[0].note] (a note on the word)
From *interno*, inner: to go deep inside, or to be held there. Some commentators also hear the sense to become threefold, of the Trinity; others reject that.

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[1].gloss] (set under the word)
bound, as a book is

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[1].note] (a note on the word)
Said of books, *legare* is to sew the sheets together and give them a cover.

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[2].gloss] (set under the word)
a book · Latin: a roll

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[2].note] (a note on the word)
Latin *volumen*, a thing rolled up, and so a roll of writing, from *volvere*, to turn. Twice in the *Paradiso* Dante uses *volume* for the turning heavens. In *Inferno* 1 it is Virgil’s book, and rhymes with *lume* there too.

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[3].gloss] (set under the word)
lies apart in quires

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[3].note] (a note on the word)
From *quaderno*, a quire: four sheets folded and sewn, eight leaves. To *squadernare* a book is to unsew it. Some twenty lines earlier the Sibyl’s oracle is lost on light leaves in the wind.

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[4].gloss] (set under the word)
things that exist in themselves

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[5].gloss] (set under the word)
qualities that exist in things

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[6].gloss] (set under the word)
how the two are related

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[6].note] (a note on the word)
The old commentators took *costume* as natural workings, and Longfellow follows them. Recent ones take it as the relation of substance to accident.

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[7].gloss] (set under the word)
blown together · fused

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[7].note] (a note on the word)
Latin *conflare*: to blow together, to kindle a fire, and from that to melt and fuse metals.

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[8].gloss] (set under the word)

[plates[1].lenses[1].notes[8].note] (a note on the word)
Latin *simplex*, onefold: *sem-*, one, with a root found in *plicare*, to fold. Longfellow reads *one simple light*. The Treccani dictionary cites this line for the sense *no more than*.

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Line 83 ends on <b>etterna</b>, eternal. In terza rima, the chained rhyme of this poem, its sound must come back twice, and the two words that bring it are verbs of opposite sense: <b>s’interna</b>, is held deep within, and <b>si squaderna</b>, lies apart in quires. One subject has both verbs. So the rhyme, which makes the end of a line stand out, sets there the gathered and the scattered state of the same things. The line between them ends on <b>volume</b>, and <b>lume</b>, light, the same word without its first two letters, answers it. Six times in the poem a line ends on the word for volume, and each time the word for light rhymes with it.

[plates[1].lenses[2].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage, and lines 85 to 87. The chain’s rule: the Treccani *Vocabolario* under *terzina*; the two verbs, under *internare* and *squadernare*. That rhyme underlines a line’s end: the *Enciclopedia dell’Italiano* under *rima*, and Baum, *The Principles of English Versification*, chapter 3. Counted in the Italian printed here: lines ending on the word for volume (*Inferno* 1.84; *Paradiso* 15.50, 23.112, 26.119, 28.14, 33.86).

### Tab “Sentence” (sentence)

[plates[2].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Sentence

[plates[2].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The last three lines make one statement, and its subject comes last. First the verb, <b>volgeva</b>, was turning; then what is turned, my desire and my will; then a wheel that is evenly moved. Only the last line names what turns them: <b>l’amor</b>, love. A sentence that holds back what completes its sense is called periodic; here that is the subject. In all seven lines no verb has Dante for its subject: his wings are not enough, his mind is struck, power fails. Auden gave the <i>Comedy</i> as his example of a quest in which the vision is given by grace and reached by no act of the dreamer.

### Tab “Measure” (beat)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Measure

[plates[2].lenses[1].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
eleven syllables: the last accent always on the tenth and, as a rule, another on the fourth or the sixth

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Dante’s line has eleven syllables. The last accent always falls on the tenth and, as a rule, another on the fourth or the sixth. In Italian, stresses tend to fall on every second syllable, and verse tends to avoid two side by side. The last line keeps that pattern exactly. Line 142 ends on two stresses together: <b>mancò possa</b>, failed and power, its ninth syllable and its tenth. A stress on the ninth, a study of Dante’s verse says, strikes against the tenth and brings out the word it falls on. Here that word is <i>mancò</i>: power, the line says, failed his high fantasy, the faculty that receives images.

[plates[2].lenses[1].points[0].say] (a numbered remark)
The last syllable of *fantasia* is the sixth of the line, and stressed. *Qui*, here, is the seventh, and can be heard stressed too: two more stresses together, at the middle of the line.

[plates[2].lenses[1].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)
The last syllable of *mancò* and the first of *possa* are both stressed: the ninth and tenth of the line, with nothing unstressed between them.

[plates[2].lenses[1].points[2].say] (a numbered remark)
Stress on syllables 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10. Twice a final vowel runs into the vowel that begins the next word, and a small arc ties the two. So thirteen written syllables count as eleven.

[plates[2].lenses[1].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage: the stresses as one reader hears them, by the rules of the Italian line (*Enciclopedia dell’Italiano* and Treccani *Vocabolario*, under *endecasillabo*). Where stresses tend to fall: Luca Zuliani, “Sulla cesura dell’endecasillabo,” p. 173; Bruno Germano, *Il solfeggio dei versi*, p. 24. A stress on the ninth: *Enciclopedia Dantesca*, under *endecasillabo*, section 6. Fantasy as the faculty that receives images: Singleton’s note to line 142.

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[2].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[2].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
In terza rima the middle line of each group of three gives the rhyme to the next group. So the chain cannot end on a group: its middle line would have no answer. Every canto ends on one line more. The word left waiting is <b>velle</b>, Latin for <i>to will</i>, used as a noun: his will. The line that answers it ends the poem on <b>stelle</b>, stars. The poem has <i>velle</i> twice, both times rhymed with <i>stelle</i>. <b>Mossa</b>, moved, the rhyme word before, returns in the last line as <b>move</b>, moves, the word that ends the first line of the <i>Paradiso</i>. Auden wrote that the poem’s structure fixes even the number of its lines; the rhyme scheme requires this one.

[plates[2].lenses[2].stands] (at the foot, under “What each caption stands on”)
The marks on the passage; the closing line of the chain: the Treccani *Vocabolario* under *terzina*. *Velle* and *move*: the same dictionary under *velle* and *muovere*, and the *Enciclopedia Dantesca* under *velle*. Counted in the Italian printed here: every canto has a multiple of three lines, plus one; *velle* comes twice, here and in [canto 4](#/read/dante-italian/par-04:8); *move* ends the first line of [canto 1](#/read/dante-italian/par-01:0). Auden: *Forewords and Afterwords*, p. 269.

### Tab “Whole” (shape)

[plates[2].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
Whole

[plates[2].lenses[3].what] (printed over the drawing)
the hundred cantos of the Comedy: how many lines each has, how every canto ends, and the last word of each of the three parts

[plates[2].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
The <i>Comedy</i> has 100 cantos: one to open the whole and 33 for each of three parts. Every canto is a chain of three-line groups closed by a single line. The parts run to 4,720, 4,755 and 4,758 lines. Auden speaks of a mathematical structure that fixes even the number of lines. At the end of the <i>Purgatorio</i> Dante says the pages laid out for that part are full, and the curb of art lets him go no further. Each part ends on <b>stelle</b>, stars: the <i>Inferno</i> on seeing them again, the <i>Purgatorio</i> on being ready to rise to them, the <i>Paradiso</i> on the love that moves them.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In an essay on Kafka, Auden sorts the quests of literature into kinds and gives the *Comedy* as his example of the Dream Quest. The journey is after no object. It seeks knowledge of the spirit, a sight of what is real behind what appears, and the dreamer, awake again, can from then on live his earthly life. The dreamer stands for everyman. Seeing will not of itself change him, Auden adds, but one who has seen and stays the same has more to answer for than those who never saw.

[auden[0].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
it is not by any act or virtue of his that he attains this vision, for the vision is a gift of Divine Grace

[auden[0].source]
“K.’s Quest,” in *The Kafka Problem*, edited by Angel Flores (New York: New Directions, 1946), pp. 47–48

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In *New Year Letter*, published in the spring of 1941, before the course, Auden imagines himself on trial before a court of dead poets, and the judge who presides over his case is Dante. He draws him as a gaunt, weathered explorer who ruined his worldly prospects and gave his whole self, feeling, senses, will and thinking, to what lies beyond nature. With rational love for a guide he crossed Hell, Purgatory and Heaven, and he saw, exactly and in detail, all that surrounds and sustains the soul.

[auden[1].source]
*New Year Letter* (1941), Part 1; in *The Collected Poetry of W. H. Auden* (1945), pp. 269–70

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Donald Pearce, who took the course in 1941, describes the final examination. Auden told the class they would write out, from memory, seven of the *Comedy*’s cantos in the prose of Carlyle and Wicksteed, the version, he said, that Eliot used in *The Waste Land*. The list began with *Inferno* 3 and 5 and finished with this canto. When a committee of students called the task impossible he cut seven to five. Pearce says the last canto took him one afternoon to learn, and that in the examination he wrote without a stop for the full three hours. Others in the class remembered the number of cantos as six.

[auden[2].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), pp. 153–54; for the six cantos, Charles H. Miller, *Auden: An American Friendship* (1983), pp. 26–27 and 77–78

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden did not accept that poetry is simply what cannot be translated. What the words sound like, how they move, and whatever meaning hangs on sound, a rhyme or a pun, cannot be carried over. Pictures and comparisons taken from what the senses know can be, in part, and so can a poet’s own way of seeing the world. His test: put a poem of Goethe’s and one of Hölderlin’s into literal prose, and a reader can still tell that two different men wrote them.

[auden[3].source]
“Writing,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 23–24

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
The stresses are marked as one reader hears them, by the rules of the Italian line. A few syllables in lines 139 to 144 can be heard or counted another way. The captions lean only on the ninth and tenth syllables of line 142 and on the last line.

[caveats[1]]
What a stress on the ninth syllable does is a general remark about Dante’s line in the *Enciclopedia Dantesca*. Line 142 is not among its examples: that the remark holds there is this page’s own application. No commentary read for this page marks the stresses of these lines.

[caveats[2]]
Three meanings are disputed. The last phrase of line 90 is read as one simple light or as a mere glimmer. What *disio* and *velle* each name in line 143 is debated; here they are only desire and will. That *s’interna* also means *is threefold* is rejected by some commentators.

[caveats[3]]
Auden’s remark is general: the poem’s structure, he says, dictates even the number of verses. That the closing line of each canto and the line counts are cases of it is this page’s reading. His essay “K.’s Quest” was read only in search extracts of a scanned copy.

[caveats[4]]
That seven cantos were cut to five is one student’s memory, printed more than forty years later. Two others in the class remembered six. Only Pearce names this canto and the prose version.
