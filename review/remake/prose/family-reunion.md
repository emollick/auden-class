## The head of the page

[title]
Furies in the drawing room

[where]
*The Family Reunion* (1939), the whole play

[about] (under “The passage”)
Amy, Lady Monchensey, has kept Wishwood, a cold country house in northern England, unchanged for the eight years her eldest son has been away. On her birthday the family gathers and Harry comes home. His wife was lost overboard from a liner a year ago. He says he pushed her, and at the window he sees figures that, he says, the others do not see. Before the night is over he learns what his father once planned, and he leaves the house.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Following Eliot’s account, Auden wrote that this play went far toward a verse close to speech, though he could not hear the three stresses Eliot counted in each line. The theme, he wrote later, is spiritual election, and Eliot’s comedy convention is bound to make the call seem to come to Harry, and to pass over his brother John for being stupid. He was certain Eliot did not mean it.

[view.source]
“Eliot on Eliot,” *The Observer*, 28 October 1951, and “T. S. Eliot So Far,” *The Griffin*, March 1953; both in *Prose*, vol. III (2008), pp. 258 and 355

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
Eliot wanted a rhythm close to the speech of his day. By his account the play’s lines vary in length (8 to 16 syllables in the first speech), and each has three stresses, the syllables a speaker leans on, divided two and one by a pause, a caesura. The marks are this page’s hearing, not Eliot’s. By them Amy’s last line keeps the rule. The second line has four stressed words, and <i>can</i> may take a fifth. It stands where Auden opened the play at random: he could read no line there with fewer than four stresses, and heard five in what appears to be this one. On the measuring of verse, he added, each poet thinks only himself right.

Two aunts and two uncles have parts of their own, and at five places they speak with one voice as a chorus. At all but the fourth, each of them then speaks briefly alone. ### Tab “Greek” (shape)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)

[plates[0].lenses[2].what] (printed over the drawing)
The story of Orestes as Aeschylus tells it, laid beside the play: what Eliot kept and what he changed

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
Aeschylus’s story of Orestes lies under the play. Eliot keeps its frame: a cursed house, a son come home, pursuers he sees while those around him see no one. In place of its two killings the play has a murder planned and stopped, and a push Harry thinks he may have dreamed. In Aeschylus Orestes does not repent, as Auden noted, and a court tries him and frees him. Here Agatha calls the story one of <i>sin and expiation</i>, of amends for a sin perhaps not Harry’s own, and he turns and follows his pursuers. Eliot later judged that he had not fitted the Greek story to the modern situation.

### Tab “Sight” (shape)

[plates[0].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
Sight

[plates[0].lenses[3].what] (printed over the drawing)
Who in the house sees the Eumenides, and who does not

[plates[0].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
Harry sees his pursuers, the Eumenides, at the window. His aunt Agatha, his cousin Mary and his chauffeur see them too. His mother and the chorus do not, and his brothers never arrive. Harry speaks of his <i>election</i>, his being chosen, and cannot say why it is his. John, who gets the house, is called dependable, dull and barely aware. Auden wrote that the play takes over the convention of English high comedy, where wealth and breeding can stand for gifts and virtues. When the theme is spiritual election, it is bound to seem that Harry, not John, is called because John is stupid. Auden was certain Eliot did not mean it.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Reviewing Eliot’s lecture in 1951, Auden follows his account of the play. Eliot had decided to take a subject from his own time, to find a verse rhythm near its speech, and to let the characters do the commenting. In this play, he writes, Eliot went far toward solving the problem of the verse. Then Auden tries the rule of three stresses on lines found by opening the play at random, and can read none with fewer than four. On prosody, the measuring of verse, he adds, each poet thinks only himself right. He reports Eliot’s verdict: letting the characters comment had proved more artificial than an outright chorus.

[auden[0].source]
“Eliot on Eliot,” a review of Eliot’s *Poetry and Drama*, *The Observer*, 28 October 1951; in *Prose*, vol. III (2008), pp. 257–59

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Reviewing Eliot’s collected poems and plays in 1953, Auden says that in the plays the chosen persons, Becket, Harry and Celia, take their suffering as a way to grace and a sign of special vocation. If this play and *The Cocktail Party* sound a snobbish note at times, he puts it down to technique. Intent on a stage verse that would not be arty, Eliot put off the question of convention and took over that of English high comedy, whose chief persons are aristocrats. That serves while the subject is worldly, since wealth and breeding can stand for gifts and virtues. For a theme of spiritual election it breaks down.

[auden[1].source]
“T. S. Eliot So Far,” a review of Eliot’s *Complete Poems and Plays*, *The Griffin*, March 1953; in *Prose*, vol. III (2008), pp. 352–55

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In the foreword to his T. S. Eliot Memorial Lectures of 1967, Auden sets Harry beside two other persons of Eliot’s plays. Becket, in the first long play, was a martyr known to history. What becomes of Harry is not told, Auden says, but he decides on a way of life that may well end in martyrdom, as Celia’s decision does in *The Cocktail Party*.

[auden[2].source]
*Secondary Worlds* (New York: Random House, 1968), Foreword, p. 11

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In a book of 1950, writing about heroes of revenge, Auden describes the Orestes of Aeschylus, whose story lies under the play. His killing of his mother is no free choice: it is a duty that comes to him from what he owes his father. What the Furies make him suffer is unexpected and comes from nothing in himself. He does not repent, and a divine court acquits him. Auden is not writing about Eliot’s play here.

[auden[3].source]
*The Enchafèd Flood* (1950; Vintage Books edition, 1967), pp. 109–10

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
What Auden says here was written between 1950 and 1968, nine to twenty-seven years after his course of 1941, and his remark on Orestes is not about this play. No record was found of anything he said about the play in class.

[caveats[1]]
Auden’s two reviews and his foreword were read for this page in short passages of scanned copies, not in full.

[caveats[2]]
The stress marks on the two lines are this page’s own, and each line can be heard another way. Eliot marked the stresses of no line of the play in his lecture, and Auden gave only counts. Which line he heard with five stresses is inferred from his count.

[caveats[3]]
The play was read in a later printing of the New York edition of 1939. Page numbers are that printing’s, and other printings and editions may differ.

[caveats[4]]
The counts were made on that copy: for the chorus, words, with lines that turn over on the page joined and a hyphenated name counted as one; for the first speech, syllables, where a word that can be said in one syllable or two was counted as one.
