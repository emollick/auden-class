## The head of the page

[title]
Falstaff’s prose, the King’s verse

[where]
*Henry IV, Part 2*: Act 1, Scene 2; Act 3, Scene 1; Act 5, Scene 5

[about] (under “The passage”)
Sir John Falstaff, a fat old knight, has been the companion of Prince Hal (Henry of Monmouth), heir to the throne. In the first passage, in prose, the Lord Chief Justice, who once jailed the Prince, stops Falstaff in a London street. In the second, in verse, the sick King, Henry IV, who took the crown from Richard II, is awake at one in the morning. In the third the old King is dead, and the Prince, just crowned Henry V, passes Falstaff, who waits near Westminster Abbey.

[view.says] (under “What Auden said”: a paraphrase of him)
Auden wrote that Falstaff, who belongs to a comic world of play, cannot live in the world of political history, where Shakespeare sets him. Its matter is earthly justice: the old King pays for deposing a lawful king, and his son, rejecting Falstaff in public, shows his subjects a just ruler. By *Charity* Auden means love: he calls Falstaff’s tireless effort to make people laugh a comic picture of a love that keeps nothing back.

[view.quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
a comic symbol for the supernatural order of Charity as contrasted with the temporal order of Justice symbolized by Henry of Monmouth

[view.source]
“The Prince’s Dog,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 183–84, 186–90, 198, 203–06; “Brothers & Others,” in the same book, p. 221

[plates[0].lenses[0].plain] (the figure in plain words)

[plates[0].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The Chief Justice makes three charges, and twice Falstaff answers in the judge’s own words, crossed. <b>Slender</b> means and a waste that <b>is great</b> come back as means <b>greater</b> and a waist <b>slenderer</b>. <b>You have misled</b> the Prince comes back as the Prince <b>hath misled me</b>. Rhetoric calls this chiasmus. It makes the second charge a wish and turns the third on the Prince. In Act 2 the Justice says he knows Falstaff’s way of twisting <i>the true cause the false way</i>. According to a student’s notes, Auden said in class in 1941 that Falstaff goes back to childish things, word-play among them, on purpose, to be liked and treated kindly.

### Tab “Word” (word)

[plates[0].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Word

[plates[0].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The Justice’s words are about how Falstaff lives: <b>great</b> infamy, <b>slender</b> means, great <b>waste</b>. Falstaff takes each as a word of size: a man in his <b>belt</b> cannot live in <b>less</b>, he wishes his <b>waist</b> <b>slenderer</b>, his belly is great. The lexicon of Shakespeare’s words files the judge’s <i>slender</i> under insufficient and Falstaff’s under thin, and marks the pun on <i>waste</i> and <i>waist</i>, which the 1600 printing spells alike. Later in the scene Falstaff says he was born with a round belly. Auden wrote that Falstaff cannot be conceived as anything but old and fat, and that this claim of his is believed.

### Tab “Sentence” (sentence)

[plates[0].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Sentence

[plates[0].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
In the charges Falstaff is the one who does things: <b>you</b> live, <b>you</b> have misled, and the means and waste are his. In the replies he is not. The one who lives is any man in his belt, and the one who misleads is <b>the young prince</b>. His <i>I</i> takes two verbs only: <b>I would</b>, a wish, twice, and <b>I am</b>. In his essay “The Prince’s Dog” Auden wrote that Falstaff speaks for one reason only, to show himself, and that almost all he says restates one theme, <i>I am that I am</i>. Near the end of the essay he says this does not describe him completely: Falstaff must also add the last reply here.

### Tab “Play” (shape)

[plates[0].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
Play

[plates[0].lenses[3].what] (printed over the drawing)
which parts of the play are in verse and which in prose, and who speaks which

[plates[0].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
This exchange is prose, like nearly all that Falstaff says: 5,373 words of prose in the play and eleven lines set as verse. Nine of its nineteen scenes have no prose, and he speaks in none of them. The old King speaks only verse. The Prince speaks prose in Act 2, changes to verse when Peto comes in with news from the north, and never speaks prose again. Shakespeare used prose for comic scenes, and moved between prose and verse as subject or tone required. Auden wrote that Falstaff belongs by nature to comic opera, and that Shakespeare puts him into a political chronicle where he cannot exist.

### Tab “Beat” (beat)

[plates[1].lenses[0].label] (the word on the tab)
Beat

[plates[1].lenses[0].meterName] (printed after “The measure”)
ten syllables and five beats, the line beginning off the beat

[plates[1].lenses[0].caption] (the caption)
The King’s speech is in lines of ten syllables and five beats, the line beginning off the beat. Two of its twenty-eight lines have twelve syllables, six beats by the same count, and both are shown: the second line and the last but one. Each stops in the middle, where a sentence ends, six syllables on either side. At the first stop the King turns to <b>Sleep</b>, at the second from Sleep to the low. A six-beat line tends to split into three and three, and in Shakespeare it is often two short verses joined. The line is lengthened at the King’s two turns to a new hearer, and nowhere else in the speech.

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[0].say] (a numbered remark)

[plates[1].lenses[0].points[1].say] (a numbered remark)

### Tab “Rhyme” (rhyme)

[plates[1].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Rhyme

[plates[1].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
The speech ends on a rhyme: <b>lie down</b>, <b>a crown</b>. A rhyme returning at once makes its two lines one unit. Within it the last question to Sleep ends at mid-line, and an order to the low and a statement follow. One verb serves both: the low <b>lie</b> down, the crowned head <b>lies</b> uneasy. Lecturing in New York in 1946 and 1947, Auden said that father and son each speak of how uneasy a king is: the father in this speech, whose last line he quotes, the son in <i>Henry V</i>. In Act 5 of this play the son, newly king, says that majesty <i>sits not so easy on me</i>.

### Tab “Sentence” (sentence)

[plates[1].lenses[2].label] (the word on the tab)
Sentence

[plates[1].lenses[2].caption] (the caption)
The King says <i>I</i> or <b>my</b> four times in the first five lines, and not once in the twenty-three that follow. His first question is about himself, <b>how have I frighted thee</b>, and he does not come back to it. His last question holds its second verb, <b>Deny</b>, back two lines and ends on <b>a King</b>, the place of most weight; the last line has <b>the head</b> and <b>a crown</b>. Auden told his class in 1941 to notice how hollow this King’s rhetoric is throughout the play. He traced it to a life of not listening to his feelings, which leaves the King feeling that something is wrong with him.

### Tab “Names” (sentence)

[plates[2].lenses[1].label] (the word on the tab)
Names

[plates[2].lenses[1].caption] (the caption)
Falstaff’s line names the King three times, each time as his own: <b>My King</b>, <b>My Jove</b>, <b>my heart</b>. The King gives him no name, here or in the seventeen lines to come. He calls him <b>that vain man</b>, <b>old man</b>, <b>a fool and jester</b>. Auden told his class in 1941 that Falstaff can form only personal ties and the Prince none, so their break had to come; later he wrote that to the Prince he matters no more than a court jester would. The King gives him five orders here. The last, <b>Reply not</b>, forbids the sort of answer shown in the first passage, and Falstaff makes none.

Four of the King’s phrases speak of age and death: <b>old man</b>, <b>white hairs</b>, <b>so old</b>, <b>the grave</b>. In Act 1 Falstaff told the Chief Justice that he was born with a white head. ### Tab “Balance” (figure)

[plates[2].lenses[3].label] (the word on the tab)
Balance

[plates[2].lenses[3].name] (the name of the figure)
antithesis

[plates[2].lenses[3].plain] (the figure in plain words)
two opposed things set side by side, often in phrases of the same build

[plates[2].lenses[3].caption] (the caption)
The line sets <b>less</b> against <b>more</b> and <b>body</b> against <b>grace</b>, in two phrases of one build. Rhetoric calls this antithesis. In the first passage Falstaff made a wish of the same build: greater means and a slenderer waist. The waist comes here, as an order. The means come fifteen lines later, in the same word, as an allowance to live on. Where Falstaff said <i>means</i> the King here says <i>grace</i>, which the lexicon reads in this line as virtue. No allowance, Auden wrote, can mend what the rejection does to Falstaff’s heart: what he wants is the Prince’s company.

## More from Auden

[auden[0].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Donald Pearce took the 1941 class and wrote notes after each lecture. His notes have Auden begin on *Henry IV* by calling Falstaff a comic Iago. Both are detached from the moral side of human dealings and manipulate people: Iago coldly, Falstaff by making a play or game of everything. The play’s true Machiavel, or schemer, he said, is the Prince, who studies Falstaff as his pupil and, at the end of Part 2, drops his old companions without regret, because they are of no more use to him. His father, Auden added, treated the Percies the same way. Auden’s later essay, “The Prince’s Dog,” reads Falstaff differently.

Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), pp. 144–45

[auden[1].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Later in the same lecture, as Pearce recorded it, Auden called Falstaff an opportunist without conscience and, in private, a man whose hopes have failed him, who wraps himself in fat and plays the child so as not to be hurt. He read Falstaff as a figure for the body of a lazy, swollen, greedy and sick England. He called the old King an able opportunist, and hollow, and said that when the King begs his son for feeling he gets only empty rhetoric back. Falstaff, he said, sees the Prince as his child, and cannot survive in a world that runs on calculation.

[auden[1].source]
Donald Pearce, “Fortunate Fall: W. H. Auden at Michigan,” in *W. H. Auden: The Far Interior*, edited by Alan Bold (1985), pp. 146–48

[auden[2].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In *The Dyer’s Hand* Auden wrote that the Prince has never deceived himself about Falstaff and rejects him when the rejection will have the greatest political effect. The allowance, which wins the King the admiration of those near him, does not win an audience that knows Falstaff. Falstaff is wholly devoted to the Prince, as to a son of his own, thinks the Prince loves him back, and so is happy in a false belief.

[auden[2].quote] (Auden’s own words, not to be changed)
It is Hal’s company he wants, not a pension from the Civil List.

[auden[2].source]
“The Prince’s Dog,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 190–91

[auden[3].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden gave a short fable for the three plays with the Falstaff scenes cut. England is infected by its own family doctor. A capable man with no right to practice throws him out, and the patient grows worse, until that man’s son, whom everyone thought a sick man past help, works the cure. Auden listed five things the ideal ruler needs: to know justice from injustice, to be just, to have the strength to enforce justice, to win loyalty, and to be the lawful ruler. Of Henry IV he wrote that no one, the rebels included, names an unjust act of his reign: his injustice was deposing the lawful king.

[auden[3].source]
“The Prince’s Dog,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 186–88

[auden[4].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Of Falstaff’s fatness Auden wrote that a fat man looks half like a small child and half like a woman with child, and that fatness in a man expresses a wish to be mother and child in one, and so to need no one. Falstaff’s great belly and red face, he wrote, bring to mind that England’s body politic is unwell too. He set beside them the play’s lines on a country sick, surfeited and full of rank diseases, the last of them spoken by the King a few lines after the speech in the second passage.

[auden[4].source]
“The Prince’s Dog,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 195–98

[auden[5].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Read as a parable, Auden wrote, Falstaff’s idling and drinking mark the unworldly man, and the Prince is the worldly man at his best. Such a man will treat persons as things when his end requires it, and, seen from outside, the Prince and Iago do the same two things: they deceive and they destroy. Falstaff does nothing and talks without end, so that an audience sees energy and not idleness. Up to the rejection he gives off happiness as the Prince gives off power.

[auden[5].source]
“The Prince’s Dog,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 204–06

[auden[6].says] (a paraphrase of him)
In “Music in Shakespeare” Auden wrote that music, like the sack Falstaff drinks, helps him keep up the illusion of a childlike Eden in which nothing grave can occur, and that above all he deceives himself that the Prince returns his love in full. Shakespeare saves background music for the tavern scene of Act 2. When Peto breaks in, the Prince feels ashamed and Falstaff only disappointed. That moment, Auden wrote, is the turning point of the Prince’s life, and the friendship ends with his good night.

[auden[6].source]
“Music in Shakespeare,” in *The Dyer’s Hand* (1962), pp. 510–11

[auden[7].says] (a paraphrase of him)
Auden lectured on the Henry plays again in New York in 1946 and 1947. The lectures survive in students’ notes, put together by a later editor. There he said that Hal and Falstaff are lasting opposites and enemies, and that by the end Falstaff is destroyed in body and Hal morally, with nothing of himself remaining.

[auden[7].source]
*Lectures on Shakespeare*, reconstructed and edited by Arthur Kirsch (2000), the lecture on *Henry IV, Parts One and Two*, and *Henry V*, p. 110

## Where this page may be wrong

[caveats[0]]
What Auden said in class in 1941 is known from notes that a student, Donald Pearce, wrote down after each lecture. It is not Auden’s own text. The opening of the lecture on *Henry IV* (Pearce, pp. 144–45) was read only in snippets of a scanned copy.

[caveats[1]]
Auden’s New York lectures of 1946 and 1947 are an editor’s text made from students’ notes. The book was read here only in snippets of a scanned copy; its page numbers were worked out from a running head and an editor’s note seen in those snippets.

[caveats[2]]
The stresses marked are one reader’s hearing of the lines. The two long lines of the King’s speech and several lines of the third passage could fairly be marked otherwise; *prayers* and *being* are counted as one syllable each and can be heard as two.

[caveats[3]]
The counts of verse and prose follow the text printed here: its lines, its five scenes in Act 4, and its choice of which short speeches to set as verse. Another edition will give slightly different totals.

[caveats[4]]
The second passage leaves out lines 6 to 22 of the King’s speech; lines 9 and 10, left out, also rhyme. Where the text printed here has *slenderer*, *sea-boy* and *give thy repose*, as the Folio of 1623 has, the Quarto of 1600 has *slender*, *season* and *give them repose*.
